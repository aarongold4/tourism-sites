JORDAN

Arabic and English in Jordan

English is widely spoken in tourist contexts — but a handful of Arabic phrases dramatically improves interactions. Here's the practical guide: what to learn, where Arabic matters most, and how to handle conversations beyond the basics.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 6-minute read · See our editorial standards

Jordan is one of the easier Middle Eastern countries for English-speaking travelers. Tourism is well-established, English is taught in schools, and the working English level in tourist contexts (hotels, guides, restaurants, taxi drivers in cities) is high. A monolingual English speaker can travel Jordan confidently without learning any Arabic at all.

That said, a handful of Arabic phrases — maybe 7-10 expressions you can learn in an hour — dramatically improves the quality of interactions. Jordanians universally appreciate the effort, the response is genuine warmth rather than mere politeness, and the small Arabic talents open doors to conversations that monolingual travelers miss entirely.

The essential phrases

The minimum viable Arabic for Jordan travel — these phrases cover the situations where Arabic genuinely helps:

Greetings

  • as-salaam alaykum — "peace be upon you" (the universal greeting)
  • wa alaykum as-salaam — "and upon you peace" (the response)
  • marhaba — "hello" (casual)
  • ahlan wa sahlan — "welcome" (you'll hear this constantly; respond with "ahlan beek" / "ahlan beeki" for male/female)

Thank you and please

  • shukran — "thank you"
  • afwan — "you're welcome" / "excuse me"
  • min fadlak (to a man) / min fadlik (to a woman) — "please"

Yes and no

  • na'am — "yes" (formal)
  • aywa — "yes" (casual, more common in conversation)
  • la — "no"

Practical phrases

  • bikam? — "how much?"
  • wein el-hammam? — "where is the bathroom?"
  • mayy — "water"
  • ta'am — "food"
  • hisab — "bill / check" (at restaurants)

Useful for negotiation and small talk

  • kteer — "a lot" / "too much"
  • qaleel — "a little"
  • kwayes — "good" / "okay"
  • insha'allah — "God willing" (heard constantly, used for any future plan or hope)
  • al-hamdulillah — "praise be to God" (used as a general "I'm well")

Numbers — Eastern Arabic numerals

Jordan uses both Western (1234567890) and Eastern Arabic numerals (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩). Most tourist contexts use Western numbers, but you'll occasionally see Eastern Arabic on restaurant bills, market prices, and some signage.

Spoken Arabic numbers 1-10: wahid, itnein, talata, arba'a, khamsa, sitta, sab'a, tamanya, tis'a, ashara.

Food terms worth knowing

For navigating restaurant menus, especially traditional places without English translations:

  • khubz — bread
  • laham — meat (usually lamb)
  • dajaj — chicken
  • samak — fish
  • ruzz — rice
  • salata — salad
  • jubn — cheese
  • zabadi — yogurt
  • shai — tea
  • qahwa — coffee
  • asir — juice
  • bedoun — "without" (useful for dietary restrictions: bedoun jubn = without cheese)

Where English isn't enough

Taxi negotiations. Many yellow taxi drivers have limited English. Useful: addad (meter), wein (where), hina (here). Write hotel/destination names in Arabic on your phone for showing to drivers.

Small shops and markets. Shopkeepers in downtown Amman souks and rural village shops often speak limited English. Greet first ("salaam alaykum"), use "bikam?" for the price, "ghali kteer" for "too expensive," and walk away politely if the price doesn't reach your target.

Bedouin interactions. At Wadi Rum and on Petra trails, Bedouin hosts and guides speak varying English. The single most appreciated phrase: "shukran kteer" — "thank you very much" — said warmly with eye contact.

Rural drives. On the King's Highway through smaller towns, gas station attendants and small-restaurant servers often speak only basic English. Pointing, gestures, and Google Translate close the gap.

Cultural notes on language

Religious phrases are constant. "Insha'allah" attaches to nearly any future plan; "alhamdulillah" is a common positive response; "mashallah" often follows praise. These aren't strictly religious in modern usage — they're conversational fillers similar to "fingers crossed" or "knock on wood" in English.

Politeness levels matter. Arabic has distinct masculine and feminine forms for most second-person address. Using "min fadlak" (please, masculine) when speaking to a woman is a minor mistake but noticed.

Greetings extend longer than English equivalents. When you greet a Jordanian, expect a longer exchange than "hi, how are you" — typically two or three back-and-forth lines covering health, family, and well-being before the actual conversation begins. Don't rush this; it's where rapport is built.

"Welcome to Jordan" is universal. You'll hear "ahlan wa sahlan" constantly. The response is "ahlan beek" (to a man) or "ahlan beeki" (to a woman) — literally "welcome to you."

Apps and resources

  • Google Translate — download the Arabic offline pack before traveling.
  • Duolingo Arabic — free, gamified learning. A few weeks of casual use covers the essentials.
  • Pimsleur Arabic — audio-based learning, particularly good for spoken pronunciation.
  • YouTube channels — "Learn Arabic with Maha," "Arabic Pod 101," and "Learn Levantine Arabic" all have useful free content focused on Jordan/Levantine dialect.

Frequently asked questions

Do Jordanians speak English?
In tourist contexts — yes, widely. Hotel staff, tour guides, restaurant servers at mid-range and upscale places, and shopkeepers in tourist areas all speak functional to fluent English. Taxi drivers vary. In rural areas and at Bedouin camps, English is more limited but service-essential phrases are universally understood. You can travel Jordan confidently as a monolingual English speaker.
What's the official language?
Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) is the official language. Spoken Jordanian Arabic is a regional dialect of Levantine Arabic, closely related to Palestinian, Syrian, and Lebanese Arabic. Bedouin Arabic (heard in Wadi Rum and rural areas) has additional dialectal features. As a tourist, the distinctions don't matter — any Arabic effort is appreciated.
How do I learn useful phrases quickly?
A few hours with a basic phrasebook or app (Duolingo Arabic, Pimsleur, YouTube playlists) before traveling covers the essentials. Focus on greetings, thank you, please, no/yes, bathroom, how much, and water. These 7-8 phrases cover most situations where Arabic matters.
Will my Google Translate work?
Yes, well. Google Translate has strong Arabic-English support, including camera translation for signs and menus. Download the Arabic language pack for offline use before traveling (works without internet). Many travelers use it for restaurant menus when English isn't available.
Is the Arabic script intimidating?
Less than you'd think for tourist purposes. Most road signs, restaurant menus, hotel signage, and tourist materials are bilingual (Arabic + English). Learning the Arabic numerals (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) takes 15 minutes and helps with restaurant bills in non-tourist contexts; learning the alphabet takes weeks and isn't worth it for a short trip.
