ITINERARIES

Petra in three days

The version for serious hikers, photographers, and travelers who want to actually slow down. Three days lets you add Jebel Haroun, both major trails, Little Petra at leisure, and afternoon light at every monument — without rushing anything.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 11-minute read · See our editorial standards

A three-day Petra visit is the right answer for a specific subset of travelers: serious hikers who want Jebel Haroun and the back routes, photographers who care about catching the right light at every monument, anyone who specifically wants Petra by Night without compromising the daytime schedule, and travelers who recognize that a third day at any major archaeological site is qualitatively different from the first two. The marginal cost is small — 5 JOD more than the two-day ticket, plus one additional night in a Wadi Musa hotel. The marginal experience is significant if you're the right traveler for it.

This itinerary doesn't compress the two-day plan; it adds to it. The first two days roughly follow the two-day itinerary, with smaller tweaks to spread the load. The third day is where the real difference lives — it's the day that turns a comprehensive Petra visit into a memorable one.

Day 1 — Headline monuments

The standard Day 1 from the two-day itinerary, with one change: don't try to fit in Petra by Night this evening. The advantage of three days is that you have more flexibility on when to do it.

Quick sequence: 06:00 enter, Siq to Treasury, Royal Tombs and lower city by 09:00, Monastery climb starting 09:00, descent and lunch at the Basin around 12:00, slow walk back through the lower city in the afternoon catching the Royal Tombs in good light, exit by 16:30. Early dinner in Wadi Musa, asleep by 22:00. See the two-day itinerary for hour-by-hour detail.

Day 2 — The trail and Little Petra

A slower start than Day 1. Enter the site around 07:00 — still ahead of the tour groups, but you've earned an extra hour of sleep after Day 1's climbing.

The Day 2 trail choice is the High Place of Sacrifice with the Wadi Farasa descent. This is the longer and more rewarding of the two main second-day options (the alternative is Al-Khubtha), and a three-day visit gives you the time to do it properly. The trail climbs 600 steps up Jebel Madbah to the Nabataean cultic plateau, then descends through Wadi Farasa past twelve Nabataean inscriptions, the Lion Monument fountain, the Garden Temple, and the Roman Soldier Tomb complex. Total time: 4 to 5 hours including stops. Out by 13:00.

Lunch in Wadi Musa. The afternoon is for Little Petra. Taxi to the site (10–15 JOD round trip, 15 minutes each way), free entry, walk the 350-meter slot canyon, climb the steps to the painted Biclinium (bring a flashlight for the ceiling paintings — 1st-century Nabataean wall art, the only substantial example anywhere), then continue 5 minutes further to the Beidha Neolithic site for an additional 30–45 minutes. Total Little Petra afternoon: 2 to 2.5 hours. Back to Wadi Musa by 17:30.

Evening — Petra by Night (Sun–Thu). If your Day 2 falls on a Sunday through Thursday, this is the right evening for it. You've had a less demanding afternoon than Day 1, you can grab an early dinner, and you'll be at the Visitor Centre by 20:15 ready for the candle-lit Siq walk. See the Petra by Night page.

Day 3 — Jebel Haroun or the slow visit

The third day is where the three-day visit really differentiates. Two strong options. Pick one based on your fitness and what you want from the trip.

Option A — Jebel Haroun

Petra's highest peak (1,350 meters) and the traditional burial site of Aaron, brother of Moses. A small Mamluk shrine at the summit, built in the 14th century, marks the pilgrimage site for Muslims (where Aaron is venerated as the prophet Harun). The hike is 5–7 hours round trip from the lower city, with serious elevation gain and significant desert exposure. The trail is partly marked, partly local-knowledge — hire a Bedouin guide for the day (80–100 JOD), bring 3+ liters of water per person, start at 06:00, and accept that this is a real desert hiking day.

The reward is solitude (you may not see another tourist for hours), one of the great panoramas in Jordan, and a sense of Petra's wider geographical context that the standard Visitor Centre approach completely misses. For experienced hikers, this is the version of Petra worth the third day.

Option B — The slow revisit

The anti-itinerary. Enter the site at 09:00 — late for Petra terms, but the tour groups have either come and gone or are concentrated at the Treasury rather than the lower city. Walk to a part of the site you skimmed on Day 1 or 2 and just sit. The colonnaded street near the Great Temple. The Roman theatre lower seats. The terrace above the Royal Tombs. A Bedouin tea stall along the Monastery trail.

The point isn't to see new things; it's to actually absorb the things you've already seen. After two days of constant motion, the third-day slow visit is when many travelers report finally understanding what Petra is. Bring a book. Take photographs you didn't have time to compose before. Talk to one of the Bedouin guides for an hour — many of them are Bdoul (the tribe that lived in the caves until 1985) and their family stories are extraordinary.

The slow third day is genuinely the best part of many three-day visits. We'd argue it's the better choice unless you specifically want the Jebel Haroun summit.

Option C — The back route from Little Petra

For travelers who want a real hike but not the full Jebel Haroun commitment. The back route from Little Petra to the Monastery is a 6 km trail that enters Petra from the north and emerges at the back of the Monastery plateau, skipping the 800-step climb entirely. The route takes 3 to 4 hours one-way and is one of the great walking routes in the region.

The catch is the logistics: you start at Little Petra (taxi from Wadi Musa) and end at the Monastery, which means you then have to walk the full length of Petra back to the Visitor Centre (another 2 hours), or arrange a Bedouin guide who can lead the route in either direction and arrange transport back to your hotel. Either way, this is a 5–7 hour day with proper hiking demands. Worth it for travelers who want a sense of the wider Petra landscape from above.

Some practical notes on three-day pacing

Three days at Petra is more cumulative walking than most travelers expect — easily 25–30 km on foot across the three days. Pacing matters. A few things that help:

Don't book Day 3 too tight against your departure flight. If you're flying out of Amman or Aqaba the evening of Day 3, you'll be exhausted from a full day at Petra and the 3-hour drive afterward. Build a buffer.

Take an afternoon nap between Day 1 and Day 2. If you've done Day 1 properly, you'll be tired by 17:00. A 30-minute nap before dinner is the difference between a productive Day 2 and a sluggish one.

Different shoes for the trails. Day 1 is mostly hard-packed sand and stone. Day 2 and Day 3 trails involve genuine hiking surfaces. Trail runners or light hiking shoes are ideal; dress sneakers are not.

Plan dinner around the schedule. Wadi Musa restaurants get crowded between 19:30 and 21:00 with day-tripping tour groups returning. Eat earlier (18:30) or later (21:30) for better service.

Frequently asked questions

Who actually needs three days at Petra?
Serious hikers (Jebel Haroun and the back routes), photographers (afternoon light at every monument plus seasonal wildflowers), travelers who want a leisurely pace rather than a compressed visit, and anyone who specifically wants to add Petra by Night without compromising daytime schedule. For most travelers, two days is enough. For these specific cases, three days is genuinely the right call.
What's the additional cost of a three-day ticket?
The three-day Petra ticket is 60 JOD for visitors staying overnight in Jordan — just 5 JOD more than the two-day ticket. The Jordan Pass Expert (80 JOD) includes the three-day ticket plus the visa fee. The marginal cost of extending is essentially trivial; the real cost is the extra night in a Wadi Musa hotel (typically $60–180 USD depending on category).
Is Jebel Haroun really worth a full day?
For experienced hikers who specifically want it, yes. Jebel Haroun is Petra's highest peak (1,350m) and the traditional burial site of Aaron, brother of Moses — a major Muslim and Christian pilgrimage site with a small Mamluk shrine at the summit. The hike is 5–7 hours round trip from the lower city, demanding both in distance and elevation gain. Most travelers should hire a local guide (~80–100 JOD for the day) — the trail isn't always obvious and the area is genuinely remote. Not recommended for first-time hikers or anyone uncomfortable with full desert sun exposure.
What should I do on each of the three days?
A common pattern: Day 1 covers the headline monuments (Siq, Treasury, Royal Tombs, lower city, Monastery), Day 2 covers the trails (Al-Khubtha OR High Place + Wadi Farasa loop, plus Little Petra), Day 3 is for either Jebel Haroun, the back route from Little Petra to the Monastery, or simply a slower revisit. The exact ordering can shift based on weather, Petra by Night scheduling, and what you missed on previous days.
Should I do Petra by Night in a three-day visit?
Yes, if your visit overlaps with a Sunday through Thursday. The advantage of a three-day plan is that you don't have to compress Petra by Night into the middle of a busy day — you can do it on whichever evening makes the most sense given the schedule. Late on Day 1 is common; some travelers prefer Day 2 evening so they have a quieter Day 3 morning. The flexibility is part of why three days works.
Is there an "anti-itinerary" for Day 3?
Yes, and it's underrated: just spend Day 3 slowing down. Re-enter the site at 09:00 rather than 06:00 (the crowds will have thinned out and you're not chasing the Treasury light again). Sit at one of the Bedouin tea stalls for an hour. Walk a part of the site you skimmed past on Day 1. Sit at the Treasury for 30 minutes just looking. The slow third day is the version of Petra that many travelers describe as the best part of the trip — not because you saw new things, but because you finally absorbed what you'd already seen.
