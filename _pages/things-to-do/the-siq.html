The Siq

The 1.2-kilometer sandstone canyon that leads to the Treasury isn't just a transit corridor — it's a deliberately designed experience, with water engineering, carved niches, and a sequence of compressions and reveals that the Nabataeans engineered to impress arriving traders.

Most travelers come to Petra to see the Treasury. What they actually remember, in many cases, is the Siq — the 1.2-kilometer slot canyon you walk through to reach it. The Siq is a natural feature (a fissure carved by water and tectonic shifts over millions of years), but the experience of walking through it is partly natural and partly engineered. The Nabataeans understood that any visitor arriving at Petra from the east would have no choice but to enter through this narrow canyon, and they treated it as the city's ceremonial front door. The water channels on the walls, the small carved niches, the dam at the entrance, even the rhythm of compressions and openings as you walk — all of it is part of an arrival sequence that culminates in the famous "Treasury reveal" moment, when the canyon widens by a few degrees and the rose-pink facade comes into view.

Understanding what the Siq is and what to look for makes the walk substantially more interesting. Most visitors hurry through it in 20 minutes, eyes up, taking iPhone video. The travelers who slow down — who look at the carved channels still visible on the walls, who notice the small niches that once held god-images, who pause at the dam and understand what the Nabataeans were managing for — come out the other end with a different sense of what they're looking at.

The geology

The Siq is a natural fault in the sandstone, not a man-made carving. It runs roughly 1.2 km from the modern dam at its eastern entrance to the point where it opens onto the Treasury plaza. The canyon walls range from 80 meters to over 180 meters in height, and the floor width varies from about 3 meters at its narrowest to about 12 meters at its widest. The sandstone itself shifts color through pink, red, orange, and grey as you walk — the famous "rose-red city" description applies most strongly to the Siq and the Treasury, where late-morning light brings the color out at its most saturated.

The Nabataean water engineering

The most visible Nabataean intervention in the Siq is the system of water channels carved into the walls on both sides. These channels — one on the left, one on the right as you walk in — originally carried ceramic pipes that delivered drinking water from springs near Wadi Musa to the city centre. The channels are still visible at intervals along the walk, particularly clear in the middle section of the canyon. The dam at the eastern entrance, rebuilt by Petra's modern authorities on the original Nabataean foundations, diverts winter flash-flood water away from the city — without this control, the lower city would flood every winter, as the area around the Treasury has done historically when the dam has failed.

The Nabataeans were a desert civilization, and their water management was the technical achievement that made Petra possible. The pipes and channels you see in the Siq are the most accessible expression of that system. The wider water infrastructure — cisterns, terraces, hidden aqueducts running through tunnels — is mostly invisible to a modern visitor but is what kept a city of 25,000 people supplied in a region with under 100mm of annual rainfall.

The carved features

Along the walls, several Nabataean carvings remain visible. About 400 meters in from the dam, on the right-hand wall, look for the eroded relief of a man leading two camels — a small but unmistakable rock carving thought to commemorate the caravan trade that made Petra wealthy. Beyond it, several small niches are cut into both walls. These originally held figurative stelae or images of Nabataean gods — most are now empty, the figures lost to time and the elements, but the rectangular slots in the rock are unmistakable once you know what to look for.

A few hundred meters before the Treasury reveal, the canyon widens slightly into a small open area, and on the left, a larger carved relief depicts another figure leading camels. This is the second of the two surviving caravan reliefs and is somewhat better preserved than the first. From here, the walk to the Treasury is another 5–7 minutes, with the canyon narrowing one more time before the final reveal.

The Treasury reveal

The moment most visitors come to Petra for. After 25 minutes in a canyon that has been mostly under 8 meters wide, the walls widen by perhaps two meters and the carved facade of the Treasury comes into view through the gap. The first sight is fragmentary — a column, an entablature — and then the full facade appears as you take another few steps forward. The Nabataean planners almost certainly designed the final approach for exactly this compression-and-release effect; you cannot get a partial view of the Treasury from further back, only the full reveal at this specific point.

The reveal works best in the morning, when the Treasury facade is in direct sunlight and the Siq behind you is still shadowed. By mid-afternoon, the Treasury is in shadow and the effect is less dramatic. The early-morning visit (06:00 to 08:00) is the only time of day when you can experience the reveal with the plaza largely empty of other people.

What's next

After the Treasury, the canyon continues — the Outer Siq, with the Street of Facades on your left and a gradual descent into the lower city. From here you'll reach the Roman theatre, the Royal Tombs, and the path to the Monastery. The full things to see at Petra page covers the rest of the route. For evening visits, Petra by Night is the candle-lit Siq experience that runs Sunday through Thursday.

Frequently asked questions

How long is the Siq?
About 1.2 kilometers from the dam at the modern entrance to the point where it opens onto the Treasury plaza. The walk takes 20–30 minutes at an unhurried pace, longer if you stop to look at the water channels and the carvings. The full distance from the Petra Visitor Centre gate to the start of the Siq is another 800 meters, mostly downhill, so the total approach walk is roughly 2 km.
How narrow is the Siq?
At its narrowest, about 3 meters wide; at its widest, about 12 meters. The walls rise 80 to 180 meters above the canyon floor. This combination — a tall, narrow slot canyon — is what makes the Siq one of the great architectural approaches in the ancient world. The Nabataeans built the city deliberately behind this natural defensive feature.
Did the Nabataeans carve the Siq?
No — the Siq is a natural geological feature, a slot canyon cut by water and tectonic activity over millions of years. What the Nabataeans did was engineer the water management within it. Visitors today can still see the two water channels running along the walls (one on each side), the dam built at the entrance to divert flash flood water away from the city, and the carved niches that held Nabataean gods or memorial stelae. The Siq is natural; what makes it remarkable is what the Nabataeans built on top of nature.
Is the Siq safe? What about flash floods?
In good weather, completely safe. In heavy rain, the Siq is a slot canyon and behaves like one — flash floods are real and have killed visitors. The most recent serious incident was in November 2018, when at least 12 people died in a flash flood near Petra. The site authorities now monitor weather and close the Siq when heavy rain is forecast. The simple rule: if it has been raining or is forecast to rain heavily on the day of your visit, check at the Visitor Centre before entering, and accept that the site may close for a few hours.
What should I look for in the Siq?
The two water channels carved along the walls (one on each side, originally fed by ceramic pipes), the carved camel-and-trader relief about halfway through (heavily eroded but visible), the small niches that held Nabataean deity figures (most figures gone, the niches remain), and the dam at the entrance that the Nabataeans built to control flash flood water. Most travelers walk through the Siq looking up at the walls; the engineering details on the walls themselves are the real story.
Can I see the Siq during Petra by Night?
Yes — Petra by Night is fundamentally a Siq experience. The route runs from the Visitor Centre, through the Siq lit by over 2,000 candles, to the Treasury plaza for the projection show. The Siq itself in candlelight is, for many visitors, more memorable than the Treasury reveal at the end. The experience runs Sunday through Thursday evenings from 20:30; it is a separate ticket (30 JOD) and is not included in the Jordan Pass.
