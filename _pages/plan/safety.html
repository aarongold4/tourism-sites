SAFETY

Is Petra safe?

The honest answer: yes, very. The real safety concerns at Petra are environmental — heat, falls, the rare but serious flash flood risk in the Siq — not security. Here's what to actually plan for.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 6-minute read · See our editorial standards

Petra is one of the safer tourist destinations in the Middle East, and Jordan more broadly is one of the more politically stable countries in the region. The State Department and FCDO travel advisories consistently rate Jordan at the second-lowest risk level (the same as most European destinations), with specific exceptions only for the immediate Syrian and Iraqi borders far from Petra. Violent crime against tourists is rare. The tourism infrastructure is established, the tourism police are present at major sites, and the local culture is hospitable in ways that genuinely matter when you need help.

The real safety concerns at Petra are environmental and physical rather than security-related. Heat exhaustion on the Monastery climb. Falls on uneven Nabataean stone steps. Sunburn for under-prepared travelers. Flash floods in the Siq during heavy rain — rare but genuinely dangerous. This page covers what to actually plan for.

Heat and sun

The single most common source of medical incidents at Petra. The combination of direct sun, sandstone reflection, low humidity, and significant physical exertion produces fast dehydration even in travelers who feel they're drinking enough. The risk is most acute on the Monastery climb (800 steps with limited shade), the High Place trail (similar), and the Al-Khubtha route in summer.

The practical defenses:

  • Drink water continuously — 2 to 3 liters per person per day minimum, more in summer
  • Start the major climbs early (before 09:00 in summer; 10:00 is fine in winter)
  • Wear a hat and sunscreen
  • Take rest stops at the Bedouin tea stalls along the trails
  • Watch for early warning signs: dizziness, nausea, stopping sweating, confusion. Any of these are reasons to descend immediately and find shade.

The Petra Authority maintains medical support at the Visitor Centre and can dispatch help to most parts of the site. Bedouin staff at the tea stalls are also responsive — they've seen heat exhaustion many times and know the signs.

Falls and trip hazards

The second-most-common injury. The Nabataean stone steps at the Monastery, the High Place, and around the Royal Tombs are uneven, polished smooth by 2,000 years of foot traffic, and sometimes wet from morning condensation or rare rain. A twisted ankle or scraped knee is common; more serious falls happen too.

The defenses:

  • Wear proper shoes — trail runners or hiking shoes, not sandals or dress shoes (see what to wear)
  • Don't rush, particularly on descent
  • Use trekking poles if you have them — they help significantly on the Monastery descent
  • If carrying a heavy camera, use both straps or a chest strap to keep your hands free
  • Don't try to take photographs while walking

Flash floods

The most serious low-frequency risk. The Siq is a slot canyon, and slot canyons can flood violently when heavy rain falls in the surrounding watershed — even rain that's some distance away can produce flash flooding in the canyon below.

The most recent serious incident at Petra was in November 2018, when sudden heavy rain in the Wadi Musa watershed produced a flash flood in the Siq and lower city. At least 12 people died, mostly schoolchildren on a field trip. The Petra Authority responded by upgrading the weather monitoring system, reinforcing the dam at the Siq entrance, and instituting closures during heavy rain forecasts.

For travelers in 2026, the practical rule is simple: if it has been raining heavily or rain is forecast for the day of your visit, check the Visitor Centre's current advisories before entering. They will close the Siq if conditions warrant. If you're inside the site when conditions change, follow staff instructions immediately — Petra workers know the warning signs and will direct visitors to higher ground.

Personal security

Crime against tourists at Petra is rare. The site has uniformed tourism police, the path is busy with visitors and Bedouin sellers, and the local economic dependence on tourism creates strong incentives for the community to keep visitors safe. Petty theft is the highest-probability risk — keep your phone secured, don't leave bags unattended, and use hotel safes for passports.

For solo female travelers specifically: Jordan is among the safer Middle Eastern destinations for women traveling alone. Some commercial attention from Bedouin sellers and occasionally unwanted social interest are common; harassment is rare. Practical recommendations:

  • Dress modestly (knees and shoulders covered) — see what to wear
  • Use established hotels rather than informal homestays
  • For the more remote trails (Jebel Haroun, the back routes from Little Petra), hire a licensed female-friendly guide rather than going alone
  • Trust your instincts — Bedouin sellers can be persistent but generally back off when given a firm "la, shukran" (no, thanks)
  • Skip the donkey rides — these involve sustained close contact with Bedouin men in remote sections of the trail and are the most-reported source of inappropriate interactions

Animal welfare and your safety

The donkeys, horses, and camels at Petra are part of the local Bedouin economy. The welfare situation has improved over the past decade with intervention from the Petra National Trust and other organizations, but conditions vary significantly between operators.

From a personal safety perspective: the Monastery donkey ride has produced serious accidents over the years — visitors falling off, animals losing their footing on the steep trail, and rare cases of visitors being kicked or bitten. We don't recommend the Monastery donkey ride for either welfare or safety reasons. The shorter horse-drawn carriage ride from the Visitor Centre to the Treasury is significantly safer.

Other practical safety notes

Currency and ATMs. The ATM at the Visitor Centre is reliable but limit your daily withdrawal to what you'll spend. Don't carry large amounts of cash on the trails.

Phone and communications. Cell coverage inside Petra is patchy — the main routes have signal, the side trails often don't. Download offline maps before entering. The Petra Authority and the major hotels can relay emergency messages.

Drugs. Cannabis and other drugs are illegal in Jordan and penalties are serious. Sellers occasionally approach tourists in Wadi Musa town offering hashish — decline firmly.

Photography of military or government facilities. Not relevant at Petra but worth knowing for the rest of your Jordan trip — military installations, government buildings, and border crossings should not be photographed.

Travel insurance. Worth having. The cost of a medical evacuation from Jordan to a European or American hospital is substantial; travel insurance with evacuation coverage is the standard recommendation for travelers doing any physical activity at Petra or in Wadi Rum.

Emergency contacts

  • General emergency in Jordan: 911
  • Tourist Police: 196 (general) or staff in the Petra Visitor Centre
  • Ambulance: 199
  • Petra Visitor Centre main: Available through your hotel

For US citizens, the US Embassy in Amman is 24/7 for emergencies (+962 6 590 6000). For UK citizens, the British Embassy in Amman (+962 6 590 9200). EU citizens: most major countries have embassy presence in Amman.

Frequently asked questions

Is Petra safe for tourists?
Yes. Petra and Jordan more broadly are among the safer destinations in the Middle East — political stability, low violent crime, an active tourism police force at major sites, and welcoming local culture. Travelers should still take basic precautions, but the major safety concerns at Petra are environmental (heat, falls, flash floods in extreme weather) rather than security-related.
What about flash floods in the Siq?
A real but uncommon risk. The Siq is a slot canyon and flash floods have killed visitors historically — most recently in November 2018, when at least 12 people died in a sudden flooding incident. The Petra Authority now monitors weather closely and closes the Siq when heavy rain is forecast. The practical rule: if it has been raining heavily or rain is forecast for the day of your visit, check with the Visitor Centre before entering, and accept that the site may close temporarily.
What's the biggest safety risk at Petra?
Heat exhaustion, particularly on the Monastery climb in summer. The 800-step ascent in 35°C heat is genuinely dangerous and has caused real medical emergencies. Drink water continuously, climb early (before 10 AM in summer), and turn back if you feel dizzy or stop sweating. The second-most-common risk is falls — the Nabataean stone steps are uneven and polished smooth, and a twisted ankle or worse is common. Wear proper shoes (see what-to-wear page) and don't hurry.
Is it safe for solo female travelers?
Generally yes, with the caveats that apply across the Middle East. Jordan is one of the safer destinations in the region for women traveling alone — the tourism infrastructure is established, the local culture is hospitable, and violent crime against tourists is rare. Some women report persistent commercial attention from Bedouin sellers and occasionally unwanted social interest. Dressing more modestly than necessary, traveling with a guide for the more remote trails, and using established hotels rather than informal homestays all reduce friction.
What's the emergency number in Jordan?
911 for general emergency. Specific to Petra, the Tourism Police maintain a presence at the Visitor Centre and along the main paths — they wear visible uniforms and most speak English. For medical emergencies at the site, flag down any uniformed staff member; the response system at Petra is reasonably well-developed. Have your hotel's number saved on your phone for non-emergency assistance.
Should I drink the tap water?
Tap water in Jordan is technically potable in most areas including Wadi Musa, but bottled water is the standard practice for tourists to avoid mild stomach issues from mineral differences. Buy bottled water at your hotel or the Visitor Centre. Inside Petra, water is available at Bedouin tea stalls (~2 JOD per bottle, rising to 3-4 JOD at the Monastery cafe). For long days on the trail, bring more water than you think you need.
