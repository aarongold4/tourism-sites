The High Place of Sacrifice

A Nabataean ritual plateau on top of Jebel Madbah, 170 meters above the main path through Petra. The shorter alternative to the Monastery climb, with the best descent route in Petra: through Wadi Farasa past the Lion Monument and the Garden Temple.

The High Place of Sacrifice is the most accessible of Petra's several Nabataean cultic plateaus, and the one most travelers should consider if they want a real climb but cannot commit to the 800-step Monastery ascent. The hike is roughly half the length of the Monastery trail (600 steps versus 800), the summit views are arguably even better, and — critically — the recommended descent through Wadi Farasa turns the High Place visit into a one-way loop that passes several of Petra's most interesting hidden monuments.

The name is partly a misnomer. The Arabic "al-Madhbah" means "slaughter place," which led 19th-century explorers to assume the plateau was used for animal sacrifice in the literal Old Testament sense. The current archaeological view is more nuanced: the plateau was a major Nabataean cultic site dedicated probably to the chief god Dushara and his consort Al-Uzza, with animal sacrifice as one ritual element among incense offerings, communal meals, and what may have been quarterly or annual pilgrimage festivals. The "blood drains" cut into the rock did channel something — but most likely libations and rinse water rather than the dramatic torrents that Victorian travelers imagined.

The climb

The trailhead is a signposted set of stone steps on the right side of the main path through Petra, just past the Roman theatre and before the colonnaded street begins. The first 200 meters of climbing are the steepest — concentrated switchbacks up a narrow gully called Wadi al-Mahfur. After that the trail eases, with several flatter sections and a couple of Bedouin tea stalls where you can rest. About two-thirds of the way up, the trail passes the two famous obelisks: large stone pillars, roughly six meters tall, carved in place by quarrying away the surrounding rock until the pillars stood free.

The obelisks themselves are interesting. They are not constructed monuments — every grain of stone in them was already there before the Nabataeans started carving. The work was about removal: cutting away the rock around two pre-marked pillars to leave them standing on the saddle. Most current interpretations read them as Nabataean nefeshes — memorial markers for the dead — rather than religious obelisks proper. The location, prominent and visible from below, may have been deliberately chosen to commemorate Petra's stonemasons.

From the obelisks, another 15 minutes of climbing brings you to the main plateau. The full ascent takes 45 minutes to an hour at a moderate pace.

The plateau

The High Place itself is a roughly 65-meter-long flat area carved out of the summit of Jebel Madbah. The Nabataean ritual installations on the plateau include:

The main altar block. A rectangular raised platform with steps cut into it on one side, situated at the eastern end of the plateau. This is what 19th-century travelers called the "sacrificial altar." Drainage channels carved into the rock around it would have channeled fluids — almost certainly libations rather than blood, despite the popular characterization.

The sunken courtyard. Adjacent to the altar, a roughly square area cut down into the bedrock. This may have functioned as a triclinium (a Roman-style banqueting room) where pilgrims would have shared a communal meal as part of the ritual.

The second altar. A smaller carved block at the western end of the plateau, oriented differently, possibly used for incense offerings rather than animal sacrifice.

The views. The plateau sits on the western edge of the city, looking out over the lower city, the Roman theatre, the Royal Tombs, and on a clear day all the way to the Wadi Araba rift valley toward Israel. This is arguably the best single panorama in Petra.

The Wadi Farasa descent

The standard recommendation: don't descend the same way you came up. Take the western path down into Wadi Farasa. The route is steeper than the east trail in places but passes some of Petra's most interesting hidden monuments, and the descent route exits at the colonnaded street rather than back at the theatre, making the whole excursion a one-way loop.

A few highlights along the Wadi Farasa route, in order as you descend:

Twelve Nabataean inscriptions. Carved into a smoothed rock face under a small overhang in the upper part of the path. These memorial inscriptions name several Nabataeans and are some of the better-preserved examples of Nabataean script in the city.

The Dushara betyl niche. Slightly off the trail, easy to miss. A combined aniconical block (representing the god Dushara) and a figurative bust (probably the deified King Obodas) in a high niche cut into the cliff face.

The Lion Monument. A large carved relief of a lion in the cliff face, with a basin below. Almost certainly originally a public fountain — water flowed from the lion's mouth into the basin, providing a drinking source for pilgrims descending from the High Place. The hydraulic engineering is gone but the carving remains striking.

The Garden Temple and Roman Soldier Tomb. Near the bottom of the route, a complex of monumental tombs and triclinium structures named after a relief of a Roman soldier on one of the facades. This area is one of Petra's quieter zones and worth the additional 20 minutes of exploration.

From the Roman Soldier Tomb, the path joins the colonnaded street and you can walk back toward the entrance via the Royal Tombs and the Treasury. Total time for the full High Place + Wadi Farasa loop: 3 to 4 hours from the trailhead at the theatre back to the Treasury area.

Monastery or High Place?

The most common decision question. The honest answer: if you only have one day at Petra and can fit only one high-altitude trail, do the Monastery. The facade at the top is the largest monument in Petra and the climb is the more iconic of the two. If you have two days, do both — Monastery on Day 1, High Place + Wadi Farasa loop on Day 2 (or vice versa). The two trails are different enough — Monastery is a destination, High Place is a circuit — that doing both is genuinely additive rather than repetitive.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get to the High Place of Sacrifice?
The trail starts on the right side of the path just past the Roman theatre, where you'll see a small signpost pointing up. The east route up Jebel Madbah is steep but well-engineered Nabataean stone steps, and takes about 45 minutes to an hour at a moderate pace. The plateau at the top is at approximately 170 meters above the main path. Most travelers do this as a half-day excursion from the Treasury area.
How long does it take?
Plan for 3 to 4 hours total: 45–60 minutes up, 30 minutes at the summit, then either 30–40 minutes back down the same path, or 90 minutes descending through Wadi Farasa with stops at the Lion Monument, Garden Temple, and Roman Soldier Tomb. The Wadi Farasa route is the better option — it produces a one-way loop rather than a there-and-back hike, and the descent passes some of Petra's most interesting hidden monuments.
Is it harder than the Monastery climb?
Shorter, similar difficulty. The High Place of Sacrifice climb is about 600 steps versus the Monastery's 800, but the elevation gain is concentrated more steeply at the start, which makes the first 15 minutes feel harder. Most travelers who climb both report the Monastery as the longer and more demanding hike, but the High Place as the steeper-feeling one. For travelers with limited time who want one high-place trail, the High Place + Wadi Farasa loop is the more efficient choice.
What's actually at the top?
A roughly 65-meter-long carved plateau on the summit of Jebel Madbah, with several Nabataean installations for ritual use: a rectangular sunken courtyard, a stepped altar, a smaller raised altar block, drainage channels carved into the rock, and the foundations of a small temple or shelter. Most likely, the chief god Dushara was worshipped here through incense offerings and animal sacrifice — though the Arabic name "al-Madhbah" (slaughter place) may overstate what actually happened: animals were probably killed below on the western path and brought up cleaned, with the high place itself reserved for the ritual meal.
What are the obelisks?
Two large stone pillars about six meters tall, standing on a saddle of the mountain just below the main plateau. They are not constructed obelisks — they are carved in place, with the surrounding rock quarried away to leave them standing. Most scholars now interpret them as Nabataean nefeshes (memorial markers for the dead) rather than religious obelisks proper, possibly commemorating quarrymen who died working on Petra's monumental architecture.
What's the descent through Wadi Farasa like?
A 90-minute walk through one of Petra's most overlooked areas. The path is steep at first, then levels out as it joins the valley floor. Along the way: twelve Nabataean memorial inscriptions carved on a rock face, a large carved Lion Monument (probably a fountain in antiquity, with water emerging from the lion's mouth into a pool), the Garden Temple, and the elaborate Roman Soldier Tomb complex. The route ends back at the colonnaded street in the lower city, completing a full loop without retracing steps.
