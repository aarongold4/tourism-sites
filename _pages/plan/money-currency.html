Money & the lari

Georgia runs on the lari (GEL), taps cards almost everywhere, and remains one of Europe's kindest capitals to a budget — with a short list of cash moments and machine traps worth knowing cold.

Last reviewed: June 7, 2026

The headline is friction-free: contactless cards pay for the metro and buses directly at the gate, restaurants and shops tap without ceremony, and Bolt renders taxi cash obsolete. The cash economy survives precisely where the trip gets textured — the Dry Bridge's blankets, family wineries in Kakheti, guesthouse payments, the bath attendant's scrub fee, and mountain extras like the Gergeti 4x4 — so the working pattern is a modest float in small notes, refreshed from sensible ATMs.

"Sensible" means two disciplines. Compare ATM operator fees (machines vary; central bank-branch ATMs behave best), and refuse dynamic currency conversion everywhere it's offered — the screen proposing to charge your home currency is selling you the worst exchange rate in the building. Pay in lari, always, on every machine and terminal, and your own bank's rate does the rest.

What budgets actually feel like

Recent published comparisons keep Tbilisi firmly among Europe's cheapest capitals, and the texture matters more than any single number: eating brilliantly is the budget option, transit is statistically free, and sleeping well costs little — so the categories that move a budget are the chosen luxuries: small-group tours, the design-hotel tier, and a natural-wine habit that the city makes dangerously easy. Tipping stays gentle (check bills for included service before adding ten percent), haggling lives only in the markets, and the overall sensation — for travelers arriving from the West — is of prices from a kinder decade. Exact figures drift with the lari; the structure above doesn't.

Cards, cash, and what things cost

The currency is the Georgian lari (GEL), and the practical picture is easy: cards work nearly everywhere a tourist spends — restaurants, hotels, supermarkets, Bolt — and contactless is the norm, including on the metro and buses. ATMs from the major banks (TBC, Bank of Georgia) are plentiful and reliable; withdraw a modest cash float and keep small notes for the places cards don't reach: the sulfur baths, the Dry Bridge market, bakeries, churchkhela stalls, the Gergeti 4x4, and rural day-trip stops. Watch your own bank's foreign-transaction and ATM fees, and always decline the machine's offer to “convert to your home currency” (dynamic currency conversion) — choosing to be charged in lari is reliably cheaper.

The headline most visitors love is that Georgia is genuinely affordable. A generous sit-down dinner with wine runs single-digit to low-double-digit dollars per person at most local restaurants; city transit and Bolt cost pocket change; and the food and wine that anchors a trip is the budget option, not the splurge. Tipping is gentle: many restaurants fold a service charge into the bill (check before adding more), and where they don't, rounding up or roughly ten percent is generous; for drivers and guides a tip is appreciated rather than expected. Currency exchange offices (look for genuinely fee-free rates near Liberty Square and Rustaveli) beat airport counters, but with cards this universal you'll exchange far less cash than you expect.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need cash at all?

Some, always. Cards tap everywhere modern — supermarkets, restaurants, the metro and buses — but the cash economy persists exactly where trips get good: market stalls, the Dry Bridge, small guesthouses, the bath attendant’s kisa fee, rural day-trip stops, and the Gergeti 4x4 (20 GEL, cash only). Carry a modest float in small notes and replenish from ATMs as needed.

What’s the ATM and conversion trap to avoid?

Two, both universal: ATM operator fees (compare a couple of machines; bank-branch ATMs in the center behave best) and dynamic currency conversion — the screen offering to charge you in your home currency. Always decline DCC and pay in lari (GEL); your own bank’s rate beats the machine’s every time.

How does tipping work?

Gently. Many restaurants add a service percentage to the bill — check before doubling up. Where it isn’t added, around ten percent is generous; rounding up satisfies taxis and cafes; the bath mekise is paid their scrub fee in cash directly, rounded up if they earned it. Nothing is demanded anywhere.

What does a day actually cost?

Published comparisons consistently place Tbilisi among Europe’s cheapest capitals: hearty restaurant meals for single-digit dollars’ equivalent, transit for pocket change, generous budget rooms, and even the design-hotel tier undercutting Western peers. The honest summary: food and transport feel nearly free; tours, design hotels, and wine habits are where budgets actually move.

Should I exchange money before arriving?

No need — airport ATMs work on arrival, city exchange offices post fair rates for major currencies, and card acceptance covers the gaps from hour one. Arrive with a working card and patience; the lari comes to you.
