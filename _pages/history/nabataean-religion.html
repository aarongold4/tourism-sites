Nabataean religion

A polytheistic Arab religious tradition centered on the chief god Dushara and the principal goddess Allat. Sacred stones rather than figurative statues, high places rather than enclosed temples, and a gradual 4th-5th century transition to Christianity. Here's what shaped the religious landscape of Petra.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 7-minute read · See our editorial standards

Nabataean religion is one of the less-discussed aspects of Petra, partly because the surviving evidence is more fragmentary than the architectural record. We don't have a complete Nabataean theological text or a continuous tradition of religious history; what we know comes from inscriptions, the surviving religious architecture, the carved religious imagery throughout Petra and other Nabataean sites, and references in Greek and Roman sources. Despite the fragmentary evidence, the broad outline of Nabataean religious practice is reasonably clear — and understanding it adds significant depth to a Petra visit.

This page covers the principal Nabataean gods, the distinctive religious traditions (the betyl tradition of representing deities as sacred stones, the high-place tradition of open-air mountain sanctuaries), the surviving religious architecture at Petra, and the gradual transition to Christianity in the late Roman and Byzantine periods. The framing throughout: this is the religious context that explains many features you see at Petra.

The principal deities

Dushara

The chief Nabataean god, the principal male deity of the pantheon. Dushara was associated with mountains (his name means roughly "the one of [the mountain] Shara" — referencing a sacred mountain near Petra), with fertility and abundance, and with royal protection. He was the patron deity of the Nabataean royal house and the principal recipient of major civic religious dedications.

Dushara was typically represented by a betyl — a sacred stone, sometimes plain, sometimes with abstract carving suggesting facial features (eyes, sometimes a nose) but without full figurative anthropomorphism. The aniconic representation was distinctive; while Hellenistic and Roman cult statues commonly used full human form, the Nabataean tradition preserved an older Semitic-Arab preference for non-figurative or minimally-figurative divine representation.

In later periods (1st century CE onward), some Nabataean religious art shows Dushara in more anthropomorphic forms, probably reflecting Greek and Roman cultural influence. But the betyl tradition continued alongside these newer forms and remained the more characteristically Nabataean approach.

Allat (and her counterparts)

The principal female deity, variously called Allat, al-Uzza, or Manat depending on the period and region. (These may have been three separate goddesses, three names for one goddess, or a goddess complex — the surviving evidence doesn't fully resolve the question.) Allat was associated with celestial bodies, particularly the planet Venus and possibly the sun, with protection and fertility, and with maternal/feminine attributes generally.

Like Dushara, Allat was often represented by a betyl, sometimes with abstract carved features. Later periods show more anthropomorphic representations, particularly in regions with stronger Greek influence (the famous statue of Allat from Palmyra is a Hellenized depiction).

The broader pantheon

Beyond Dushara and Allat, the Nabataeans worshipped a broader pantheon including local mountain spirits, ancestral deities, and various Hellenistic and Egyptian gods absorbed through trade and cultural contact. Isis (the Egyptian goddess) appears in Nabataean iconography from the 1st century BCE onward; Dionysus, Tyche (Fortune), and other Greek deities were incorporated into Nabataean religious vocabulary during the period of strongest Hellenistic influence.

The pantheon's openness to incorporating foreign deities was characteristic of ancient polytheism more broadly. Nabataean religion accommodated both the indigenous Arab tradition and broader Mediterranean influences without internal contradiction — the same person could honor Dushara as the chief god and recognize Isis or Tyche as legitimate divine forces in their respective domains.

The betyl tradition

The use of sacred stones to represent deities is one of the most distinctive features of Nabataean religion. The word "betyl" comes from the Semitic bet-el ("house of god") and refers to a religious tradition shared across the broader ancient Near East — Hebrew, Phoenician, Aramaean, and Arab religious cultures all used sacred stones in various forms.

At Petra and other Nabataean sites, betyls take several forms:

Plain rectangular blocks. The simplest form — a rectangular stone, often standing 0.5-2 meters tall, sometimes set on a base, representing a deity without any figurative carving. Many of the carved niches throughout Petra originally held this kind of betyl stone.

Minimally-carved betyls. Stones with abstract carved features — typically simplified eyes and occasionally a nose, but no mouth or other facial detail. The "god-block" formulation suggests divine presence without claiming full anthropomorphic representation.

More elaborately decorated betyls. Stones with carved frames, decorative borders, or inscriptions identifying the specific deity. These show influence from broader Mediterranean religious art while maintaining the basic non-figurative form.

The carved niches throughout Petra — small rectangular cavities cut into the rock face, sometimes with framing details — were typically betyl shrines. The stones themselves are mostly gone (taken for reuse or lost over centuries) but the niches remain. Once you know what to look for, betyl niches appear all over Petra: along the Siq, at the Treasury plaza, in the lower city, along the trails to the High Place and Monastery.

The high places

The high-place tradition is the second distinctively Nabataean religious feature visible at Petra. High places are open-air ritual sites on elevated locations — mountain summits, prominent rocky outcrops, ridge lines — used for sacrifices and religious gatherings. The tradition continues older Semitic-Arab religious practice (the Hebrew Bible repeatedly references "high places" of Canaanite religion, using the same religious form).

The major Nabataean high places at Petra:

The High Place of Sacrifice (Madhbah). The most visited and most accessible. A 30-45 minute climb above the Royal Tombs leads to a flat summit with carved altars, water channels for ritual cleansing, drainage channels for sacrificial blood, and a paired obelisk arrangement at the highest point. The site was used for animal sacrifice and possibly for processional ceremonies. The carved features are partly preserved and clearly visible during a visit.

Jebel Ma'aiserat. A smaller high place on the mountain north of the Treasury, less visited but with similar features (altars, drainage channels). Accessed via informal trails from the lower city.

Other high places. Multiple smaller high-place sites exist on the mountains around Petra, varying in scale and preservation. Some are accessible via the back routes; others require dedicated hiking.

The high-place tradition was a major religious form during the Nabataean period and continued under Roman rule until Christianization gradually closed the open-air pagan ritual sites in the 4th-5th centuries.

Temples and other religious architecture

The Nabataeans built enclosed temples in addition to the high places — the major examples at Petra:

Qasr al-Bint. The largest free-standing temple at Petra, probably dedicated to Dushara. See the Qasr al-Bint page for the architectural detail. The building stood as the religious anchor of the western civic axis.

The Temple of the Winged Lions. Across the colonnaded street from Qasr al-Bint, this temple is probably dedicated to a goddess (Allat or possibly Isis). The winged-lion sculptures that give the temple its modern name are visible at the on-site Petra Museum.

The Great Temple complex. The function of this large structure is debated; it may have served religious, civic, and royal functions in combination. See the Great Temple page.

Beyond Petra, the temple complex at Khirbet et-Tannur (about 100 km north of Petra in central Jordan) preserves the most complete Nabataean temple architecture outside Petra itself. The complex was dedicated to Dushara and Allat and is one of the best sources for understanding Nabataean religious art.

Religious art

Nabataean religious art combines aniconic (non-figurative) and figurative traditions in distinctive ways. The aniconic tradition (betyls) coexists with figurative sculpture showing Dushara, Allat, and other deities in increasingly anthropomorphic forms during the late Nabataean and Roman periods. The result is a religious-artistic vocabulary distinct from both the dominant Greek-Roman figurative tradition and the rigorously aniconic later Islamic tradition.

Surviving Nabataean religious art:

  • Carved betyl niches and standing betyls throughout Petra and other Nabataean sites
  • The figurative sculpture from the Temple of the Winged Lions and Khirbet et-Tannur
  • Decorative reliefs on tomb facades (the Treasury's figurative sculpture, the Monastery's carved details, the Royal Tombs' decorative panels)
  • Smaller portable religious objects (terracotta figurines, ceramic vessels with religious imagery, ritual bronze objects) in museum collections

The transition to Christianity

Nabataean pagan religion didn't end abruptly. The Roman annexation of 106 CE didn't change the religious practice of the local population significantly — Romans accommodated local religious traditions and continued Nabataean worship of Dushara, Allat, and the broader pantheon. Religious practice continued essentially unchanged for another 200 years under Roman rule.

The major shift came with the Christianization of the Roman Empire in the 4th-5th centuries. Constantine's recognition of Christianity (early 4th century) and the gradual establishment of Christianity as the imperial religion brought Christian institutions to Petra. By the 5th century, the city had a significant Christian community, building churches over earlier pagan sites and creating the Byzantine Christian community whose churches and mosaics are partially preserved today.

The transition was gradual rather than violent. Some pagan practice continued at remote rural sites longer than at the urban center; some elements of the older religious vocabulary survived in Christian context (saint cults at former high-place locations, Christian appropriation of older pilgrimage routes); and the broader Bedouin population probably maintained some elements of pre-Christian religious practice into the early Islamic period.

Frequently asked questions

Who were the Nabataean gods?
The principal deity was Dushara, the chief Nabataean god — a male figure usually depicted as a mountain god, associated with both protection and fertility. The major female deity was Allat (alternatively al-Uzza or Manat in different periods), the principal goddess. Beyond these two, the Nabataeans worshipped a broader pantheon including local mountain spirits, ancestral deities, and (through trade contact) various Hellenistic and Egyptian gods. The religion was polytheistic but with a clear top tier of Dushara and Allat at the center.
What are betyls?
Sacred stones representing deities, often without figurative carving. The word comes from the Semitic bet-el (literally "house of god") and refers to a religious tradition shared across the broader ancient Near East where deities were represented by unworked or minimally-carved stones rather than by anthropomorphic statues. The Nabataeans used betyls extensively — many of the niches you see carved into the rock at Petra originally housed betyl stones. The tradition reflects the broader Semitic and Arab aniconic religious preference.
Did the Nabataeans have temples?
Yes, but the relationship between temples and religious practice was different from Greek and Roman conventions. Qasr al-Bint at Petra is the largest surviving Nabataean temple, probably dedicated to Dushara. Smaller temples and shrines existed at Petra and at other Nabataean sites (the temple complex at Khirbet et-Tannur is particularly well-preserved). High places — open-air ritual sites on mountaintops, with carved altars and processional paths — were a distinctively Nabataean and broader Arab religious feature. The High Place of Sacrifice at Petra is the most famous example.
What were the high places?
Open-air ritual sites on elevated locations, used for sacrifices and religious gatherings. The Nabataean tradition continued an older Semitic and Arab practice of mountain-top religious sites — the Hebrew Bible repeatedly references "high places" in connection with Canaanite religion, and the Nabataean version is the same religious form. The High Place of Sacrifice at Petra is the most accessible example — a 30-minute climb above the Royal Tombs to a flat summit with carved altars, water channels for ritual cleansing, and a small obelisk pair. Other high places at Petra are visible on adjacent mountains.
When did the Nabataeans convert to Christianity?
Gradually, during the 4th-5th centuries CE. The conversion of the broader Roman Empire to Christianity under Constantine (early 4th century) and his successors brought Christian institutions to the Nabataean territories. By the 5th century, Petra had a significant Christian population, building churches over earlier pagan sites and creating the Byzantine Christian community whose churches and mosaics are partially preserved today. Some pagan practice continued at remote sites longer, but the major shift happened over about 150 years.
