The Sphinx: evidence and myth

No monument attracts theories like this one. Here's the full file — what the converging evidence shows, what's honestly debated, and why the spectacular claims keep failing the ground they stand on.

Last reviewed June 6, 2026◆9-minute read

Photo: kallerna · CC BY-SA 3.0 · Wikimedia Commons

The Great Sphinx is the world's oldest colossal statue and its most theorized: a 73-meter lion with a king's head, carved from the living bedrock of the Giza plateau, and buried, re-dug, restored, worshipped, defaced, and argued over for four and a half thousand years. The visiting experience is covered in the viewing guide; this page is the history — evidence first, debates second, myths last, each given its honest weight.

The case for Khafre

No inscription on the Sphinx names its maker — the starting fact every theory exploits. The attribution to Khafre rests instead on convergence. Geology: the statue was carved by quarrying a horseshoe trench into the plateau, and the quarried stone is traceable into the construction of Khafre's complex — the Sphinx is, physically, a byproduct of his building program. Architecture: it sits beside Khafre's valley temple, on the axis of his causeway, with the so-called Sphinx Temple in front of it built from the enclosure's own blocks in the same masonry style. Iconography: the nemes-crowned royal head matches Fourth Dynasty portraiture, conventionally Khafre's — though the face's weathering keeps that reading soft. The genuine scholarly minority view shifts the credit within the family — to Khufu, or to the short-reigned Djedefre honoring his father — which is what the real debate looks like: an argument over which king of one generation, conducted in quarry logistics and masonry joints.

The Dream Stela and the long afterlife

The Sphinx's documented history is mostly the history of sand. Within centuries it was buried to the neck; around 1400 BC the prince who became Thutmose IV recorded — on the granite Dream Stela still standing between the paws — that the statue promised him the throne in exchange for excavation. He dug, he reigned, and the New Kingdom made the Sphinx a god: Horemakhet, Horus in the Horizon, with its own temple, cult, and royal pilgrimages. Further clearances and restorations followed under the Saites, the Romans, and — after the sand won again — the excavators of the 19th and 20th centuries; the fully exposed monument is substantially a 1930s achievement, and the masonry skirts on its lower body are layered restoration from pharaonic times to the present, conservation being the Sphinx's permanent condition.

The nose, examined

The break preserves tool marks: the nose was deliberately chiseled off, well before Napoleon — pre-Napoleonic drawings already show it missing, retiring the cannonball story. The most cited account, from the medieval historian al-Maqrizi, attributes the damage to a 14th-century iconoclast outraged by peasants making offerings to the statue. The ceremonial beard, fragments of which sit in the British Museum and Cairo, appears to have been a New Kingdom addition rather than original equipment — the Sphinx was modified by its own civilization for a thousand years.

The erosion debate, honestly

The strongest-sounding alternative claim deserves its fair hearing. The water-erosion hypothesis observes deep, rounded weathering in the Sphinx enclosure's walls and argues it required prolonged heavy rainfall — pushing the carving back to 7000 BC or earlier, before Egypt's desertification. The mainstream response, from both geologists and Egyptologists: the enclosure's limestone is sharply banded in quality, and its weathering profile tracks those bands; salt crystallization and capillary moisture replicate the rounded forms without ancient downpours; known wet intervals within the dynastic era cover the remainder; and — the decisive context — a monument-carving culture millennia before Djoser would have to have left other traces in a valley that is the most excavated landscape on Earth, and left none. A real anomaly would be thrilling; this one dissolves under the geology it invokes.

The Hall of Records and the void industry

The hidden chamber of wisdom beneath the paws originates not in any text or excavation but in the trance readings of the American psychic Edgar Cayce in the 1930s. Decades of subsequent surveys — drilling, seismic, ground-penetrating radar — have mapped the bedrock and found natural fissures and three small, empty, documented cavities. The pattern generalizes: Giza's genuine mysteries (the muon-detected voids in the Great Pyramid, the unexcavated plateau) are real and under active study; the manufactured ones survive by never being specific enough to dig.

Why the myths persist — and why the truth is better

The Sphinx attracts projection because it is genuinely strange: colossal, faceless with age, older than almost everything, and silent. But the evidenced story outclasses the fictions — a king's quarry waste turned into the first colossus; a statue that outlived its own name, got a second career as a god, and bargained a prince onto the throne; four millennia of sand wars still ongoing. The fuller Fourth Dynasty context is in the Old Kingdom in brief, and the people who carved it in who built the pyramids.

The conservation century

The modern Sphinx is as much a conservation story as an ancient one, and the episode worth knowing is the 1980s cement crisis: well-intentioned repairs using hard mortar and oversized blocks trapped moisture against the soft middle strata, accelerating exactly the decay they meant to stop, until a shoulder block's dramatic 1988 fall forced the reckoning. The response became a model — the hard repairs removed, breathable lime mortars and hand-cut limestone matched to the original, continuous monitoring of groundwater and salt, and the acceptance that the statue is a patient under permanent care rather than a problem to be solved once. That history explains what you see from the enclosure: the patchwork of repair generations on the lower body, the scaffolding that migrates seasonally, and the access rules that keep visitors off the soft stone. It also frames the right response to every "the authorities are hiding something behind the barriers" video: what the barriers protect is four-and-a-half millennia of soft limestone from the only force that has ever genuinely threatened it at scale — us, in large numbers, well-meaning and heavy-footed.

Standing before it, after all this

What the file above buys you at the enclosure rail: the strata of the erosion debate visible as actual bands in the body; the repair generations readable as a timeline in masonry; Thutmose's stela between the paws as the oldest restoration receipt on Earth; the alignment to Khafre's causeway answering the authorship question in plain sight; and the scaffolding, when present, as the latest sentence in a conservation story longer than most civilizations. The Sphinx rewards informed looking more than any single monument at Giza — the mystery-industry version offers secrets it cannot show you, while the evidence version is carved into the statue where anyone can check it.

Frequently asked questions

Who built the Sphinx?

The consensus, resting on converging evidence: Khafre, builder of Giza's second pyramid, around 2500 BC. The statue sits beside his valley temple, aligned with his causeway, carved from his builders' quarry, and the face has long been read as his royal portrait. A minority view assigns it to his father Khufu or brother Djedefre — a real scholarly debate, but one about which Fourth Dynasty king, not which civilization.

Is the Sphinx thousands of years older than the pyramids?

The 'water erosion hypothesis' argues the enclosure's weathering requires heavy rainfall ending long before 2500 BC. Mainstream geology and Egyptology have examined and rejected it: the limestone's banded quality, salt weathering, and known wet intervals account for the erosion, and a vastly older Sphinx would require an advanced culture that left no other trace in Egypt's exhaustively excavated record. It remains the internet's favorite Giza claim and the field's settled question.

What is under the Sphinx?

Bedrock, a few natural fissures, and three small documented cavities that lead nowhere — mapped by decades of drilling, seismic, and radar surveys. The 'Hall of Records' is a 1930s psychic's prophecy, not archaeology. The genuinely interesting voids at Giza are inside the Great Pyramid, found by muon scanning — covered on the construction page.

Why is it called the Sphinx?

It isn't — originally. The Greeks applied their word (their sphinx was a female riddle-monster) two thousand years after construction. New Kingdom Egyptians called it Horemakhet, 'Horus in the Horizon,' and worshipped it as a god in its own right; its original Fourth Dynasty name is unknown.
