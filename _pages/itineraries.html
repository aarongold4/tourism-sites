ITINERARIES

Itineraries built on ordering, not wish lists

Most Giza itineraries fail on sequencing — wrong entrance, wrong hour, museum legs in the wrong order. These plans are built around opening times, traffic reality, and where the crowds will be when you are.

Last reviewed June 6, 2026◆6-minute read

Photo: kallerna · CC BY-SA 3.0 · Wikimedia Commons

Giza rewards sequencing more than almost any major site. Arrive at the gate at 8am opening and you'll have the plateau in soft light with thin crowds; arrive at 11am and you'll share it with every tour bus in Cairo under vertical sun. Add the Saqqara and Dahshur legs and the ordering question compounds — which site first, where lunch fits, how Cairo traffic shapes the return.

The itineraries in this cluster are built plans, not lists: timed, ordered, with the decision points marked. Take them whole or use them as scaffolding.

The five rules every good Giza day obeys

One: own the opening. The plateau's 7am hour is the trip's scarcest resource — softest light, coolest air, thinnest crowds, freshest interior-ticket allocation — and every plan here spends it there (the full timing logic). Two: cross Cairo as little as possible. The river crossing is 45-90 unpredictable minutes; good days cluster their stops by bank. Three: museums take afternoons. GEM and the Egyptian Museum are air-conditioned; the desert is not — sequence accordingly. Four: book the capacity-limited things first. Great Pyramid interior tickets and peak-season show seats drive the schedule; everything else flexes around them. Five: end days early. Cairo evenings reward a rooftop and a rest more than a forced fourth site — underplanning the back half of each day is the veteran move.

Matching the plan to your trip shape

Cruise stop or layover: the half-day plan — plateau at opening, viewpoint, Sphinx, out by noon — is purpose-built for hard deadlines, with the layover variant adding the airport math. One full day: the classic pairing of a pyramid morning with a GEM afternoon, or — our stronger recommendation when transport is arranged — the three-site day (Giza, Saqqara, Dahshur) that trades the museum for the full engineering story in stone. Two days: the canonical structure: day one west bank (plateau morning, GEM afternoon), day two south (Saqqara and Dahshur) with Memphis as the lunch stop. Three or more: add the Egyptian Museum on Tahrir, Islamic Cairo's mosque-and-bazaar circuit, and Coptic Cairo — at which point you've done Cairo properly and the Luxor question opens (Egypt Travel takes it from there).

The transport spine

Every itinerary here runs on the same three solutions: ride-hailing for the city legs (Uber and Careem both work well — specifics in Getting There), a hired car-and-driver or private tour for the Saqqara-Dahshur day where no practical alternative exists, and feet plus the free shuttle on the plateau itself. Hotel location sets the difficulty of each morning, which is why the hotels cluster and this one assume each other: the split stay — a pyramid-side night before the plateau morning, central Cairo otherwise — is the structural cheat that makes every plan below run smoother.

Budgeting the days

Rough per-person math for the standard plans, excluding hotels: the independent half day runs about $25-35 (rides plus the 700 EGP base ticket); the full independent day with GEM roughly $50-70 with interiors and museum admission; the private three-site day $80-130 all-in with tickets and tips; and the two-day program $150-250 depending on interior choices and guide use. Egypt's ticket prices step up frequently — the numbers above lean on the fees guide, which carries its own review date.

Seasonal adjustments to every plan

Winter peak (Dec-Feb): the plans run as written, with two amendments — book the capacity-limited pieces (Great Pyramid interior, show seats) days ahead, and carry a real layer: 7am on the plateau starts near 10°C. Summer (Jun-Aug): compress everything toward the edges — plateau at opening sharp, indoors (GEM, the Tahrir museum) by 11, optional second outdoor window after 4pm; hydration becomes logistics, not advice. Ramadan:sites open later and close earlier (the shifted windows), restaurants run irregular daytime hours outside hotels, and evenings turn festive — shift the museum legs later and join the post-iftar city. Shoulder months (Apr-May, Sep-Oct): the plans at their best, no amendments.

The sequencing mistakes these plans prevent

The recurring ways Giza days go wrong, each engineered out above: arriving mid-morning into peak crowd and heat (the 7am rule); museum-first days that spend cool hours indoors and hot ones on sand; crossing Cairo twice in a day (one bank per day); discovering the interior allocation sold out at noon (pre-buy); stacking a fourth site onto a tired afternoon (end early); and treating Saqqara as a maybe (it's the second-best thing in this guide — the case).

Packing for the plans

Every itinerary above assumes the same daypack: real walking shoes, brimmed hat, sunscreen applied before arrival, more water than seems reasonable, small bills for the informal economy and a card for the official one (how payment works now), the ticket QRs saved offline, and a layer in winter. The full kit logic lives in the Plan Your Visit cluster; the point here is that no plan on this page needs more than a small bag — and the interiors punish big ones.

Energy budgeting: the resource the plans actually allocate

Every itinerary in this cluster is secretly an energy budget. The plateau days spend legs and heat tolerance; the museum afternoons spend attention; the city evenings spend social bandwidth — and the plans sequence them so no two adjacent blocks draw on the same account. The practical corollaries: jet-lagged arrivals are an asset, not a problem (the 5am wake-up is exactly what the 7am gate wants — schedule the pyramid day first and spend the jet lag before it fades); interiors are the highest-cost line items and two in one day is the veteran's maximum; and the afternoon collapse around day three is a feature to plan for, not a failure — the three-day program's gentle third morning exists because of it. Travelers who override the budget — four sites, two interiors, and a bazaar in one day — consistently report the same result: day four spent horizontal.

The contingency day: khamsin, closures, and the art of the swap

Build one swap into any multi-day plan and most disruptions become trivia. The mechanics: the city day (museum + Islamic Cairo) has no 7am dependency and no weather exposure, which makes it the universal donor — a spring sandstorm, an unexpected site closure, or a rough night all resolve by promoting the city day and demoting the outdoor one. The pyramid day itself should never be the trip's last day for exactly this reason: a Giza visit with no tomorrow behind it has no insurance. Cruise passengers and layover visitors, who can't carry a spare day, buy their insurance differently — hard return-time guarantees and operators whose business is the buffer (that math).

Eating your way through the plans

The itineraries name their meal anchors deliberately, because Cairo punishes improvised midday hunger and rewards planned eating. The pattern across all of them: a real breakfast (hotel buffet or village rooftop — the day's cheapest performance enhancer), the lunch as the day's hinge (Mena House terrace on plateau day, the Saqqara-road gardens on the southern day, harbor fish if Alexandria happens), and dinner where the evening already is — rooftops facing floodlights, Zamalek's restaurant streets, the old city's grills after the bazaar. Street food slots in as texture, bought by the busy-stall rule. The one anti-pattern worth naming: the "we'll find something at the site" plan, which at Giza means a visitors'-center café or desert-priced vendor water, and at Saqqara means nothing at all. The plans eat before they're hungry; so should you.

Choosing your plan in sixty seconds

The cluster's decision tree, compressed. Count your full days in Cairo. Half a day or a layover: the half-day route or the layover plan — the compressed plateau, nothing else. One day: the pyramids-plus-GEM classic, no variations needed. Two days: add the southern triangle, the trip's best upgrade. Three days: add the city day and you've completed the set this site considers the canonical Cairo. Four-plus: depth (Meresankh, second museum passes, Coptic mornings, Alexandria) or distance (the Luxor week). Then apply your two modifiers: season (summer compresses everything toward dawn; Ramadan shifts the clocks) and party (kids and limited mobility take the family and accessibility playbooks wholesale). Every named plan links from this page; every plan assumes the same three constants — tickets bought the night before, the 7am gate, one bank of the Nile per day — and every plan survives contact with reality better the earlier you accept that the constants are the plan.

The cluster's promise

What every plan above is actually selling: not a schedule but a guarantee that the trip's scarce resources — golden hours, knees, attention, patience — get spent where they buy the most. Follow any of them loosely rather than literally; swap freely along the marked joints; and keep only the constants, because they are the plans: tickets the night before, the 7am gate, one bank per day, lunch as the hinge, and out of the heat by the hour the heat stops being interesting. Everything else is preference, and the pages exist to make your preferences cheap to have.

Frequently asked questions

Is half a day enough for the pyramids?

For the Giza plateau alone, yes — a well-ordered morning covers the three pyramids, the viewpoint, and the Sphinx in 3-4 hours. What half a day can't do is add Saqqara and Dahshur, which is where the visit goes from photo stop to education. If you have one full day, the three-site combination is the strongest single day in Egypt.

Should I do Giza on my first day in Cairo or my last?

First morning if you can. Jet lag works in your favor — early waking puts you at the gate at opening, which is the single best crowd and light strategy available. It also leaves slack later in the trip if anything goes wrong.

How many days do you need in Cairo total?

Three full days covers the essentials without rushing: one for Giza (or Giza + Saqqara + Dahshur), one for the Egyptian Museum / Grand Egyptian Museum and Islamic Cairo, one for Saqqara if not yet covered, Coptic Cairo, and Khan el-Khalili. Two days works with discipline; four adds breathing room.
