HISTORY

The New Seven Wonders vote

On July 7, 2007, Petra was named one of the New Seven Wonders of the World — alongside the Great Wall, the Colosseum, Christ the Redeemer, Machu Picchu, Chichen Itza, and the Taj Mahal. The vote was commercial and controversial, but the impact on Petra tourism was enormous and lasting.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 6-minute read · See our editorial standards

For most people who've heard of Petra, the route to that awareness ran through the 2007 New Seven Wonders of the World designation. Before 2007, Petra was known primarily to history-engaged Western travelers, biblical-archaeological enthusiasts, Hollywood-cinematography aficionados (the Indiana Jones connection), and the broader middle-Eastern-travel community. After 2007, Petra was known to virtually everyone on the global tourism map as one of the seven most significant heritage sites on Earth.

The transition was driven by a commercial, controversial, and methodologically dubious internet vote — and the results have nonetheless been substantial and lasting for Petra. This page covers what happened in 2007, who organized it, the controversies around the process, and the impact on Petra's continuing role as a global tourism destination.

The vote itself

The New 7 Wonders Foundation, a Swiss-based private organization founded by the filmmaker and entrepreneur Bernard Weber, organized the New Seven Wonders campaign over roughly seven years (2000-2007). The basic structure:

The initial nomination phase. Around 200+ sites were nominated globally as candidates. Sources included government tourism agencies, cultural institutions, and individual nominations submitted through the foundation's website.

The narrowing process. A panel of architects and cultural figures (chaired by Federico Mayor, former UNESCO director-general, though UNESCO itself didn't endorse the project) narrowed the candidates to 21 finalists. The criteria emphasized architectural significance, cultural value, and broad geographic representation.

The public voting phase. From 2005 to mid-2007, the public voted on the 21 finalists via the foundation's website and phone services. The voting allowed unlimited votes per person, with paid SMS voting available as a revenue stream for the foundation. The foundation claimed approximately 100 million total votes, though the count is disputed.

The announcement. The results were announced at a ceremony in Lisbon, Portugal on July 7, 2007 (the choice of the 07/07/07 date was deliberate marketing). The seven winners were:

  • The Great Wall of China
  • Petra (Jordan)
  • Christ the Redeemer (Brazil)
  • Machu Picchu (Peru)
  • Chichen Itza (Mexico)
  • The Colosseum (Italy)
  • The Taj Mahal (India)

The Great Pyramid of Giza was subsequently named an honorary eighth wonder after the Egyptian government protested its initial inclusion as a competing candidate (Egypt argued the pyramid was already a recognized original Seven Wonder and shouldn't compete with newer sites).

The controversies

The New Seven Wonders process has been criticized on several fronts:

UNESCO distance. UNESCO, the United Nations cultural heritage organization, publicly stated in 2007 that it had "no link" with the New 7 Wonders Foundation and that the foundation's selection process didn't meet academic or cultural-heritage standards. UNESCO emphasized that its own World Heritage Site designation (Petra was already on the UNESCO list since 1985) was the authoritative international cultural heritage recognition.

Vote methodology. The unlimited-vote-per-person structure meant the results reflected organized voting campaigns as much as genuine global opinion. Countries with strong tourism marketing organizations (Jordan, Peru, Brazil, India in particular) conducted aggressive vote-mobilization campaigns, including paid SMS voting and government-encouraged participation. Whether the final list reflects "the world's choice" or "the world's marketing budgets" is a fair question.

Commercial dimensions. The New 7 Wonders Foundation generated significant revenue through the voting process (paid SMS votes, sponsorship arrangements, branding partnerships, ceremony events). The commercial structure raised questions about whether the project was primarily a cultural-heritage exercise or primarily a profit-making venture wearing cultural-heritage clothing.

Selection biases. Critics noted that the 21 finalists and the seven winners over-represented sites in countries with strong tourism marketing infrastructure and under-represented sites in regions with less developed tourism economies. The final seven includes major sites from China, Latin America, Europe, and South Asia, but excludes sub-Saharan Africa entirely, includes only one Middle Eastern site (Petra), and has limited representation of Pacific or Southeast Asian sites.

Egyptian government objection. Egypt's protest about the Great Pyramid's inclusion was loud and public. Egypt argued the pyramid was already a recognized wonder and shouldn't be put through a popular vote against newer sites; the foundation's response (honorary eighth status) was a compromise that didn't fully satisfy the Egyptian position.

Why Petra was chosen

Despite the methodological controversies, the New Seven Wonders result on Petra was substantively justified. Petra had strong claims:

Architectural significance. The combination of carved monumental architecture (the Treasury, the Monastery), the integration with dramatic natural setting (the Siq, the surrounding cliff geography), and the broader urban-archaeological complex (the colonnaded street, the Royal Tombs, Qasr al-Bint) gave Petra a strong case on cultural and architectural merit.

Cultural significance. The Nabataean civilization, the trade-route history, the religious-architectural integration, and the broader cultural arc from antiquity through the present (including the modern Bedouin community) provided substantial cultural-historical depth.

Visual recognizability. The Treasury facade is one of the most photographed and iconic architectural images globally. The Indiana Jones franchise had introduced Petra to millions of viewers in the 1989 film The Last Crusade, which used the Treasury as the exterior of the Holy Grail temple. The visual recognition factor mattered for popular voting.

Jordan's tourism mobilization. The Jordanian government and tourism authorities ran an aggressive vote-mobilization campaign, encouraging Jordanian citizens, diaspora communities, and international supporters to vote for Petra. The mobilization was real and probably contributed substantially to the vote totals.

The impact on Petra tourism

The post-2007 impact on Petra tourism was substantial and lasting:

Visitor numbers. Annual visitation rose from approximately 600,000 in 2006 to over 1 million by 2010, a 65-70% increase in four years. The Arab Spring disruptions of 2011-2012 temporarily reduced numbers, but the broader long-term lift has been sustained. By 2019 (pre-pandemic), Petra was hosting approximately 1.1 million annual visitors, with continued growth projected.

Infrastructure investment. The post-2007 visitor surge drove significant tourism infrastructure investment in Wadi Musa — new hotels, expanded restaurants, improved access roads, expanded Visitor Centre facilities. The town's tourism-based economy grew substantially. See Petra hotels for the current accommodation landscape.

International marketing. Petra's New Seven Wonders status became central to Jordan's international tourism marketing. The designation is featured in virtually every Jordan tourism promotional materials and is one of the primary draws for first-time Jordan travelers.

Economic significance. The post-2007 lift to Jordan's tourism economy is estimated in the hundreds of millions of dollars annually, with Petra accounting for a substantial portion of the country's tourism revenue. The site is one of Jordan's most economically valuable cultural assets.

Petra's broader heritage status

The 2007 New Seven Wonders designation is one of several international recognitions Petra holds:

  • UNESCO World Heritage Site (1985). The senior and most academically rigorous designation. UNESCO listed Petra in 1985 for its "outstanding universal value" as a cultural site. The UNESCO designation predates and outranks the New Seven Wonders for serious cultural-heritage purposes.
  • Petra Archaeological Park (1993). Jordanian national protection designation for the broader Petra archaeological area, including conservation regulations and visitor management framework.
  • Wadi Rum and Petra dual designations. Both major Jordan tourism sites are UNESCO listed (Wadi Rum was added in 2011), reinforcing the broader status of southern Jordan as a major heritage destination.

The New Seven Wonders designation is the most popularly recognized of these but not the most institutionally significant. For tourism marketing purposes, however, it's the title that resonates most with general audiences.

What it means for the visit

Practical implications for travelers visiting Petra today:

  • Crowds. The post-2007 visitor surge means Petra is busier than it was in earlier decades. The high-season crowds (March-May, September-November) are substantial; planning for early-morning arrivals and avoiding peak tour-group windows matters more than it did in the pre-Wonders era.
  • Infrastructure quality. The post-Wonders investment means Petra now has high-quality visitor infrastructure — comfortable hotels at all price points, the modern Visitor Centre and museum, well-maintained trails and signage, organized guide services. The visit is logistically easier than it was 20 years ago.
  • Marketing context. The "New Seven Wonders" status is central to how Petra is presented in guidebooks, tour-operator marketing, and broader tourism content. Travelers should understand that this is a popular designation rather than an academically authoritative one — Petra's significance is genuine regardless of the 2007 vote.

Frequently asked questions

When was Petra named a New Seven Wonder?
July 7, 2007 — the 07/07/07 date when the results of the New Seven Wonders of the World vote were announced at a ceremony in Lisbon. Petra was selected alongside the Great Wall of China, Christ the Redeemer (Brazil), Machu Picchu (Peru), Chichen Itza (Mexico), the Colosseum (Italy), and the Taj Mahal (India). The Great Pyramid of Giza was added as an honorary eighth wonder after the other classical-list site (the only original Seven Wonders still standing).
Who organized the vote?
The New 7 Wonders Foundation, a Swiss-based private organization founded by Bernard Weber, a filmmaker and entrepreneur. The vote was conducted via internet voting and phone calls between 2000 and 2007, with 21 finalists narrowed from an initial list of 200+ candidates. The total vote count is disputed but the foundation claimed approximately 100 million votes worldwide. The vote was a commercial undertaking with significant marketing dimensions, not an academic or governmental designation.
Was the vote controversial?
Yes, in several ways. (1) UNESCO publicly distanced itself from the project, noting that the methodology didn't meet academic or cultural-heritage standards. (2) The Egyptian government initially objected to the Great Pyramid being included as a candidate (the pyramid was already an original Seven Wonders site and Egypt felt it shouldn't compete with newer sites); the foundation responded by giving the pyramid honorary status. (3) The vote allowed unlimited voting per person (no IP restrictions, no identity verification), which raised questions about whether the results reflected genuine global opinion or just organized lobbying. (4) The geographic distribution of finalists skewed toward sites with strong national governments willing to invest in promotion.
What was the impact on Petra tourism?
Substantial. In the years immediately following the 2007 designation, Petra saw 30-50% increases in annual visitation, reaching over 1 million annual visitors by 2010 (up from around 600,000 in 2006). The Jordan tourism economy benefited significantly, with hotel construction, expanded tourism infrastructure, and broader international awareness. The 2010-2011 Arab Spring disruptions temporarily reduced visitor numbers, but the broader long-term lift from the New Seven Wonders designation has been sustained. The economic value to Jordan is estimated in the hundreds of millions of dollars annually.
How does the New Seven Wonders list compare to the original?
The original Seven Wonders of the World (compiled in classical Greek and Roman lists from the 2nd century BCE through the 6th century CE) was a Mediterranean-centered list: the Pyramid of Giza, the Hanging Gardens of Babylon, the Statue of Zeus at Olympia, the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus, the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, the Colossus of Rhodes, and the Lighthouse of Alexandria. Only the Great Pyramid still stands. The New Seven Wonders list is geographically more diverse and includes sites from the post-classical period — Petra being the only New Wonders site predating the year 1000.
