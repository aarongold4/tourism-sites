Free walking tours

Tbilisi's tip-based tours are better than the format's reputation — often led by the same guides as the paid versions, on the same lanes, with the price decided by you at the end.

The model is simple and honest once stated plainly: reservation free, departure scheduled, route professional, and the guide paid entirely by what the group tips at the end. Tbilisi's free-tour scene matured years ago — the established operators run daily English departures on the classic Liberty Square–Sololaki–Abanotubani–Narikala territory plus alternative themes (backstreets, Soviet legacy, street art), with guides who treat the format as a stage rather than a consolation prize. For solo travelers it doubles as a social mixer; for budget travelers it's the best value in the city; for everyone it's a low-risk way to calibrate whether you want more guiding or none.

The etiquette carries the system. Book the free reservation (departures cap), show up — no-shows quietly kill the model — and bring cash lari for the end. The fair benchmark: somewhere around what a couple of budget lunches cost per person for a good 2.5–3 hour tour, scaled up for excellence. The transaction is warm, not awkward; Georgian guides have made an art of the closing hat-pass.

Free or paid: the honest comparison

Choose free when budget leads, when you're solo and want company, or when you want a same-day departure without commitment. Choose a paid small group when you want a hard cap on group size, included extras, and recourse if things go sideways — and choose private when the economics of Georgian guiding make "your own historian for a morning" cost less than two marketplace seats elsewhere in Europe. Many travelers stack them: free tour on day one for orientation, then a food tour with the budget the first one saved.

The etiquette, in practice

The tip-based model only works if everyone plays their part, so treat the unwritten rules as the price of admission. Book the free reservation — departures cap, and walk-ups can be turned away — and actually show up, because no-shows quietly kill the format for the guides who depend on it. Bring cash in lari for the end; nothing deflates a great tour like fumbling “do you take cards?” at the closing hat-pass. The fair benchmark is roughly what a couple of budget lunches cost per person for a solid two-and-a-half to three-hour tour, scaled up when the guide is genuinely excellent — and they often are, since many of the same people lead the paid departures too.

Know what you're trading. Free tours skew larger and younger, run rain-or-shine on fixed schedules, and exclude the extras — the cable-car ticket, a tasting — that a paid tour folds in. That's the right deal for budget travelers, for solo visitors who want company on day one, and for anyone wanting a same-day option without commitment. If you'd rather a hard cap on group size and recourse if things go sideways, a paid small-group walk is the upgrade; if you want the route bent around your own pace, the economics of private guiding in Georgia make it a smaller leap than you'd expect. Many travelers simply stack them: a free tour to orient on day one, then a food tour with the budget the first one saved.

Frequently asked questions

Is a free tour actually free?

The price is whatever you tip, and tipping is the point: the guide’s income is the hat at the end. Pay-what-you-wish, not zero. Skip the tip only if the tour genuinely failed you — and if money is truly tight, guides would still rather have you along than not.

How much should I tip?

Calibrate to a budget meal or two per person for a solid 2.5–3 hour tour, more for an exceptional guide — the common local guidance lands around what 10–20% of a paid tour would cost. Bring lari in cash; ending a great tour with “do you take cards?” is the avoidable anticlimax.

Do I need to book?

Yes — the established operators take free reservations through their sites or platforms like GuruWalk, and peak-season departures cap out. Booking also gets you the meeting-point details and weather updates, which arrive (of course) by WhatsApp.

How do free tours differ from paid group tours?

Route and quality overlap heavily — many guides work both formats. The real differences: free tours skew larger and younger, run rain-or-shine on fixed schedules, and exclude extras like the cable car ticket. Paid small groups buy you a head-count cap and inclusions; free tours buy flexibility and a price you set.

Which operators run them?

A handful of established names run daily English departures — Tbilisi Free Walking Tours, Tbilisi Hack, and the GuruWalk roster among them — typically offering a classic Old Town route plus alternative themes (backstreets, Soviet legacy, hidden courtyards). Check recent reviews for the named guide; that’s where the quality lives.
