PLAN YOUR VISIT

Plan your Petra visit

Petra rewards planning more than most destinations — the difference between a great trip and a frustrating one is mostly decisions you make before you arrive. Here's the complete planning guide.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 13-minute read · See our editorial standards

Petra is unusual among major tourist destinations in how much your experience depends on a handful of decisions you make weeks before you arrive. The site is enormous — 264 square kilometers of canyon, cliffs, and tombs — but the practical access to it is funneled through a single visitor centre, a single set of opening hours, and one entrance ticket policy with a quirk that catches thousands of travelers off guard every year. Get the basics right and you'll have a great trip. Get them wrong and you'll spend money unnecessarily, miss the best light, or arrive at the gate unable to enter.

This page is the index to the planning decisions, organized roughly in the order you'll need to make them. The first set is about tickets and money — the Jordan Pass, the entrance fee structure, the visa-on-arrival quirk that affects day-trippers from Israel. The second is about timing — what month to visit, how many days you need, when the site is open. The third is about practical logistics — what to bring, what to wear, how to handle Petra with kids or with mobility constraints, and how to think about safety.

If you read nothing else, read the Jordan Pass page. It's the single decision that has the biggest impact on the cost of your trip, and the one most travelers don't fully understand until they're already at the airport.

Timing your visit

Petra is open year-round, but the experience is dramatically different in different seasons. The site sits at 850 meters of elevation in southern Jordan's mountainous desert, which produces wide daily temperature swings — 40–50°F between dawn and afternoon is normal — and a real summer-versus-winter contrast that catches visitors off guard either way. Spring (March–May) and autumn (September–November) are the consensus best times. Winter is genuinely cold and quiet. Summer is brutally hot and surprisingly empty.

The other timing question that matters is how long you stay. The honest answer for most visitors is two days. One day works only if you start at 06:00 and accept that you'll skip either the Monastery hike or the high-place trails. Three days is for serious hikers, photographers, or anyone who wants a leisurely visit. Spend less than a full day at Petra and you've missed the trip — there's no version of "just popping in for a few hours" that produces a satisfying experience.

The day-of timing matters too. The site officially opens at sunrise, but realistically you want to be inside the gate by 06:00 in summer or 06:30 in winter — the Treasury plaza is genuinely yours for about 90 minutes before the first tour buses arrive. By 11:00, the cruise-ship and Eilat day-trip crowds are at peak; by 14:00, the heat in summer is real and the light has flattened. The classic itinerary — do the Monastery in the morning while you have energy, save the Treasury for the late-afternoon walk back when the light is golden and the crowds have thinned — is the right one.

What to bring, what to wear

Petra is a walking trip. The site is six kilometers end-to-end, the trails add another three to ten kilometers depending on which you do, and the surface is a mix of hard-packed sand, uneven stone, and ancient steps that have been polished smooth by 2,000 years of foot traffic. The single most important piece of gear is a real pair of walking shoes — not sandals, not loafers, not the Birkenstocks half the cruise-ship crowd shows up in. Trail runners or light hiking shoes are ideal. Petra has retired more pairs of shoes than most national parks.

The second most important thing is sun protection. The canyons offer some shade in the morning, but the lower city and the trails are exposed, and the UV at 850m elevation in a desert is severe. A wide-brimmed hat (not a baseball cap), high-SPF sunscreen, and at least two liters of water per person are baseline. There's a single restaurant inside the site and a few Bedouin tea stalls along the trails, but you can't rely on them — bring your own water and snacks.

On clothing: Jordan is a moderate but conservative country, and Petra in particular is in a deeply traditional Bedouin region. The practical rule for women is "shoulders and knees covered" — light long-sleeve shirts and loose trousers or long skirts work well. Men have a wider berth but should still avoid tight or revealing clothing. Layers matter more than people expect, especially in winter and during shoulder seasons — the canyon at 06:00 in March can be 45°F and the Monastery viewpoint at 14:00 the same day can be 75°F.

Special considerations

Most of Petra's planning guidance assumes a fit adult traveling without kids. That's a reasonable default, but it skips three groups of travelers who have legitimate questions that don't get answered well anywhere else: families with young children, travelers with mobility constraints, and travelers visiting during Ramadan. The pages below cover each in detail.

The short version on each: Petra with kids is genuinely doable but exhausting, and most families are happiest with a half-day approach rather than trying to do the full site. Accessibility is mixed — the main path through the Siq and to the Treasury is doable in a wheelchair with help (and the site offers electric carts as a paid alternative), but anything beyond the lower city is steep stone steps that are inaccessible. Ramadan visits are quieter and have a different rhythm — daytime hours are shorter, restaurants are limited until iftar, and the pace of the country slows down in ways that some travelers find welcome and others find frustrating.

Frequently asked questions

What's the single most important thing to know before visiting Petra?
Buy a Jordan Pass before you arrive in Jordan. It bundles your visa-on-arrival fee (40 JOD), Petra entry (50–60 JOD depending on number of days), and free entry to over 40 other sites. The 80 JOD Jordan Explorer pass pays for itself the moment you land and visit Petra once. The catch: the visa waiver only works if you buy the pass before arrival and stay at least two nights in Jordan.
How many days do I need at Petra?
Two days is the right answer for most visitors. One day covers the headline monuments — the Siq, Treasury, Royal Tombs, Roman theatre, and the Monastery — but only if you start at 6 AM and don't stop walking. Two days lets you do at least one of the high-place trails without rushing and gives you a second pass at the Siq in different light. Three days is for hikers planning Jebel Haroun or photographers who want the Treasury at sunrise.
When does Petra open and close?
Petra is open daily from sunrise. The Visitor Centre operates roughly 06:00 to 17:30 in summer (April–October) and 06:00 to 16:00 in winter (November–March). You should be inside the gate by 06:00 if you want the Treasury before tour groups arrive. Last entry is typically an hour before closing, but the realistic last-entry time is much earlier — you cannot see anything meaningful in less than 4–5 hours, and the walk back from the lower city to the entrance alone takes 45 minutes.
Do I need to book Petra entrance tickets in advance?
No. Tickets are sold at the Visitor Centre on the day of your visit, in cash or card, and queues are rarely long. You can also pre-purchase online at petrapass.pdtra.gov.jo, but it doesn't give you any meaningful advantage — you still go through the same security check. The exception is if you have a Jordan Pass, which acts as your ticket and lets you skip the ticket booth entirely.
Is Petra safe for tourists?
Yes, by any reasonable measure. Jordan is one of the most stable countries in the region, and the tourism industry — particularly around Petra and Wadi Rum — operates under tight government oversight. The realistic risks are sunstroke, dehydration, and falls on the steep stone steps to the Monastery and high places, not crime or political instability. The US and UK travel advisories typically rate Jordan at the same level as most European countries; check the current guidance from your home government before any trip.
Do I need cash? Can I use credit cards?
Bring some Jordanian Dinars in cash. Hotels, tour operators, and the Petra ticket booth take cards, but the small purchases that make up most of a Petra day — Bedouin tea at trail viewpoints, donkey rides, snacks, tips — are cash-only. ATMs in Wadi Musa are reliable; we'd recommend withdrawing 50–100 JOD per person per day. Don't change money at the airport (terrible rates) and don't carry US dollars expecting to use them — most vendors quote in JOD and the conversion math will lose you money.
