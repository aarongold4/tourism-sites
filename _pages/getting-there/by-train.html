Getting There

By train

Georgia's headline rail service links the capital to the Black Sea in five civilized hours — and the overnight sleepers to Yerevan and Baku stitch the whole Caucasus together for the unhurried.

Last reviewed: June 7, 2026

The Tbilisi–Batumi day train is the country's flagship: a modern double-decker running the cross-country route in about five hours, center to center, with assigned seats, luggage racks that cope, and scenery that earns a window — river gorge giving way to the green west and finally the sea. Against the highway it wins on everything but raw schedule flexibility: no white-knuckle overtaking, no roadworks lottery, and you arrive in Batumi a person rather than a passenger. For the coastal extension most week-long trips consider, this train is the argument.

Booking is the one discipline: Georgian Railways sells online weeks ahead, tickets are nominal (passport details required), and summer weekends sell out for real. Buy when dates firm, pick the upper deck for views, and treat the station's metro connection — Station Square, two stops from the center — as part of the service's case.

Rails beyond Batumi

The international sleepers — overnight to Yerevan, overnight to Baku — are slower than any bus and better than every bus: compartments, border-crossing theater, tea, and the particular sociability of Caucasus rail. They suit loop itineraries and travelers who count the journey as content; they frustrate anyone optimizing hours. Domestically beyond Batumi, the network thins and the marshrutka and coach grid takes over — which is its own experience, covered across this cluster. Day-trippers note: the classic excursions (Kazbegi, Kakheti) are road routes — the train is for changing regions, not for day trips.

Routes, classes, and booking

Georgian Railway is a quietly excellent way to move around the country, and the flagship is the Tbilisi–Batumi line: a modern Stadler double-decker covering the roughly 380 km to the Black Sea in about five and a half hours, city center to city center, with first and second class that are both comfortable and cheap by any standard. Other useful runs include Zugdidi (the gateway for transfers up to Svaneti and Mestia), Kutaisi and Poti in the west, and the seasonal overnight services. International sleepers connect Tbilisi to Yerevan in Armenia and, at times, Baku in Azerbaijan, though schedules and the political openness of borders shift — verify currency before planning around them.

Book through the official Georgian Railway website or app, where seats for the popular Batumi departures genuinely sell out in summer — reserve a few days ahead in July and August rather than turning up at the window. Trains leave from the central Tbilisi Station at Station Square, on the metro and reachable by the airport bus, so connections are painless. First class buys more space and quieter carriages for a small premium worth taking on the long west-coast run. As a rule the train beats the marshrutka on comfort and the plane on city-center-to-city-center time for Batumi; for the coast and the western loop it's the obvious spine, tying Batumi and Kutaisi back to the capital.

Frequently asked questions

How long is the Tbilisi–Batumi train?

Around five hours city-center to city-center on the modern double-decker service — competitive with driving once you count the coast road’s traffic, and incomparably gentler. Assigned seating throughout.

How do I book?

Online through Georgian Railways (tkt.ge also sells), or at station counters. Booking opens weeks ahead; summer weekends and holidays genuinely sell out, so buy when your dates firm. You’ll need passport details per passenger — tickets are nominal.

Which class is worth it?

The classes differ modestly in pitch and price; the meaningful upgrades are seat position — upper deck for views — and traveling light enough to manage the racks. This is a comfortable day train in every class, not a luxury-or-misery split.

What about the night trains to Yerevan and Baku?

Both run — slow, sociable, sleeper-format crossings that suit Caucasus loop trips and rail romantics. They’re a different product from the Batumi day train: book compartments rather than seats, budget patience at the borders, and treat the journey as the destination. Where do trains leave from in Tbilisi?

Central Station (Station Square), directly on the metro — two stops and a tap from the center. Arrive with the usual half-hour margin; platform announcements are bilingual enough to manage.
