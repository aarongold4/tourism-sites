The Monastery (Ad-Deir)

Petra's largest monument sits at the top of 800 steps, half a day's walk from the Visitor Centre, and the climb is the single most rewarding effort in Petra. Bigger than the Treasury, less crowded, and arguably more impressive in person.

The honest answer up front: yes, the 800-step climb to the Monastery is worth it. The Monastery (Ad-Deir, الدير in Arabic) is the largest monument in Petra — 48 meters wide and 47 meters tall, dwarfing the more famous Treasury by a significant margin — and the fact that it sits at the top of an hour-long climb is the reason it remains one of Petra's quieter experiences even in high season. Most cruise-ship and day-trip visitors stop at the Treasury, photograph the Royal Tombs, and turn around. The travelers who push on to the Monastery describe Ad-Deir more often than the Treasury as the highlight of their Petra day.

The climb itself is part of the experience. The Nabataeans carved a processional path of 800 (or 850, depending on how you count) stone steps directly into the cliff face on the western edge of the city, leading up and around a series of sandstone outcrops to the broad terrace where the Monastery sits. The ascent takes 40 to 60 minutes at an unhurried pace, with rest stops at the Lion Triclinium and at several Bedouin tea stalls along the way. The descent is faster — 30 to 45 minutes — but the steps are uneven and polished smooth, and a good pair of walking shoes is non-negotiable.

What you're looking at

Despite the name, the Monastery isn't a monastery. The Arabic name comes from the Christian crosses carved into the interior walls during the Byzantine period, when the chamber appears to have been adapted for use as a Christian church or hermitage. The original Nabataean structure is significantly older — almost certainly built in the mid-1st century AD, contemporary with the late phase of Petra's monumental construction program. Most archaeologists now believe Ad-Deir was either a royal tomb (possibly dedicated to the deified King Obodas I) or a ceremonial gathering hall used for religious feasts and Nabataean cult practices.

The facade is carved in the same broad style as the Treasury — a two-storey arrangement of columns, pediments, and a central tholos with an urn on top — but rougher in execution, less ornate, and at a much larger scale. Where the Treasury's facade is intricate and Hellenistic, the Monastery is monumental and more austere. The niches between the columns once held figural sculptures, now lost. The interior, accessible only by looking in from the doorway, is a single large empty chamber roughly 12 meters square and 9 meters high.

The climb, step by step

The trail to the Monastery begins behind the Basin Restaurant, in the lower city about 3.5 km from the Petra Visitor Centre. From the Treasury, allow roughly 45 minutes to walk to the trailhead, through the Royal Tombs and the colonnaded street. The trail itself is well-marked and impossible to lose — it is the only path up the western cliff. The ascent is steady rather than steep, with switchbacks and occasional flat stretches where you can recover. Bedouin tea stalls at three points along the route sell water (2 JOD), tea (1–2 JOD), and snacks. They are also useful navigation markers if you want to break the climb into stages.

About two-thirds of the way up, the trail passes the Lion Triclinium, a smaller rock-cut chamber with two lion sculptures flanking its entrance. It's worth a five-minute detour. From there, another fifteen minutes of climbing brings you out onto a broad sandy terrace, and the Monastery emerges suddenly to your right — half-hidden by an outcrop until you're almost in front of it. That first sight is, for most visitors, the moment that justifies the climb.

Beyond the Monastery

A small Bedouin cafe sits across the terrace from the Monastery facade, with carpets laid out for sitting and a view that is, plausibly, the best in Petra. Take a tea (it's the right thing to do). The other thing worth doing is walking past the cafe along a small marked path to one of two viewpoints — "the End of the World" or "the Best Viewpoint" depending on which sign you read — about ten minutes further. From the cliff edge, you can see across the western desert toward Wadi Araba and, on a clear day, all the way to the Israeli Negev. The drop is unfenced and serious; mind your footing.

When to climb

The Monastery faces west. This is the single most important fact about timing your visit. In the morning, the facade is in deep shadow and the rose-pink color of the stone is muted. In the late afternoon, from roughly 14:00 to 17:00, the sun illuminates the facade directly and the carving glows. The classic Monastery photograph requires afternoon light.

The catch is the climb itself. In spring and autumn, the morning is much cooler and the climb is easier; the afternoon temperature in the canyon makes the same climb significantly harder. In summer (June through August), the morning climb is the only safe option — afternoon temperatures regularly reach the upper 30s Celsius (95°F+) and the climb in those conditions has been responsible for genuine medical emergencies. In winter (December through February), the afternoon is the better option both for light and for warmth, and the climb is comfortable at any time of day.

The compromise most travelers make on a two-day Petra visit is to climb the Monastery in the late afternoon of Day 1, accepting the warmer climb in exchange for better light, and to do the rest of the site (Royal Tombs, High Place of Sacrifice, Petra by Night) around it. The alternative — climb in the morning, do the rest in the afternoon — works fine but means accepting a darker Monastery in your photographs.

Frequently asked questions

How many steps to the Monastery?
Roughly 800, though counts vary between 800 and 850 depending on whether you include the smaller flights at the bottom and top. The path begins behind the Basin Restaurant in the lower city and climbs steadily up a Nabataean-engineered processional route. Plan for 40 to 60 minutes going up at an unhurried pace, 30 to 45 minutes coming down. The steps themselves are uneven — some are tall, some shallow, all polished smooth by 2,000 years of foot traffic.
Is the Monastery climb worth it?
For visitors with reasonable fitness, yes — it is the single most rewarding effort in Petra. The Monastery is larger than the Treasury (about 48 meters wide and 47 meters tall versus the Treasury's 40 meters), more remote, less crowded, and arguably more impressive in person. Many returning visitors describe Ad-Deir rather than the Treasury as the highlight of their trip. The view from the small viewpoint just past the Monastery, out across the western desert toward Wadi Araba, is one of the great panoramas in the region.
Should I take a donkey up?
We don't recommend it. The donkey rides up the Monastery trail are inexpensive (around 20 JOD return) but the welfare conditions for the animals are mixed, the descent in particular is genuinely uncomfortable for the rider, and the experience of climbing the Nabataean steps yourself is part of why the arrival at the Monastery is so powerful. If you genuinely cannot manage the climb, the donkey is available. If you can, walk it. The Bedouin men running the donkeys will offer aggressively — a polite "la, shukran" (no, thanks) is the standard response.
How long do I need at the Monastery?
Plan for 90 minutes to 2 hours total: 45–60 minutes climbing, 20–30 minutes at the Monastery itself (including walking out to the viewpoint past it), and 30–45 minutes descending. Most travelers stay longer than they expected — the cafe across from the facade serves tea with one of the world's great views, and the temptation to sit and just look at the carving for a while is real.
When is the best time to visit the Monastery?
The Monastery faces west, which means it receives direct sunlight in the late afternoon and is in shadow in the morning. For photography of the facade itself, late afternoon (roughly 14:00 to 17:00) is when the rose-pink stone glows. For climbing comfort — particularly in spring and autumn — the morning is much cooler and easier. The compromise most travelers make is to climb in the morning, accept a less photogenic facade, and come down before the heat builds. In summer (June through August), there is no good answer; the climb is genuinely dangerous past mid-morning.
Was it actually a monastery?
Not originally. The name comes from Christian crosses carved into the interior walls during the Byzantine period, when the chamber may have been used as a church. The building itself dates to the mid-1st century AD — Nabataean construction, almost certainly built as either a royal tomb (some scholars argue it was dedicated to King Obodas I, who reigned earlier and was deified) or as a ceremonial gathering hall for religious feasts. The interior is a single empty chamber. You can look in from the entrance but cannot enter.
