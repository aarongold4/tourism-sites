Money & Currency: The Denar Guide

A currency pegged for decades, cards in the cities, cash in the villages.

HomePlan Your TripMoney & Currency: The Denar Guide

Macedonian money is refreshingly drama-free: the denar has held its euro peg near 61.5 MKD = €1 since the 1990s, which means no exchange-rate roulette, easy mental math (knock off two zeros, multiply by 1.6) and prices that stay where guidebooks left them. The only skill to learn is the cash-card boundary.

Getting denars

ATMs are the default and they're everywhere in towns — bank-attached machines offer the fair pegged rate; the usual rules apply about declining "conversion to your home currency" offers on the screen (always choose to be charged in MKD). Exchange offices (menuvačnica) in city centers post competitive rates for euro cash with minimal spread; the airport kiosks are the predictable exception. There is no black-market angle worth anyone's time — the peg killed it decades ago.

The cash-card boundary

Think of it as a building-age rule: anywhere modern takes cards (hotels, supermarkets, city restaurants, fuel stations), anywhere older or smaller runs on cash (bazaar stalls, green markets, village tavernas, monastery candle counters, bus tickets, beach loungers, parking attendants, Matka boatmen). A practical float of 2,000–3,000 MKD (€32–48) in small notes covers a cash day comfortably; break big notes at supermarkets, since the 5,000-denar note embarrasses small vendors.

The euro shadow-economy

Accommodation, tours, taxis on long runs and anything aimed at foreigners will often be quoted in euros — a regional habit, not a scam. Paying those euro quotes in actual euro cash is normal and accepted; converting them to denars at the peg is equally fine. Just keep daily life in denars and you'll never overpay on a fuzzy rate.

Tipping and small print

Tipping is appreciated, modest and uncomplicated: round up or add ~10% in restaurants for good service, round taxi fares to the next note, a coin or two for bar staff, a few hundred denars for guides who earned it. No service charges hide on bills. Final notes: keep exchange receipts if you'll reconvert leftover denars at departure, and spend coins as you go — nobody outside the country wants them, charmingly including most souvenir budgets.

Frequently asked questions

What currency does North Macedonia use?

The Macedonian denar (MKD), pegged to the euro at roughly 61.5 MKD per euro for over two decades — so prices are stable and conversion is easy: 100 MKD ≈ €1.60, 1,000 MKD ≈ €16.

Can I pay with euros?

Hotels and tour operators often quote and accept euros; day-to-day businesses legally trade in denars and shops will usually decline euro cash or give poor implicit rates. Pay denars for daily life, euros only where they're quoted.

Are cards widely accepted?

In cities, hotels, supermarkets and most restaurants — yes, contactless included. Keep cash for the bazaar, markets, village tavernas, monasteries, buses, parking and small museum tills. ATMs are everywhere in towns.
