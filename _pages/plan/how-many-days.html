HOW LONG

How many days for Petra?

One day, two days, or three. The honest answer for most travelers is two — and the reasons are the Monastery climb, the changing light on the monuments, and the way a second day at any major site is qualitatively different from the first.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 8-minute read · See our editorial standards

The short answer: two days. One day is the compressed version that works only at full pace and skips real attractions. Three days is for hikers, photographers, or travelers who specifically want a leisurely visit with Little Petra and Petra by Night fitted in. For most people on most trips, two days is the version of Petra that justifies the international flight to get there.

The longer answer has to do with how Petra actually unfolds. The site is 264 square kilometers but the meaningful visitable area is roughly a 6-kilometer line from the Visitor Centre, through the Siq, past the Treasury and the Royal Tombs, down through the lower city, and up to the Monastery at the far end. Walking this line one-way takes about three hours without stops. Adding the Monastery climb adds another 2.5–3 hours round trip. Adding one of the high-place trails (Al-Khubtha or the High Place of Sacrifice) adds another 3–4 hours. A "full" Petra visit is more time than a single day can comfortably contain.

This page walks through what each duration actually buys you, with honest assessments of what you'll skip at each level.

One day

One day at Petra works as a curated rather than comprehensive visit. The realistic schedule: enter at 06:00 (set an alarm — this matters more than any other decision you'll make), walk the Siq to the Treasury in the cool morning light with the canyon mostly to yourself, continue through the Street of Facades and the Royal Tombs, visit the Roman theatre, and arrive at the foot of the Monastery climb by 10:30. Climb the 800 steps to the Monastery in 60 minutes, spend 45 minutes at the top, descend in 45 minutes. Late lunch at the Basin Restaurant. Walk back through the lower city in afternoon light, take a final look at the Treasury (now in shadow but empty of crowds), and exit by 16:00.

That schedule covers the headline monuments. What you skip: the Al-Khubtha trail to the Treasury viewpoint photograph, the High Place of Sacrifice, Little Petra, Petra by Night, the Royal Tombs interiors in their afternoon light, and any meaningful time at the Petra Archaeological Museum near the entrance. You also skip the experience of slowing down — one day at Petra at this pace is more transit than visit.

One day works best for: travelers on tight Jordan loops where Petra is one of four or five destinations, fit travelers who can manage the pace, and day-trippers from Amman who specifically want a "saw it once" visit rather than a deep one.

Two days

The recommended duration for most travelers. Two days lets you do the Monastery and at least one of the high-place trails, see the Royal Tombs in both morning and afternoon light, fit in Petra by Night without rushing, and have meaningful time at the lesser-known sites like the Garden Temple and the Roman theatre interior.

The standard two-day plan: Day 1 enters at 06:00, walks to the Treasury, does the Royal Tombs and the lower city in the morning, climbs to the Monastery in the early afternoon, and exits by 16:30. Petra by Night that evening if it's a Sunday through Thursday. Day 2 enters at 06:30, does the Al-Khubtha trail to the Treasury viewpoint OR the High Place of Sacrifice with the Wadi Farasa loop, lunch at the Basin Restaurant, afternoon at Little Petra or back at the Royal Tombs in better light, exit by 16:00.

The two-day visit benefits dramatically from a Wadi Musa hotel — both for the 50 JOD vs 90 JOD entry fee difference and for the practical reality of getting up early enough to be at the gate at 06:00. We cover hotels on the hotels pillar.

Three days

For specific kinds of travelers: serious hikers, photographers, and travelers who explicitly want a slower visit. Three days lets you add Jebel Haroun (a full-day strenuous hike to Petra's highest peak and the traditional burial site of Aaron), Wadi Farasa as a separate destination rather than a return route from the High Place, and the back route from Little Petra to the Monastery — all of which are excluded from the standard two-day plan.

Three days also gives you afternoon light at every major monument, which matters if photography is part of why you're there. The Royal Tombs in afternoon light, the Monastery facade glowing in the late afternoon, the Treasury at the dawn or sunset hour, and the colonnaded street in golden hour — all of these are afternoon experiences that a two-day visit can only sample.

Three days is overkill for most travelers and the wrong answer for tight Jordan loops. For the right traveler, it's the version of Petra that becomes unforgettable.

Less than one day: don't

Half-day Petra visits, "stop on the way through" Petra visits, and morning-only Petra visits do not produce a satisfying experience. The minimum useful Petra visit involves walking the full 1.2-kilometer Siq, seeing the Treasury, walking 3 kilometers further to the Monastery climb, doing some portion of the climb, and walking back. That is itself an 8–10 km walking day. Compressing it into a half day means skipping either the Monastery, the lower city, or both.

If you only have a half day in Jordan, our honest recommendation is to skip Petra and visit Wadi Rum or the Dead Sea instead — both of which are more rewarding on a short visit than a rushed Petra.

The Jordan Pass requirement

The Jordan Pass waives your 40 JOD visa fee if you stay in Jordan at least two nights. For travelers who would otherwise day-trip Petra from Eilat or do a one-night Petra visit from Amman, this is a quiet reason to extend to a two-night stay — the saved 40 JOD is roughly equivalent to a night in a budget Wadi Musa hotel, and a two-night Wadi Musa visit gives you the two days at Petra that we recommend anyway. The math compounds: the two-night Petra visit gets you the 50 JOD entrance fee (versus 90 JOD for non-accommodated visitors), the Jordan Pass discount, and a meaningfully better visit. Frequently asked questions

What's the minimum time I need at Petra?
One full day, from 06:00 entry to 16:30 exit, is the absolute minimum that produces a satisfying visit. Anything less — a half-day, a "stop for a few hours en route" — is genuinely not worth the trip. Petra is too large, the access too funneled (everyone walks through the same Siq), and the most rewarding monuments too far from the entrance for a half-day visit to work. If you can't commit to a full day, our honest advice is to skip Petra and visit Wadi Rum or the Dead Sea instead.
Is one day enough for Petra?
It depends on your fitness and your expectations. One day at full pace — 06:00 entry, no extended breaks, walking until 16:00 — gets you the Siq, the Treasury, the Royal Tombs, the Roman theatre, the colonnaded street, and either the Monastery climb OR the High Place of Sacrifice (not both). You will not have time for Petra by Night, the Al-Khubtha trail, Little Petra, or the back routes. For travelers on a tight schedule who accept a curated rather than comprehensive visit, one day works.
Why do you recommend two days?
Three reasons. First, two days lets you do the Monastery without rushing — the climb takes 2.5–3 hours round trip and seriously cuts into a one-day visit. Second, it lets you experience Petra in different light: morning shadow in the Siq, afternoon sun on the Royal Tombs, golden-hour walk back from the Monastery. Third, the second day at any major archaeological site is qualitatively different from the first — you stop trying to see everything and start actually noticing things. The two-day visit is the version of Petra most travelers remember.
Who should consider three days?
Serious hikers (Jebel Haroun, Wadi Farasa, the back route from Little Petra), photographers (the rose-pink light cycle at each major monument plus the seasonal wildflowers around Beidha), and history-focused travelers (the Byzantine churches and the museum get short-changed on shorter visits). Three days also gives you Petra by Night without compromising your daytime schedule. For most travelers, three days is overkill; for those who specifically need it, it is genuinely the right call.
Can I do Petra as a day trip from Eilat or Amman?
Yes, but with reservations. A day trip from Amman gives you about 5–6 hours on site if you take an organized tour, less if you self-drive. A day trip from Eilat involves the border crossing twice (allow half a day for each crossing) and gives you about 4 hours on site. Neither produces a great visit — you skip the Monastery, you arrive when the Treasury is at peak crowd, and you leave before the afternoon light. Day-trippers also pay the 90 JOD non-accommodated entry fee instead of the 50 JOD overnight fee. If you can possibly stay one night in Wadi Musa, do — both the experience and the math are dramatically better.
Does it matter which days of the week I visit?
Less than most guides suggest. Petra is open every day. Friday is technically the day of rest in Jordan, but Petra itself stays open and Wadi Musa's restaurants operate normally for tourists. Crowds are slightly higher on weekends (Saturday and Sunday in Jordan, since Friday is the rest day), and slightly lower on Tuesday and Wednesday. The bigger pattern is cruise ship arrivals in Aqaba — those ships disgorge day-trippers who arrive at the Treasury around 10:30. If you arrive at 06:00, the day of the week is irrelevant.
