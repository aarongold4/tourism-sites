Alexandria: the day trip, honestly

Two and a half hours north by train, Egypt changes civilizations — pharaohs give way to Ptolemies, the desert to the Mediterranean, koshary to grilled sea bass. A great day out, for the right trip. Here's the case, both ways.

Last reviewed June 6, 2026◆6-minute read

Photo: Radosław Botev · CC BY 3.0 pl · Wikimedia Commons

Alexandria is the trip extension this site recommends most cautiously and enjoys most: founded by Alexander, capital of the Ptolemies, home of the ancient world's Library and Lighthouse — and a city whose famous antiquity survives mostly as atmosphere, footprints, and one spectacular underground exception. As a day trip it's a genuine pleasure; as a priority it loses to Saqqara and Islamic Cairo on any short itinerary. Five-plus days with the core program done? Take the train.

Getting there: the train, and only really the train

Express trains run Cairo (Ramses station) to Alexandria in roughly 2.5-3 hours, frequently, at modest fares in comfortable air-conditioned classes — book the first morning departure and the day works. The station experience runs on the standard kit: tickets bookable online or at windows, small bills, and the platform-finding patience of any big terminus. Driving the Desert Road matches the time with none of the calm; day-tour packages from Cairo bundle a car and guide for the usual private-day rates and suit travelers who want zero logistics (the format math).

The day, shaped

Morning, the ancient layer: the catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa — a multi-level Greco-Roman necropolis where Egyptian gods wear Roman armor, the day's unmissable sight and the one place the buried city surfaces intact — then Pompey's Pillar and the Serapeum ruins nearby. Lunch: grilled fish on the eastern harbor, the city's true second monument. Afternoon, the successors: the Citadel of Qaitbay at the harbor's tip, a 15th-century fortress built from and upon the fallen Pharos Lighthouse, then the Bibliotheca Alexandrina — the striking modern reincarnation of the Library, its reading room worth the entry alone. The corniche stitches it together; ride-hailing works citywide. Train home by early evening, Cairo by nine.

Setting expectations

The honest brief: ancient Alexandria lies beneath the modern city and its harbor — visible to divers, teasing from museum cases — so travelers expecting Giza-grade monuments will find instead a faded-grand Mediterranean city with superb moments. That's the charm or the disappointment, depending entirely on the expectation you pack. Ticketing runs the national card-first system at modest prices; the seafood, the sea air, and the catacombs justify the fare regardless.

Frequently asked questions

How do you get from Cairo to Alexandria?

The train is the answer: roughly 2.5-3 hours from Ramses station on the express services, comfortable and cheap, with the first morning departures making the day trip work. Driving the Desert Road takes similar time door-to-door with none of the train's calm; buses are the budget fallback.

Is Alexandria worth a day trip from Cairo?

On a five-plus-day trip with the pyramid program complete, yes — it's a different Egypt entirely: Mediterranean, Greco-Roman, seafood-and-corniche. On shorter trips, no: it competes with Saqqara-Dahshur and Islamic Cairo and loses to both. It's the bonus track, not the album.

What are Alexandria's essential sights?

Four anchors fill the day: the catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa (the Greco-Egyptian underworld mashup), the Citadel of Qaitbay standing on the Lighthouse's footprint, the modern Bibliotheca Alexandrina, and Pompey's Pillar with the Serapeum. Plus the real fifth sight: seafood lunch on the harbor.

Can you see anything of the ancient Lighthouse and Library?

Footprints and successors: Qaitbay's fortress was built from and upon the collapsed Pharos, and the Bibliotheca consciously reincarnates the Library. The ancient city itself lies under the modern one and the harbor — some of it visible to divers — which is Alexandria's poignancy: the most famous ancient city with the least to show.
