HISTORY

The Roman annexation

In 106 CE, the Roman emperor Trajan formally incorporated the Nabataean kingdom into the empire as the province of Arabia Petraea. The change ended Nabataean political independence but left the population, religion, and architecture largely intact. Here's what happened and why it matters.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 7-minute read · See our editorial standards

The Roman annexation of the Nabataean kingdom in 106 CE is the most consequential single event in Petra's history — the moment after which Petra ceased to be an independent capital and became a provincial city in someone else's empire. But the change was administrative rather than catastrophic. Unlike the violent conquests of Carthage, Gaul, or Dacia, the annexation appears to have been largely peaceful, with the Nabataean elite negotiating integration rather than fighting it. The visible Petra mostly survived; the political reality on top of it changed dramatically.

This page covers what happened in 106 CE, why Rome wanted the territory, what changed and didn't at Petra in the post-annexation period, and how the event fits into the broader arc of Petra's history. The framing throughout: this is the pivot from Nabataean to Roman Petra, and understanding the transition helps make sense of the layered architecture visible today.

The setting

By the early 2nd century CE, the Nabataean kingdom was past its peak but still significant. The 250 years since the kingdom's effective consolidation under the Aretas dynasty had brought enormous wealth (the Incense Road revenues), monumental construction (the Treasury, Monastery, Royal Tombs, all complete by this point), and political sophistication (independent diplomacy with Rome and Parthia, professional military, large urban population). But the underlying dynamics had begun to shift:

The trade was changing. The Greco-Roman discovery of Indian Ocean monsoon patterns in the 1st century CE opened direct sea routes between Mediterranean ports and southern Arabia/India. The overland Incense Road was still significant but no longer the only or the cheapest option. The Nabataean monopoly was eroding.

The political environment was tightening. Roman expansion under successive emperors had brought direct imperial control to most of the Mediterranean basin and the Levant. The Nabataean kingdom was an isolated client kingdom in a region otherwise under direct Roman administration. The arrangement worked when Rome valued indirect control; under emperors who preferred direct control, it became anomalous.

Trajan's policy. The emperor Trajan (98-117 CE) pursued active territorial expansion — Dacia (modern Romania) was conquered in 101-106, Mesopotamia briefly annexed in 116, and various smaller territories incorporated throughout his reign. The Nabataean kingdom fit the pattern of territories ready for direct integration into the imperial structure.

The annexation itself

The Nabataean king Rabbel II (70-106 CE), the last independent ruler of the kingdom, died in 106 CE. The exact circumstances are unclear — some sources suggest natural death, others suggest violence or coercion, others don't comment on the manner. What's clear is that on Rabbel's death, the Roman government formally incorporated the kingdom rather than allowing the normal succession to proceed.

The historical record is sparse on the specific events of 106 CE. The Roman historian Cassius Dio mentions the annexation briefly; Plutarch and other contemporary sources are silent. The lack of detailed accounts probably reflects the relatively peaceful nature of the transition — there was no major military campaign to celebrate, no triumph to record, no dramatic confrontation that would generate the kind of historical attention that violent conquests received.

The likeliest reconstruction: the Nabataean elite negotiated peaceful integration in exchange for continued local administrative roles, with the Roman imperial structure assuming control of foreign relations, trade route administration, and the broader political framework. The local population's daily life probably changed very little in the immediate aftermath of the annexation.

The new Roman province was called Arabia Petraea (referring to Petra as the principal city, though the provincial capital eventually moved to Bostra in southern Syria for practical administrative reasons). The province extended across the former Nabataean territories from the Hauran in the north through Petra and into the Hejaz region in the south.

What changed at Petra

The post-annexation period brought significant changes to Petra, though most of them were subtle rather than dramatic:

The wealth shifted. The most important change. Petra's monumental construction had been funded by the Nabataean royal court's share of trade revenues. Under Roman administration, those revenues went to the Roman imperial treasury. The Petra elite lost the concentrated wealth that had funded the Aretas-era construction program. The result is visible in the architectural record: the monumental construction effectively stops with the annexation. No new major tomb facades, no new major temples, no new monumental projects on the scale of the earlier period.

Roman civic construction. While Nabataean-style monumental construction stopped, Roman-style civic construction continued. The colonnaded street was rebuilt or expanded in Roman fashion. The Roman theatre was expanded to its full 6,000-8,500 seat capacity. Roman administrative buildings were added. The result was a hybrid urban fabric where Nabataean tomb facades coexisted with Roman civic infrastructure.

Trade route administration. The trade through Petra continued — the city remained a significant regional center — but with Roman officials managing the routes, collecting tolls, and providing protection. The shift moved economic decision-making from local Nabataean institutions to provincial Roman ones.

Cultural absorption. Greek and Latin gradually displaced Nabataean Aramaic in official contexts (inscriptions, administrative documents) while Nabataean Aramaic persisted in popular use. Roman cultural forms (theatre, baths, civic religion) coexisted with continuing Nabataean practices. By the 3rd-4th centuries, the distinctly Nabataean cultural identity was merging into the broader provincial Roman culture, though the substrate remained recognizable.

Religious continuity. Notably, the Nabataean religion continued largely unchanged. Romans accommodated local religious traditions, and Dushara, Allat, and the broader Nabataean pantheon continued to receive worship for another 200 years. The shift to Christianity came later (4th-5th centuries) and was driven by broader Mediterranean changes rather than by anything specific to the annexation.

The Roman period at Petra

Petra remained a significant city throughout the Roman period (106-330 CE) and into the Byzantine period that followed. The population probably reached its absolute peak in the 2nd-3rd centuries CE, with estimates of 25,000-30,000 inhabitants — slightly higher than the Aretas-era peak, reflecting continued urban growth under Roman administration despite the loss of independent political status.

Key features of Roman-period Petra:

  • The Roman administrative center. Petra served as a major regional administrative city, with Roman officials based at the city for managing the southern portions of Arabia Petraea.
  • The expanded civic infrastructure. The Roman theatre expansion, the rebuilt colonnaded street, the Roman-style baths, and the broader civic architecture all date to this period.
  • The continued trade role. While the overall trade volume declined as sea routes expanded, Petra remained a significant trade entrepot through the 3rd century CE.
  • The military presence. Roman troops were stationed in the region throughout the period, providing security for trade routes and projecting imperial power.

The Roman period at Petra effectively ended with the political and economic disruptions of the late 3rd century CE — the crisis years of the empire, the rising importance of the eastern provinces under Diocletian, and the broader shifts that led to the Byzantine period. By the time of the 363 CE earthquake, Petra had already begun its long decline, though it remained substantially populated for several more centuries.

What survived

The Roman annexation preserved more than it destroyed. The visible Petra today is largely a Nabataean creation, with Roman additions layered on top:

Nabataean and pre-annexation: The Treasury, the Monastery, the major Royal Tombs, the rock-cut tombs throughout the site, the original water management infrastructure, Qasr al-Bint, the Temple of the Winged Lions, the High Place of Sacrifice.

Roman additions: The Roman theatre expansion (the upper rows that cut through earlier tombs), the rebuilt colonnaded street, the Roman-style civic buildings of the lower city, the post-annexation modifications to existing structures.

The fact that the Nabataean monuments survive at all reflects the peaceful nature of the annexation. Violent conquests typically result in deliberate destruction of the previous regime's monuments; the Roman administrators at Petra preserved the older monuments and added their own contributions alongside, producing the layered architectural fabric still visible today.

The Wadi Mujib Inscription

One of the more direct pieces of evidence for the annexation comes from an inscription found at the Wadi Mujib gorge crossing on the King's Highway, north of Petra. The inscription, dating to the early 2nd century CE, marks the completion of road improvements under Roman administration — "from Aila to Bostra," tracing the route from the Gulf of Aqaba (Aila) through Petra to the new provincial capital at Bostra. The road project is one of the more visible signs of Roman administrative integration of the former Nabataean territories.

The Via Nova Traiana (Trajan's New Road) was the major Roman highway through Arabia Petraea, running from the Gulf of Aqaba up through Petra to Bostra and beyond. Sections of the road remain visible at multiple sites, including stretches along the King's Highway and at various Roman-era milestone markers.

Frequently asked questions

When did Rome annex the Nabataean kingdom?
106 CE, during the reign of Emperor Trajan. The annexation was relatively peaceful — the last Nabataean king Rabbel II died in 106, and the Roman government formally incorporated the kingdom rather than waiting for the succession process. The new Roman province was called Arabia Petraea, with its capital eventually moving to Bostra in southern Syria, though Petra remained an important regional center for centuries afterward.
Was it a violent conquest?
Not significantly. Unlike the violent conquests of Carthage, Gaul, or Dacia, the annexation of the Nabataean kingdom appears to have been largely administrative. The historical sources don't describe any major military campaign, and the archaeological evidence at Petra doesn't show widespread destruction from this period. The likeliest explanation is that the Nabataean elite negotiated peaceful integration in exchange for continued autonomy in local administration, with the Roman imperial structure assuming control of trade routes and external relations. The transition probably took place over months rather than years.
What changed for Petra after annexation?
Several things. (1) The wealth that had funded monumental construction shifted from the Nabataean royal court to the Roman imperial treasury — Petra's elite no longer had the resources for major new tomb and temple construction. (2) The trade routes continued but under Roman administration, with revenues going to Rome rather than to local rulers. (3) Roman-style civic infrastructure was added — the colonnaded street was rebuilt or expanded in Roman fashion, and Roman administrative buildings were constructed. (4) The political role of Petra shifted from independent capital to provincial city.
Why did Rome want to annex it?
Trade route control, primarily. The Nabataean kingdom controlled the chokepoint between Arabia and the Mediterranean — the toll revenues and the broader economic importance made it valuable to Rome. There was probably also a strategic dimension: integrating the Nabataean territories closed a potential vulnerability on the eastern frontier and brought the entire Levant under direct Roman administration rather than relying on client-king arrangements. Trajan's broader policy was active expansion of imperial control; the Nabataean annexation fit that pattern.
Did the Nabataean culture survive?
Yes, in modified form. The population of Petra and the broader Nabataean region didn't change after annexation — the same people, speaking the same language, practicing the same religion, continued living in the same places. What changed was the political structure on top of this continuity. Nabataean religious practice continued for two centuries under Roman rule. The Nabataean Aramaic script gradually gave way to Greek and Latin in official contexts but persisted in popular use. By the Byzantine period, the distinctively Nabataean cultural identity had merged into the broader regional culture, but the cultural substrate remained recognizable.
