GETTING THERE

Renting a car

The most flexible way to do a Jordan loop. $25-45 USD per day for an economy car, well-maintained roads, English signage. Here's where to rent, what it costs, and the practical realities of driving in Jordan.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 7-minute read · See our editorial standards

Renting a car is the right choice for most Jordan travelers. The combination of flexibility (leaving Petra when you want, stopping at the King's Highway castles, reaching Wadi Rum on your own schedule), reasonable pricing ($25-45 USD per day for an economy car), good road infrastructure (the major highways are well-maintained and well-signed in English), and the relatively short distances involved (Amman to Petra is 235 km, Petra to Wadi Rum 100 km) make a rental car a strong choice for a 4+ day trip.

This page covers the practical version: where to rent, what to expect from pricing and insurance, the driving conditions on Jordanian highways, and the few situations where a rental isn't the right call. The framing throughout: less complicated than first-time travelers fear, but with a few specific considerations worth knowing in advance.

Where to rent

Queen Alia International Airport (AMM)

The default option for most travelers. All major international rental companies have counters in the arrivals hall: Hertz, Avis, Budget, Sixt, Europcar, Enterprise, and several local Jordanian companies (Monte Carlo, Royal Tours, Reliable). Pickup is straightforward — show your passport, license, and credit card; the agent runs through paperwork; you're driving within 30-45 minutes of leaving the arrivals hall.

Airport pickup adds about 10-15% to the rental cost compared to in-town offices, but the convenience is significant if you're heading directly to Petra or other destinations rather than staying in Amman first.

Central Amman

Multiple rental offices in Jabal Amman, Abdoun, Shmeisani, and downtown. Lower rates than airport pickup but requires taking a taxi from your hotel to the rental office. Best for travelers who arrive in Amman, spend a day or two there, and then pick up a car for the southern portions of the trip.

Aqaba King Hussein International Airport (AQJ)

For travelers flying into the southern airport (mainly European routes). Fewer rental options than Queen Alia but the major companies (Hertz, Budget, Sixt, plus local companies) maintain counters here. Pricing similar to Queen Alia.

One-way rentals

Possible but with significant restrictions. Most companies don't allow drop-off at a different location than pickup; the ones that do (typically Hertz, Avis, Budget) charge one-way fees of $50-150 USD depending on the locations. For travelers wanting to fly into AMM and out of AQJ (or vice versa), the one-way fee can be worth it for the time saved.

What it costs

Approximate 2026 pricing for tourist-class rentals:

  • Economy (Hyundai Accent, Kia Picanto, similar): $25-40 USD per day. Adequate for two travelers with carry-on luggage; tight for larger groups or extensive luggage.
  • Compact and mid-range (Hyundai Elantra, Toyota Corolla): $35-55 USD per day. Comfortable for two with full luggage or three travelers.
  • SUV and 4WD (Hyundai Tucson, Mitsubishi Pajero): $60-100 USD per day. Useful for larger groups, longer trips, or travelers planning extensive off-tarmac exploration (the standard rental 4WDs are not for serious off-roading, but they handle gravel roads and minor rough sections better than sedans).

Fuel: 95-octane petrol runs about 0.95 JOD per liter ($5.20 USD per gallon equivalent). A typical Jordan loop uses roughly 80-120 liters total (Amman → Petra → Wadi Rum → Dead Sea → Amman is about 800 km round trip with stops), totaling $60-90 in fuel.

Insurance: Basic CDW typically included in the daily rate. Super CDW or zero-deductible cover adds $8-15 per day. Most travelers should take the upgrade for peace of mind.

Total for 7 days: A typical mid-range rental with full insurance for a Jordan week runs $300-450 plus fuel — significantly cheaper than hiring a driver ($600-1,000+) or relying entirely on tours.

The driving conditions

Highways (Desert Highway, Route 15)

Modern divided highway, well-maintained, well-signed in English and Arabic. Speed limits 90-110 km/h depending on section. Heavy truck traffic in places but generally easy driving. The Desert Highway is the fastest route between Amman and the south; allow about 3 hours Amman to Petra.

The King's Highway (Route 35)

Two-lane mountain road with switchbacks, dramatic terrain, and elevation changes. Well-maintained pavement but the Wadi Mujib crossing involves 25-30 minutes of switchback descent followed by 25-30 minutes of switchback ascent. Drive conservatively, use lower gears on steep descents, don't pass on blind curves. See the King's Highway page for the full detail on this route.

Amman city driving

The most challenging part. Heavy traffic, steep hills, assertive merging, occasional unmarked intersections, drivers who use horn-based signaling more than blinkers. First-timers find it intimidating for the first 30-60 minutes; most travelers acclimate within a day. Strategies that help: avoid rush hour (07:30-09:30 and 17:00-19:00), use the GPS in your phone (Google Maps works well in Amman), park at your hotel rather than circling for street parking, take taxis within the city rather than driving short distances.

Petra (Wadi Musa) driving

Easy. Wadi Musa is a small town with two-lane streets and modest traffic. Parking at hotels is generally free; parking at the Petra Visitor Centre is 1-2 JOD per day. Most of Petra is non-drivable (it's the archaeological site); the car stays parked while you're inside.

Wadi Rum approach

The drive from Petra to Wadi Rum is on the Desert Highway plus a paved access road to the Wadi Rum Visitor Centre. Straightforward, well-signed. Once you arrive at the Visitor Centre, you leave the rental car in the secure parking lot (free for guests of the Bedouin camps) and transfer to a Bedouin 4WD for the trip into the protected area.

Practical tips

License requirements

Your home country driver's license is accepted for the first 30 days. An International Driving Permit (IDP) is technically required by Jordanian law but is rarely enforced for short-term tourist rentals. Most rental companies accept home country licenses without an IDP. For trips longer than 30 days or for travelers wanting extra protection in the (unlikely) event of a roadside issue, get an IDP from your home automobile association for $20-30 before traveling.

Insurance options

Always take Collision Damage Waiver (CDW). Mandatory third-party liability is typically included in the basic rate. The upgrade options:

  • Super CDW / Zero deductible: Removes the ~$500-1,500 deductible on basic CDW. Adds $8-15 per day. Worth it for most travelers.
  • Personal accident insurance: Covers driver and passengers for medical costs. Adds $3-8 per day. Likely duplicated by travel insurance if you have it.
  • Theft protection: Often included in the basic CDW. Check the specific terms.

Many major credit cards include rental car insurance as a cardholder benefit (Chase Sapphire Preferred and Reserve, Amex Platinum, Capital One Venture, others). Check before traveling and bring documentation if you plan to rely on credit card coverage instead of buying the rental company's upgrade.

Fuel

95-octane petrol is the standard for rental cars; 92-octane is also available (slightly cheaper but specifically not recommended by rental companies). Diesel is available but uncommon in passenger rentals. Most service stations are full-service (the attendant pumps the fuel) but a few are self-service in Amman.

Pay in cash or by credit card. Most stations accept both. The fuel quality is consistent and reliable; you don't need to worry about contamination or grade variations.

Parking

Most hotels provide free parking for guests. Street parking in Amman is sometimes paid (street meters or municipal lots); in Petra, Wadi Rum, and smaller towns, parking is free. Park in lit, visible locations overnight. Don't leave valuables visible in the car.

Returning the car

Return with the same fuel level you picked up at (or pay the rental company's fuel premium). Allow extra time for return processing at the airport — typically 20-30 minutes including paperwork and damage inspection. Document any damage you notice with phone photos before handing over the keys.

When NOT to rent

Three traveler profiles where a rental isn't the right call:

  • Amman-only trips. If you're not leaving the capital, taxis and Uber/Careem cover all your needs more cheaply and less stressfully than a rental.
  • Budget-focused trips. The JETT bus connects Amman → Petra → Wadi Rum → Aqaba on a fixed schedule for roughly $30-40 USD round trip — dramatically cheaper than rental + fuel. Less flexible but workable for travelers on tight budgets.
  • First-time Middle East travelers nervous about driving. Hiring a private driver (60-100 JOD per day) handles the driving for you while preserving most of the flexibility benefits. Worth the premium for travelers who'd rather not deal with the learning curve.

Frequently asked questions

Should I rent a car in Jordan?
For most travelers planning a Jordan loop covering Petra, Wadi Rum, and other destinations, yes. Rental cars cost roughly $25-45 USD per day for an economy vehicle, the roads are well-maintained and well-signed in English, and the flexibility is significantly better than relying on JETT bus schedules or hired drivers. Exceptions: travelers staying only in Amman (use taxis and rideshare), travelers on tight budgets willing to work with JETT timing, and travelers nervous about driving in unfamiliar countries.
Where do I rent?
Three main options. (1) Queen Alia International Airport — all major international rental companies (Hertz, Avis, Budget, Sixt, Europcar) plus several Jordanian companies have airport counters. (2) Central Amman — multiple offices in Jabal Amman, Abdoun, and downtown. (3) Aqaba King Hussein Airport (AQJ) — for travelers flying into the southern airport. Airport pickups are most convenient; in-town offices are slightly cheaper but require taxi to reach.
What does it cost?
Economy cars run $25-40 USD per day, mid-range $40-60, SUVs $60-100. A typical 7-day rental for a couple totals $200-350 including basic insurance. Add roughly 50% on top for fuel, depending on how much you drive — a Jordan loop covering Amman, Petra, Wadi Rum, and the Dead Sea uses about $60-90 in fuel. International rates from major companies are sometimes higher; local Jordanian companies (Monte Carlo, Reliable, Royal Tours) typically come in 20-30% cheaper with similar service quality.
Is the driving difficult?
Highways are easy. Amman city driving is intimidating until you get used to it. The Desert Highway and King's Highway are well-maintained, well-signed, and easy to navigate. Amman itself has heavy traffic, assertive merging, and steep hills — first-timers find it stressful for the first few hours. Most travelers handle the overall trip fine with normal defensive driving habits. Park outside Amman center where possible, take taxis within the city, and use the rental for inter-city travel.
What about insurance?
Always take the basic Collision Damage Waiver (CDW) from the rental company. Many major credit cards include rental car insurance as a cardholder benefit — check before traveling and bring documentation. Third-party liability insurance is mandatory in Jordan and typically included in the basic rate. For extra peace of mind, top-up insurance (Super CDW or zero-deductible cover) typically adds $8-15 USD per day. The standard Jordan rental insurance package handles most situations.
Driving license requirements?
Your home country license works for the first 30 days. An International Driving Permit (IDP) is technically required by Jordanian law but is rarely enforced for short-term tourist rentals — most rental companies accept home country licenses without an IDP. For trips longer than 30 days, an IDP becomes essential. Get one from your home automobile association (AAA in the US, etc.) for $20-30 before traveling.
