HISTORY

The pyramids after the pharaohs

The Old Kingdom ended; the pyramids' story didn't. Scribes scribbled on them, Herodotus misreported them, Cairo was partly built from them, and a sultan tried to demolish one. The afterlife is half the biography.

Last reviewed June 6, 2026◆7-minute read

Photo: V Manninen from Funland · CC BY 2.0 · Wikimedia Commons

Most monuments have a history; the pyramids have an afterlife — four and a half millennia of being visited, robbed, quarried, mythologized, and admired by every civilization that passed through Egypt. The marks of that afterlife are all over the plateau once you know to look, and they explain almost everything about the monuments' present appearance that construction doesn't.

Antiquity's own tourists

The pyramids were ancient to the ancients: New Kingdom scribes, visiting a thousand years after Khufu, left graffiti praising the monuments of their forefathers — heritage tourism, attested in ink. Ramses II's son Khaemwaset restored Old Kingdom monuments and labeled his work, history's first conservator. Then came the Greeks: Herodotus's fifth-century BC visit produced the first surviving guidebook account — vivid, influential, and wrong in the durable ways (the hundred thousand slaves, the checked-out priests' tales) that the workforce page spends its length correcting. Under the Ptolemies and Romans, Giza ran as a full attraction — the Sphinx periodically dug out for emperors, Memphis selling the ancient world's souvenirs.

The quarry centuries

The monuments' greatest losses were economic, not dramatic. After earthquakes (the great 14th-century shocks above all) loosened the casings, medieval Cairo treated the pyramids as the world's best-stocked quarry: the gleaming Tura limestone went, cartload by cartload, into the mosques, walls, and bridges of the Islamic city — meaning a walk through historic Cairo is partly a walk through recycled pyramid. Earlier, the 9th-century tunnel attributed to Caliph al-Ma'mun forced the Great Pyramid's modern entrance (the passage you still use), finding chambers antiquity's robbers had long emptied. And in the 12th century a sultan's full demolition campaign against Menkaure's pyramid spent months to achieve one gash — the failed vandalism that doubles as the construction's finest review.

Legends, science, and the long return

The afterlife also grew the mythology: medieval traditions cast the pyramids as Joseph's granaries or antediluvian treasure vaults, Renaissance and Enlightenment travelers measured and speculated, and the nineteenth century's mystical pyramidology built numerological castles that Petrie's tape measure demolished. Every era, in short, saw its own reflection in the stones — which is the page's closing instruction for your visit: the missing casings, the forced tunnel, the gash, the graffiti, the scaffolding are not damage to regret past the photos. They are the biography, legible on the surface, of the only buildings on Earth old enough to have outlived every civilization that ever admired them — so far.

The afterlife's newest layer

The story's current paragraph is being written in the same materials as ever — attention and stone. The attention layer is digital now: the pyramids as backdrop for concerts and brand spectacles, as fuel for streaming mystery content, as the planet's most photographed antiquity; the stone layer is custodial, with conservation as the era's signature intervention rather than quarrying or tunneling. Future historians of the monuments will read our layer as legibly as we read al-Ma'mun's tunnel: the floodlights, the visitor center, the scan instrumentation, the careful mortar. The afterlife continues, in other words, with us in it — which is the page's real point: a visit is not observing the pyramids' history but joining it, briefly, as antiquity's latest guests.

The afterlife even has a soundtrack of departures: obelisks and artifacts that left in earlier centuries stand in Paris, London, and New York, the Sphinx's beard fragment sits in the British Museum, and the repatriation conversations of the current era are the afterlife's newest negotiation. Giza's diaspora means the monuments' story is now globally distributed — and a visitor's hometown museum is, more often than not, holding a chapter of it.

Reading suggestion to close: travelers who enjoyed this chapter should hunt the historic travelers' accounts — Herodotus's pyramid passages, the medieval Arab historians on the monuments and al-Ma'mun, the Description de l'Égypte's plates, the Victorian climbing accounts — most freely available online, all best read after standing on the plateau, when every exaggeration and error becomes affectionately checkable against your own photographs.

Frequently asked questions

Did ancient people visit the pyramids as tourists?

Constantly: New Kingdom scribes left admiring graffiti at Saqqara and Giza a thousand years after construction, Greeks and Romans made the pyramids a fixture of ancient sightseeing, and Herodotus's 5th-century BC visit produced the guidebook errors (the slave story among them) we are still correcting.

What happened to the pyramids' casing stones?

Mostly medieval Cairo: after earthquakes loosened the casings, the fine Tura limestone was quarried for the city's construction across centuries — mosques and walls of Islamic Cairo carry pyramid stone. Khafre kept his summit casing; the Bent Pyramid at Dahshur kept the most.

Did anyone try to demolish a pyramid?

Yes — in the 12th century a sultan's campaign attacked Menkaure's pyramid for months and managed only the gash still visible on its north face. The effort's failure is the era's best testimony to the buildings' construction.

When were the pyramids' chambers first re-entered?

In the early Islamic period, tradition credits Caliph al-Ma'mun's 9th-century tunnel into the Great Pyramid — the forced passage visitors still use. The chambers were almost certainly emptied much earlier, in antiquity's own waves of robbery.
