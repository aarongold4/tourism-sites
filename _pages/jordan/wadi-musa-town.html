WADI MUSA

Wadi Musa — the town outside Petra

Where Petra visitors actually stay. The town's name means Valley of Moses, the population is 25,000, and the whole place is built around tourism. Here's the practical layout, the hotel zones, where to eat, and what to do when you're not at Petra.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 6-minute read · See our editorial standards

Wadi Musa is the town where Petra visitors actually stay. The name means "Valley of Moses" in Arabic, after the local tradition that this is the site where Moses struck the rock to bring forth water during the Exodus (Numbers 20:8-11). The town has been a small Bedouin settlement for centuries, with the modern tourism-focused buildup developing rapidly over the past 60 years — particularly since UNESCO World Heritage designation in 1985 and the relocation of the Bdoul Bedouin from the Petra caves to the nearby village of Umm Sayhoun the same year.

The town today has a population of about 25,000 and is essentially a service town for Petra tourism: hotels, restaurants, taxi services, souvenir shops, and the supporting infrastructure (banks, pharmacies, grocery shops, cooking schools) that makes a tourism economy function. There are no ancient ruins in Wadi Musa itself — all the famous Nabataean monuments are inside the protected Petra Archaeological Park, which begins immediately west of the town center.

The practical layout

Wadi Musa is built along a single main road that climbs from the Petra Visitor Centre uphill to the east. The road passes through several roundabouts as orientation points and is generally called "Tourism Street" through the central section. The town stretches roughly 3 km along this axis, from the Visitor Centre at the bottom (1,050m elevation) to the upper town areas (1,200m+).

Three rough zones:

The Visitor Centre area (bottom of the hill). Petra entrance, the luxury hotels (Mövenpick, Marriott), the Petra Museum, and a small commercial strip. This is where you'll arrive if you're coming by JETT bus (the bus stops near the Visitor Centre). Walking distance to the Petra gate: 0-200 meters. This is the most convenient location for early-morning entry but also the most expensive.

The Tourism Street / mid-town zone. 500m to 1.5km uphill from the Visitor Centre. Most mid-range hotels, the densest restaurant cluster, a few souvenir shops, and the main commercial life of the town. Walking distance to the Petra gate: 10-20 minutes downhill in the morning, longer back up to your hotel in the evening (you'll often take a taxi for this, around 2-3 JOD).

The upper town (the actual town center). 1.5km+ from the Visitor Centre. Cheapest hotels and hostels, the daily grocery shops, banks and ATMs, the regular town life of Wadi Musa beyond tourism. Walking to the Petra gate from here is impractical — taxis only, typically 4-5 JOD one-way.

The King's Highway (Route 35) bypasses the town to the east, connecting north toward Amman (235 km, 3 hours) and south toward Aqaba (130 km, 2 hours) and Wadi Rum (120 km).

Hotel zones

We cover specific hotel recommendations on the hotels pillar page, but the zone-by-zone summary:

Visitor Centre cluster (luxury). Mövenpick Resort Petra ($180-260/night), Petra Marriott Hotel ($200-320/night), Petra Guest House ($110-160/night). All within 5 minutes' walk of the gate.

Tourism Street (mid-range). Petra Moon Hotel, Sella Hotel, Petra Palace Hotel, Tetra Tree Hotel — all in the $50-120/night range. The largest concentration of mid-range options.

Upper Wadi Musa (budget). Valentine Inn, Cleopetra Hotel, Rocky Mountain Hotel — typically $25-50/night. Most offer free shuttles to the Visitor Centre in the morning.

Restaurants

The Tourism Street section has the densest restaurant cluster. A selection of well-regarded options:

My Mom's Recipe. Popular home-style Jordanian cooking, family-run. Mid-range pricing (10-15 JOD per person), traditional dishes done well. Sometimes a queue at dinner; arrive early.

Al-Wadi Restaurant. Larger mid-range establishment, good mansaf (Jordan's national dish — lamb in fermented yogurt sauce on rice). 12-18 JOD per person. Bus tour groups eat here, which can affect ambiance but the food remains solid.

The Three Steps. Rooftop restaurant with views over the lower town. International and Jordanian menu, 15-22 JOD per person. Worth the higher price for the setting.

Petra Kitchen. Cooking-class restaurant — you participate in preparing your own dinner with a local chef, then eat what you've made. Around 40 JOD per person including the class. A genuine highlight if you're interested in Jordanian cuisine.

The casual lunch spots. Several falafel and shawarma shops along Tourism Street offer cheap lunches (3-5 JOD for a substantial sandwich and drink). The Mövenpick and Marriott both have higher-end fine-dining restaurants for travelers who want to stay in.

Things to do in town beyond Petra

Not much, and that's a feature rather than a bug — Wadi Musa is a base for Petra, not a destination in its own right. A few specifics:

Moses's Spring (Ain Musa). A small mosque on the main road through Wadi Musa, with the spring inside identified by local tradition as the one Moses struck with his staff. Free entry; you can drink from the spring if you wish (most travelers don't). 15 minutes is enough.

Petra Archaeological Museum. At the Visitor Centre. Small but well-curated, with carved capitals, ceramics, jewelry, and statuary recovered from the Petra excavations. Open until 17:00; included with your Petra entrance ticket. Worth 30-45 minutes at the end of a Petra day.

Cooking class at Petra Kitchen. Hands-on cooking class plus dinner; around 40 JOD per person. Genuinely interesting and well-regarded. Book a day or two ahead.

Hammam (Turkish bath). Two main options in town — the Petra Turkish Bath near the Visitor Centre and the Salome Turkish Bath in upper Wadi Musa. Both offer steam, scrub, and massage packages for 20-30 JOD per person. A good post-Petra recovery option.

Hotel pools and rooftops. Most mid-range and luxury hotels have pools that are perfect for an afternoon decompression after a Petra day. The Mövenpick pool is the standard "luxury swim with mountain views" option.

Practical infrastructure

ATMs. Several reliable ATMs in the upper town and a few on Tourism Street. The Petra Visitor Centre ATM is reliable but limit your daily withdrawal to what you'll spend at the site.

Pharmacies. Several along Tourism Street and in the upper town. Reasonable selection of common medications without prescription. Pharmacy hours typically 09:00-22:00, with at least one open later for emergencies.

Grocery shops. Small daily-use shops along Tourism Street stock water, snacks, batteries, basic toiletries. Larger grocery shops in the upper town for more substantial purchases.

Taxis. Local taxis are abundant; flag from the roadside or arrange through your hotel. Standard rates: 2-3 JOD within town, 4-5 JOD between zones, 60-80 JOD to Aqaba airport, 70-100 JOD to Amman.

Internet. Wi-Fi is standard in all hotels. Cellular coverage is good across town. SIM cards available from Zain, Orange, or Umniah at small shops on Tourism Street; tourist data SIMs typically run 10-15 JOD for a week of generous data.

The cultural context

Wadi Musa is conservative by Jordanian standards — significantly more religious and traditional than Amman. The town shuts down somewhat during Friday afternoon prayers and during Ramadan daylight hours. Dress codes in town are stricter than at Petra itself: modest dress is expected, particularly for women, and short shorts or tank tops for either gender will attract attention or comments. The major hotels are exceptions where international tourism norms apply.

Alcohol is available at the major international hotels (Mövenpick, Marriott) but rarely in town-center restaurants. Drinking in public outside hotels is not done. Smoking is widespread.

The local population is mostly Sunni Muslim with a small Christian minority. English is widely spoken in tourism contexts; less so in the upper town. Most tourism workers speak conversational English; many speak some German or French as well due to the tourism market.

Frequently asked questions

What is Wadi Musa?
Wadi Musa is the town immediately outside the Petra Archaeological Park — the place where almost all Petra visitors actually stay. The name means "Valley of Moses" in Arabic, referring to the local tradition that this is where Moses struck the rock to bring forth water for the Israelites (Numbers 20:11). The town has a population of around 25,000, sits at roughly 1,100 meters elevation in the mountains of southern Jordan, and is essentially a service town built up around Petra tourism over the past 60 years.
How is Wadi Musa different from Petra?
Petra is the archaeological park; Wadi Musa is the modern town where you sleep, eat, and base your visit. They sit roughly 1 km apart — the Petra Visitor Centre is at the edge of Wadi Musa, with the town stretching uphill (east) from there. Most visitors arrive at Wadi Musa first, check into a hotel, and then walk or short-taxi to the Visitor Centre. The town has no Nabataean ruins itself — those are all inside the protected archaeological park.
Where should I stay in Wadi Musa?
Three main hotel zones. The "luxury cluster" near the Visitor Centre has the Mövenpick Resort and Petra Marriott, walking distance to the gate at premium prices. The "Tourism Street" stretch uphill from the Visitor Centre has most of the mid-range hotels — well-located, decent walking access to the gate, and the best restaurant density. The "uphill" zone further from the gate has the cheapest options including hostels, but you'll need taxis or shuttles for the gate. See the <a href="/hotels">hotels page</a> for specific recommendations.
Where do I eat?
Tourism Street has 15+ restaurants ranging from cheap shawarma to white-tablecloth Jordanian. Notable options: My Mom's Recipe (popular home-style Jordanian), Al-Wadi (mid-range with good mansaf), The Three Steps (rooftop with views), Petra Kitchen (cooking-class restaurant), and several casual falafel/shawarma spots for cheap lunches. The Mövenpick and Marriott both have fine-dining restaurants for travelers who want to stay in. Lunch in town typically runs 5-12 JOD per person; dinner 10-20 JOD.
Is there much to do in Wadi Musa beyond Petra?
Not really — and that's fine. Wadi Musa is a service town, not a destination in its own right. The interesting things to do are: visit Moses's Spring (Ain Musa, a small mosque on the main road with a freshwater spring inside, free), the Petra Museum at the Visitor Centre (small but well-curated, included with your Petra ticket), and any of the cooking classes run by Petra Kitchen and a few smaller operators. Most travelers spend evenings at their hotel pool, on a restaurant rooftop, or recovering for the next day at Petra.
What's the practical layout I need to know?
Wadi Musa is built along a single main road that climbs from the Petra Visitor Centre (at the bottom, 1,050m elevation) uphill to the east through several roundabouts. The "Tourism Street" section in the middle is where most hotels and restaurants concentrate. The "Wadi Musa town center" further uphill has banks, ATMs, pharmacies, and the daily grocery shops. The "King's Highway" (Route 35) bypasses the town to the east, connecting north toward Amman and south toward Aqaba. Taxis to the Visitor Centre from anywhere in town are 2-5 JOD.
