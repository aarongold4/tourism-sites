JORDAN

Aqaba

Jordan's Red Sea port city — and the country's most underrated destination. Coral reef diving, the Cedar Pride wreck, an alternative international airport, and the most efficient arrival point for Petra-focused trips. Here's what to actually do.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 9-minute read · See our editorial standards

Aqaba is the destination most Jordan travelers either skip entirely or include only as a transit stop. Both decisions can be defended — Aqaba isn't on the tier of Petra or Wadi Rum as a must-see — but the city earns more attention than it typically gets. The diving is genuinely world-class (less crowded than the Egyptian or Israeli Red Sea coastlines), the position makes it the most efficient arrival point for Petra-focused trips, and the food and resort options outclass what most travelers expect from a regional port city. For travelers spending more than 5 days in Jordan, Aqaba deserves at least a half-day stop.

This page covers what Aqaba actually offers, when to include it in your itinerary, the diving sites that justify the trip on their own, and how Aqaba fits into a wider Jordan loop. The framing throughout: Aqaba is the smart traveler's secondary destination, not a must-see, but with real value for the right kind of trip.

What Aqaba actually is

Aqaba is Jordan's only seaport, at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba on the Red Sea. The city has roughly 150,000 residents and serves as the country's commercial maritime gateway — container shipping, naval operations, and a small but growing tourism economy concentrated on the south beach hotel strip. Historically, the site has been continuously occupied since at least 4,000 BCE, with Islamic-era foundations on the corniche, the medieval-era Aqaba Castle (built into earlier Mamluk and Ottoman structures), and the wreck of T.E. Lawrence's Arab Revolt campaign visible in the city's recent past — Aqaba was captured by Lawrence's forces in 1917 in the famous overland attack from Wadi Rum.

Aqaba is a special economic zone (ASEZA — Aqaba Special Economic Zone Authority), which means slightly different tax rules than the rest of Jordan. Practical effect for travelers: rental cars and some imported goods are typically 10-15% cheaper here than in Amman. Fuel is also marginally cheaper.

What to do

The diving and snorkeling

The single best reason to visit Aqaba. The Gulf of Aqaba has 35+ named dive sites, with the major attractions including:

The Cedar Pride wreck. A 74-meter Lebanese freighter deliberately sunk in 1985 as an artificial reef. Now lies at 25-30 meters depth, covered in coral growth, accessible by both shore and boat dives. The most-photographed dive site in Jordan.

The Tank. An M42 anti-aircraft tank deliberately sunk at 6 meters depth as an artificial reef. Accessible to snorkelers and beginning divers, with surrounding coral growth.

The Japanese Gardens. A shore-accessible reef with dense coral and fish life. One of the most-dived sites in Aqaba, suitable for all experience levels.

Black Rock. A deeper site (20-30+ meters) with a dramatic underwater rock formation and pelagic fish. For more experienced divers.

Most dive shops cluster along the south beach near the resort strip. Expect 35-50 JOD per dive including equipment, or 25-30 JOD for snorkeling rentals plus boat fee. Major operators: Aqaba International Dive Centre, Sindbad Submarine and Marine Sports, Royal Diving Club.

Aqaba Castle (Mamluk Castle)

The 14th-century Mamluk-era castle dominates the city center on the corniche. Built on earlier foundations and modified by successive Ottoman and Arab Revolt occupations, the castle holds a small museum (Mamluk-era artifacts, T.E. Lawrence campaign material). Entry 3 JOD, free with Jordan Pass. Allow 45 minutes.

The corniche and the old town

The waterfront walk extends about 2 kilometers along the Gulf, with cafes, shisha bars, and the small old town immediately inland. Walking the corniche at sunset is one of Aqaba's better casual experiences — the Israeli coastline visible across the water to the west, the Saudi coastline to the east. The souks in the old town are working markets (gold, textiles, spices) rather than tourist-focused, with reasonably honest pricing.

Glass-bottom boats and snorkeling tours

For non-divers, glass-bottom boat tours run from the south beach for about 10-15 JOD per person for a 2-hour trip. The visibility is good enough that you can see coral and fish without getting wet. Snorkeling tours with equipment run 20-30 JOD per person for half-day trips, typically including 2-3 reef stops.

Where Aqaba fits in a Jordan trip

As the arrival point (instead of Amman)

For European travelers specifically, flying into Aqaba's King Hussein International Airport (AQJ) instead of Queen Alia in Amman saves 3 hours of driving on each end of a Petra/Wadi Rum trip. Direct flights from London (Wizz Air, Ryanair seasonal), Istanbul (Turkish Airlines, Pegasus), and several other European cities (Munich, Berlin, Vienna, Warsaw, Helsinki) make this practical. North American and East Asian travelers typically still arrive via Amman, but for European routes, Aqaba is often the cheaper and faster option. Worth checking when booking flights.

As a half-day stop on the southern leg

The most common Aqaba arrangement in a Jordan loop: drop in on the way south from Petra to the airport, or as a meal stop between Wadi Rum and the Eilat border crossing. A 3-4 hour stop covers a meal, a corniche walk, and the castle. The trip from Wadi Rum to Aqaba is 60 km / 1 hour.

As a 2-3 night dive trip

For travelers specifically interested in diving, Aqaba justifies 2-3 nights as a destination in its own right. Stay at one of the south beach hotels (Mövenpick Resort Aqaba, Kempinski Hotel Aqaba, InterContinental Aqaba) and dive 1-2 sites per day. The diving combined with Petra and Wadi Rum produces a strong 7-9 day Jordan trip that's distinctively different from the standard archaeological-focused itinerary.

Where to stay

Three zones with distinct character:

City center (corniche, old town). Best for budget and mid-range travelers. Hotels like the Captain's Hotel, Mina Hotel, or Aquavista cost 40-90 USD per night. Walking distance to the better restaurants and the working souks. Less polished but more authentic.

South beach resort strip. The Mövenpick Resort Aqaba, Kempinski Hotel Aqaba, InterContinental Aqaba, and Hyatt Regency Aqaba all sit along the south beach with private beach access, pools, restaurants, and dive-shop partnerships. $150-350 USD per night. Best for travelers prioritizing beach time and resort amenities.

Tala Bay (further south). The newer development zone — Mövenpick Tala Bay, Kempinski Tala Bay, Marina Plaza Tala Bay. More isolated, quieter, with marina access. Better for families with kids who want a contained resort experience. 20 minutes by taxi from central Aqaba.

Getting around

Aqaba is small enough to walk in most cases. The city center, corniche, and Aqaba Castle are all within a 1-kilometer radius. Taxis to the south beach resorts are 3-5 JOD; to Tala Bay are 8-12 JOD. Uber and Careem operate locally. The airport (AQJ) is 9 km north of the city center — 15 JOD by taxi.

When to visit

Aqaba has a longer comfortable season than the rest of Jordan because the Red Sea moderates temperatures year-round. Water temperature ranges from 22°C in February to 28°C in August. The best months for diving (clear water, comfortable air temperatures, lower crowds) are March-May and September-November. Summer (June-August) is hot on land (35-40°C) but the water is genuinely refreshing. Winter (December-February) has the coolest water but still divable, with the least crowded reefs and the lowest hotel rates.

Border crossings

Aqaba is the closest Jordanian city to the Wadi Araba/Yitzhak Rabin border crossing with Eilat (5 km). For travelers combining Jordan and Israel trips, Aqaba can serve as the staging point. See getting to Petra from Eilat for border-crossing logistics. Aqaba is also the closest Jordanian port to Egypt — ferries run from Aqaba to Nuweiba in the Sinai Peninsula, but the service is unreliable and most travelers crossing to Egypt go via Israel rather than the direct ferry.

Frequently asked questions

Where is Aqaba?
At the very southern tip of Jordan, on the Red Sea, where Jordan, Israel, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia all meet at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba. The city sits 330 km south of Amman, 130 km south of Petra, and 60 km south of Wadi Rum. King Hussein International Airport (AQJ) handles direct flights from London, Istanbul, and several European cities.
Why come to Aqaba?
Three real reasons. (1) Coral reef diving and snorkeling — the Red Sea coral reefs off Aqaba are world-class and significantly less crowded than the Egyptian or Eilat side. (2) An alternative arrival point — flying into AQJ instead of Amman saves 3 hours of driving on each end for Petra/Wadi Rum-focused trips. (3) A resort beach stop for travelers who want pool/sun time as part of their Jordan trip. Aqaba is not a major sightseeing destination — it's a port city with a small old town, a few historical sites, and the Red Sea as the main draw.
How long should I spend in Aqaba?
For most travelers, 0-1 nights. A half-day stop is plenty if you're not diving — see the small fort, walk the corniche, eat a meal, continue onward. For divers, 2-3 nights enables 4-6 dives across the major sites (the Cedar Pride wreck, the Japanese Gardens reef, the Power Station reef). For travelers wanting a beach-resort experience, 2-3 nights at one of the hotel-strip properties works well. Beyond 3 nights, you're probably underutilizing Aqaba relative to other Jordan destinations.
Is Aqaba good for diving?
Yes — genuinely world-class and underrated. The Gulf of Aqaba has 35+ named dive sites within easy boat or shore access, water visibility typically 20-30 meters, water temperatures 22-28°C year-round, and significantly less crowded reefs than the Egyptian or Israeli sides. Major sites include the Cedar Pride wreck (a 74-meter Lebanese freighter sunk as an artificial reef in 1985), the Tank (a deliberately-sunk M42 anti-aircraft tank at 6 meters), the Japanese Gardens (a coral-rich reef accessible from shore), and the Black Rock site. Dive shops cluster along the south beach; expect 35-50 JOD per dive including equipment.
What's the food scene like?
Aqaba has a strong seafood reputation — the Red Sea coastline produces fresh fish daily, and several restaurants in the city center specialize in grilled fish and Middle Eastern seafood preparations. Standouts: Ali Baba Restaurant for traditional Arabic seafood, Floka Restaurant for the freshest grilled fish, Romero for higher-end Italian-Mediterranean. Budget travelers eat well at the streetside falafel and shawarma places near the city center. Most resort hotels have decent in-house restaurants but the better food is in town.
Where should I stay in Aqaba?
Three zones. The city center (around the corniche and the souk) is best for budget and mid-range travelers — close to the better restaurants, the old town atmosphere, and the boat docks. The hotel strip on south beach (Mövenpick, Kempinski, InterContinental) is the resort experience — beach access, pools, spas, $150-350 USD per night. The Tala Bay area further south is more isolated but quieter — newer resorts (Mövenpick Tala Bay, Kempinski Tala Bay) with private beaches, ideal for families wanting a beach-only stay.
