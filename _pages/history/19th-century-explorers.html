HISTORY

The 19th-century explorers

After Burckhardt's 1812 visit, a generation of European travelers documented Petra through drawings, surveys, and eventually photography. Léon de Laborde, David Roberts, Edward Robinson — the figures who turned the site from a rumored ruin into an internationally-known place.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 7-minute read · See our editorial standards

The story of Petra's rediscovery doesn't end with Johann Ludwig Burckhardt's brief August 1812 visit. Burckhardt confirmed that Petra existed and produced a basic written account, but he didn't draw, photograph, or systematically document the site — his Swiss-Arab disguise required him to move quickly and avoid attention. The actual documentation of Petra for European audiences came in the decades that followed, through a generation of explorers, artists, biblical scholars, and adventurers whose work turned the rumored ruin into an internationally-known place.

This page covers the major 19th-century figures who documented Petra after Burckhardt: who they were, what they produced, and how their work shaped the modern understanding of the site. The framing throughout: the visible Petra you see today exists in the international imagination because of these decades of accumulated work.

Léon de Laborde (1828)

The first European to publish illustrations of Petra. Léon de Laborde was a French aristocrat, archaeologist, and artist who traveled through Petra in 1828, sixteen years after Burckhardt's visit. His Voyage de l'Arabie Pétrée (1830) included engravings of the Treasury, the Monastery, the major Royal Tombs, and the Siq — the first time European audiences could see what Petra actually looked like rather than reading verbal descriptions.

The Laborde illustrations are technically engravings produced from his on-site drawings, sometimes with creative liberties (slightly idealized proportions, atmospheric enhancements, the addition of figures for scale and drama). They're not photographs and shouldn't be read as exact records. But they're remarkable historical documents — the first visual representation of Petra to reach mass audiences, predating photography by several decades.

Laborde's work also included the first reasonably accurate site plan of central Petra, identifying the major monuments and their relative positions. This map became the basis for later survey work.

Edward Robinson (1838)

The American biblical scholar Edward Robinson and his colleague Eli Smith visited Petra in 1838 as part of a broader survey of biblical sites in the Levant. Robinson's work was scholarly rather than artistic — his Biblical Researches in Palestine, Mount Sinai and Arabia Petraea (1841) is a multi-volume systematic survey of Holy Land geography that effectively founded modern biblical archaeology as a discipline.

Robinson's Petra documentation focused on identifying biblical references in the local geography, recording inscriptions, and producing reasonably accurate measurements of major monuments. He didn't have Laborde's artistic flair, but he had scholarly precision that later researchers built on.

Robinson's work is also significant because it established Petra in the American imagination. American biblical scholars in the second half of the 19th century read Robinson's account and pursued American-based research on Holy Land sites — including, eventually, the American excavations at Petra in the 20th century.

David Roberts (1839)

The Scottish painter David Roberts is the figure most associated with the 19th-century visual documentation of Petra. Roberts traveled through Egypt, Sinai, Petra, and the Levant in 1838-1839, producing on-site drawings that were later worked up into the multi-volume folio The Holy Land, Syria, Idumea, Arabia, Egypt and Nubia (1842-1849). The published lithographs (technically tinted lithographs based on Roberts's drawings, with hand-coloring in some editions) became the dominant 19th-century visual representation of Petra.

The Roberts lithographs of Petra include:

  • The Treasury (Al-Khazneh) — the most famous image, showing the facade with figures in the foreground for scale
  • The Siq approach to the Treasury, capturing the dramatic narrow-canyon arrival
  • The Monastery (Ad-Deir) in its mountain setting
  • The Royal Tombs (Urn Tomb, Silk Tomb, Corinthian Tomb)
  • The Roman theatre and the colonnaded street
  • Various views of the broader Petra landscape

The Roberts images are technically interpretations rather than exact records — proportions are sometimes idealized, atmospheric effects are dramatic, the figures are deliberately placed for visual interest. But they captured Petra's essential character with extraordinary skill, and they shaped how generations of European and American readers imagined the site before photography became common.

Roberts's broader Holy Land folio remains a major collector's item; original 19th-century editions sell for tens of thousands of dollars. Reproductions of the Petra lithographs are widely available and remain among the most beautiful visual representations of the site.

Other significant 19th-century figures

John Lloyd Stephens (1836)

An American lawyer and explorer who visited Petra in 1836, between Laborde and Roberts. His Incidents of Travel in Egypt, Arabia Petraea, and the Holy Land (1837) was the first widely-read American account of Petra. Stephens went on to become famous for his later explorations of Maya sites in Central America, but his Petra account was significant in establishing the site in the American popular imagination.

The Duc de Luynes expedition (1864)

A French scientific expedition led by Honoré d'Albert, Duc de Luynes, that visited Petra and produced a major scholarly publication on the Dead Sea region. The expedition included Charles-Marie de Vogüé, a young architect whose drawings of Petra monuments were among the most accurate produced in the 19th century.

Charles Doughty (1875-1878)

A British poet and travel writer who included Petra in his extended Arabian travels of the late 1870s. His Travels in Arabia Deserta (1888) became a classic of travel literature, prized for its linguistic complexity and atmospheric description. T.E. Lawrence later credited Doughty as a major influence on his own Arabian work.

Gertrude Bell (late 1890s, early 1900s)

The British archaeologist, traveler, and political officer Gertrude Bell visited Petra multiple times in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Her detailed photographs and notes contributed to the early-20th-century systematic documentation, and her broader work shaped British policy in the post-Ottoman Middle East after World War I.

The transition to photography

The first photographs of Petra date to 1864, produced by Frank Mason Good and other early photographers working with biblical-archaeological survey teams. Wet-plate collodion photography required heavy equipment (cameras, glass plates, portable darkroom tents) that was extremely difficult to transport through the Siq, so the earliest photographs were limited in number and scope.

By the 1880s, dry-plate technology had simplified the equipment requirements, and photographs of Petra became more common. The British photographer Frances Frith produced widely-circulated commercial photographs of Petra in the 1860s-1870s. By the 1890s, Petra was being photographed by professional commercial photographers, amateur travelers, and ethnographers documenting Bedouin life.

Photography didn't entirely replace drawing and painting — the Roberts tradition continued through later 19th-century artists who came to Petra specifically to paint. But photography became the dominant documentary medium by the early 20th century, and the transition from drawings to photographs represents the boundary between the explorer-era and the modern documented era of Petra.

What the explorers contributed

Three categories of contribution from the 19th-century explorers:

Visual documentation. Laborde, Roberts, and the broader generation of artists turned Petra from a verbal description into a visually-known place. The Roberts lithographs in particular shaped the Western imagination of the site for decades and remain influential today.

Scholarly survey. Robinson, the Duc de Luynes expedition, and various 19th-century researchers established the basic facts about Petra — geographic position, major monuments, accessible routes, basic chronology — that later professional archaeology built on. The work was preliminary by modern standards, but it gave subsequent researchers a baseline to refine.

Political and diplomatic groundwork. The explorers established European and American interest in the region that ultimately led to the political and academic structures supporting modern archaeological work. The post-WWI mandate system, the founding of academic institutions like the American Center of Oriental Research, and the eventual UNESCO designation all rest on the 19th-century groundwork that put Petra into the international cultural conversation.

The complications

The 19th-century exploration period has complications that the modern critical view should acknowledge:

Damage and disruption. Some explorers took rubbings of carved inscriptions, removed small artifacts as souvenirs or for study collections, occasionally vandalized monuments with their own inscriptions, and disturbed archaeological context in ways that later professional archaeology would not allow. The damage was not catastrophic but it was real.

Colonial framing. The 19th-century explorers operated within colonial-era assumptions about European cultural superiority and indigenous lack of historical awareness. The framing led to mischaracterizations of the local Bedouin population, exaggerated claims of "discovery" that ignored continuous local knowledge, and a broader pattern of European actors writing themselves as the protagonists of someone else's history.

Selective documentation. The 19th-century work emphasized certain aspects of Petra (the monumental architecture, biblical connections, romantic atmospheric drama) while undervaluing others (the practical engineering, the continuing Bedouin community, the broader regional context). The selective emphasis shaped how Petra was understood for generations and required correction by later more comprehensive work.

Frequently asked questions

Who rediscovered Petra after Burckhardt?
A series of European travelers visited Petra in the decades after Burckhardt's August 1812 visit, each adding to the documentation. Léon de Laborde (1828) produced the first published illustrations of Petra, making the site visually known to European audiences. Edward Robinson (1838) did the first systematic biblical-geographical survey. David Roberts (1839) produced the famous lithographs that fixed Petra in the Western imagination for the rest of the 19th century. Later figures included Charles Doughty, Gertrude Bell, and T.E. Lawrence, who visited in the early 20th century.
Who was David Roberts?
A Scottish painter (1796-1864) who traveled to Egypt, Petra, and the Levant in 1838-1839. His drawings from this trip were published in The Holy Land, Syria, Idumea, Arabia, Egypt and Nubia (1842-1849), a multi-volume folio of lithographs that became the most influential 19th-century visual record of Petra. The Roberts lithographs of the Treasury, the Monastery, the Siq, and the High Place of Sacrifice circulated widely in Europe and shaped how generations of readers imagined the site before mass photography made firsthand visual access easier.
When was Petra first photographed?
1864, by Frank Mason Good, an English photographer working with biblical-archaeological survey teams. The first photographs were technically demanding given the heavy equipment required for wet-plate collodion photography and the difficulty of transporting it through the Siq. Photography largely replaced drawings as the documentary medium by the 1880s; by the 1900s, Petra was being photographed by amateur travelers, ethnographers, and professional commercial photographers alike.
Did the explorers cause damage?
Less than the modern critical view sometimes suggests, but not zero. The 19th-century European visitors took rubbings, removed small objects, occasionally vandalized monuments with inscriptions, and disturbed archaeological context in ways that later professional archaeology would not allow. But they also produced the documentation that made the site internationally known and ultimately led to its protection. The relationship between exploration and damage is complicated; the explorers operated by the standards of their time, which were not the standards of modern archaeology.
When did systematic archaeology begin?
The early 20th century, with the first systematic excavation work in the 1920s and 1930s. Brünnow and Domaszewski's three-volume Die Provincia Arabia (1904-1909) was the first comprehensive scholarly survey. The German archaeologist Walter Bachmann's documentation work in the 1910s established the basic site mapping. Sustained systematic excavation began with the Jordanian Department of Antiquities and various international partner institutions from the 1950s onward; major projects continue today, including the American Center of Oriental Research (ACOR) work that produced the Petra Church discovery in 1990.
