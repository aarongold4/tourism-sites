ACCESSIBILITY

Petra accessibility

An honest assessment of what Petra can and cannot offer to visitors with mobility constraints. The horse-drawn carriage to the Treasury, the club car service, and realistic expectations for the parts of the site that simply aren't accessible.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 6-minute read · See our editorial standards

Petra is genuinely difficult for visitors with significant mobility constraints, and we want to be honest about that. The site is a working archaeological park that has prioritized preservation of original surfaces over modern accessibility infrastructure — which is defensible as heritage policy but means the standard accommodations travelers might expect at a major museum or monument don't exist here. The Treasury and the area immediately around it can be reached with assistance. Most of the rest of Petra cannot be reached by wheelchair under any current arrangement.

What we can offer is a clear picture of what's possible, what's borderline, and what isn't — so travelers can make informed decisions. If you or someone in your party uses a wheelchair, has limited stamina, or has any condition that makes uneven terrain difficult, this page covers the practical reality of a Petra visit and the workarounds that exist.

What's accessible

The Petra Visitor Centre. Modern building, ramp access at the entrance, accessible toilets, ground-floor museum and ticket counter. Fully wheelchair-accessible.

The 800-meter path from the Visitor Centre to the Siq entrance. Paved, mostly flat with a gentle downhill grade. Walkable with a walker or cane; passable with a manual wheelchair given attendant assistance for the small inclines. The path passes the Djinn Blocks (visible from the path) and the Obelisk Tomb (also visible from the path, no climbing required).

The Siq via horse-drawn carriage. The standard mobility option. A horse-drawn carriage takes visitors through the Siq from a queueing point near the entrance to the Treasury plaza. The carriage carries 2–3 adults plus driver. Cost: ~25 JOD round trip (negotiable). The carriage handles the gentle Siq descent and the slightly bumpy cobbled floor; the ride is uncomfortable for visitors with back conditions but doable for most travelers with mobility constraints. The carriage doesn't normally allow wheelchairs aboard; you transfer to the carriage seat for the ride.

The Treasury plaza. A relatively flat open area with sandy ground. A motorized wheelchair can navigate it; a manual wheelchair will struggle in sand. Once at the plaza, you can stay as long as you want and photograph the Treasury from multiple angles. There are no formal seats but the carriage drivers can usually arrange a chair.

What's borderline

The lower city beyond the Treasury. The path continues from the Treasury plaza into the Outer Siq and the lower city — Street of Facades, then the open lower city with the Roman theatre, colonnaded street, Great Temple, and Qasr al-Bint. The path is mostly compact dirt and sand. Walkable but tiring for visitors with limited stamina; possible with a motorized wheelchair but the rough surfaces are uncomfortable.

The Petra Authority club car service. The Petra Authority operates electric club cars that can be requested to take visitors with mobility constraints from the Treasury area through the lower city. Service is on request only and sometimes irregular — call the Visitor Centre 24+ hours in advance to discuss your needs. Cost is typically modest (10–20 JOD round trip) but varies by negotiation. When the service runs, it transforms the Petra visit for travelers who otherwise couldn't reach the lower city.

The Basin Restaurant. The restaurant is at the far end of the lower city, accessible by walking or by the club car if arranged. The restaurant itself has outdoor seating that can be reached by wheelchair, indoor seating that requires a small step. Buffet service.

What's not accessible

The Royal Tombs upper terrace. Requires climbing carved Nabataean steps. Not wheelchair-accessible. Can be photographed from the lower city below.

The Monastery. 800 stone steps. Not accessible to wheelchair users. Donkey rides up the trail exist but are physically difficult and welfare-questionable; we don't recommend them as an accessibility solution. The honest position: most visitors with significant mobility constraints will not see the Monastery on a Petra visit.

The High Place of Sacrifice. 600 stone steps. Not accessible.

The Al-Khubtha trail to the Treasury viewpoint. Steep, uneven, not accessible.

Little Petra. The slot canyon itself is mostly flat and walkable; the steps to the painted Biclinium are not accessible, but the lower-level chambers are visible from the canyon floor.

Practical advice for visitors with mobility needs

Plan for a partial visit. The realistic visit for most travelers with mobility constraints is: Visitor Centre → horse-drawn carriage through the Siq → Treasury plaza → return by carriage. This takes about 2–3 hours and produces a genuine Petra experience, though a limited one. If the club car is available, add 1–2 hours for the lower city.

Call ahead about the club car. 48+ hours notice gives the Petra Authority time to arrange the service. Have your hotel call if you don't speak Arabic — the formal English-language contact systems are inconsistent.

Pick a Wadi Musa hotel with elevator access. Several mid-range and upper hotels have elevators (Mövenpick, Marriott, Petra Guest House); smaller hostels and family-run hotels often do not. The Wadi Musa main street is hilly, so hotel location matters.

Consider hiring a Bedouin guide. A licensed Petra guide (50 JOD for 2 hours) can be invaluable for visitors with mobility needs — they know which routes are easier, where the club car runs, and how to arrange the carriage and donkey services. Ask at the Visitor Centre for a guide with experience helping mobility-limited travelers.

Don't try Petra in summer. The combination of heat and difficult surfaces is unsafe for many visitors with mobility constraints. October, November, March, and April are the right months.

For visitors with other accessibility needs

Sight-impaired visitors: Petra is challenging — the variable surfaces and frequent steps make it hazardous without an attentive guide. Audio descriptions are not formally available at the site; a private guide can provide rich verbal description.

Hearing-impaired visitors: Petra is visual enough that hearing impairment is not a major barrier. Most signage is in Arabic and English. Sign language interpretation is not formally available; written communication with Bedouin guides works.

Visitors with chronic conditions affecting stamina: The horse-drawn carriage option is the key adaptation. A heart condition, lung disease, or chronic fatigue condition all benefit from skipping the Siq walk in both directions.

Frequently asked questions

Is Petra wheelchair-accessible?
Partially, and only with significant compromise. The Petra Visitor Centre, the path from the Visitor Centre to the Siq entrance (~800m, paved), and the area around the Treasury plaza can be reached by motorized wheelchair or with assistance. Beyond the Treasury, the surfaces become rough — compact dirt mixed with sand, uneven cobbles, and eventually the steep stairs of the Monastery and High Place trails. Most wheelchair users will see the Treasury and the immediate area, and that's the realistic limit.
Can I take the horse-drawn carriage if I have mobility issues?
Yes — the horse-drawn carriage from the Visitor Centre area to the Treasury is the standard mobility aid at Petra. Cost is around 25 JOD round trip (negotiable; 20 JOD for a clearly arranged round trip is common). The ride is bumpy on the cobbled Siq floor; visitors with back pain or balance issues should consider this carefully before booking. The carriage drops at the Treasury plaza and waits for the return journey on request.
What about the Petra Authority club car?
For elderly visitors and visitors with documented mobility constraints, the Petra Authority operates electric club cars that can travel the main path through the lower city. Service is on request — call the Petra Visitor Centre in advance (or have your hotel call for you) and explain your needs. Service is more reliably available with 24+ hours notice and is sometimes used during Petra by Night for visitors who can't walk the Siq at night.
Can I do Petra with crutches or a walking aid?
With effort, partially. The Siq itself is paved with uneven cobbles — challenging on crutches, particularly the descending route at the Treasury end where the surface dips. The lower city is mostly compact dirt and sand, walkable but tiring. The carriage option (Visitor Centre to Treasury and back) eliminates the hardest portion. Past the Treasury, decisions get harder — most of the lower city has no flat path, and the Royal Tombs require climbing carved steps to reach.
Are the toilets accessible?
The Visitor Centre toilets are modern and wheelchair-accessible. Inside the site, the toilets at the Treasury plaza and Basin Restaurant are basic — functional but not formally wheelchair-rated. The Monastery cafe has a small toilet that is not accessible.
What about Petra by Night specifically?
Limited accessibility. The 1.2 km Siq walk in low light is the major barrier. The Petra Authority can sometimes arrange a club car for the Siq route for visitors who genuinely cannot walk it, but the service is irregular and not guaranteed. Best contact the Visitor Centre 48 hours in advance to discuss options. For visitors with mobility constraints, the daytime visit to the Treasury is more reliably manageable than the evening experience.
