The 363 earthquake

On May 19, 363 CE, a major earthquake devastated Petra and the surrounding region. The damage was severe enough to mark the beginning of the city's long decline — the moment after which Petra ceased to be a major place. Here's what happened and why it matters.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 7-minute read · See our editorial standards

On May 19, 363 CE, a major earthquake struck the region encompassing modern Jordan, Israel/Palestine, and southern Syria. The damage at Petra was severe — many of the city's free-standing monuments collapsed or were heavily damaged, substantial portions of the water management infrastructure failed, and the broader urban fabric was disrupted in ways that the city never fully recovered from. While Petra continued to be inhabited for several more centuries after the earthquake, the 363 event marks the beginning of the long decline that eventually reduced the city to the abandoned ruin that 19th-century explorers rediscovered.

This page covers what we know about the earthquake — when and where it struck, what damage it caused at Petra, what the contemporary sources tell us, and why it matters for understanding the visible site today. The framing throughout: this is the moment after which Petra ceased to be a major place.

The historical sources

We know about the 363 earthquake from an unusual combination of Christian, Jewish, and archaeological sources that converge on a precise date and scale.

The Cyril of Jerusalem letter. The most important contemporary source is a letter from Cyril, the Christian Bishop of Jerusalem, to the Roman emperor Julian (the Apostate). Julian had ordered the rebuilding of the Jewish Temple in Jerusalem in 362 CE as part of his program of opposing Christianity by promoting other religions. Construction began in 363; the earthquake of May 19 destroyed the construction site, and Cyril interpreted the event as divine intervention against Julian's project. The letter discusses the earthquake's effects in some detail, including damage at multiple sites in the region.

Roman historical sources. The Roman historian Ammianus Marcellinus, writing in the late 4th century, references regional disruptions consistent with major seismic activity, though without specifically dating the event.

Jewish sources. Several rabbinic sources (the Talmud and related literature) reference earthquake disruption in the period, though dating is less precise than the Christian sources.

Archaeological evidence. The most extensive evidence is the physical damage itself. At Petra specifically, the archaeological excavations of the 1990s-2010s (Brown University, Wadi Mousa local missions, and others) have documented stratigraphic layers showing the 363 destruction event — collapsed columns, displaced foundations, abandoned construction projects, and rebuilding activity dated immediately after the event.

What happened on May 19, 363

The earthquake originated on the Dead Sea Transform fault system, the major fault line running south from southern Turkey through the Beqaa Valley, the Jordan Valley, the Dead Sea, the Wadi Araba, and the Gulf of Aqaba. This fault system is the boundary between the Arabian and African tectonic plates and has generated multiple major earthquakes throughout recorded history.

The 363 earthquake had an estimated magnitude of approximately 7.0 on modern measurement scales — large enough to cause widespread structural damage across a region several hundred kilometers wide. The damage radius included Jerusalem, Petra, Aila (modern Aqaba), and probably extended into the broader region of the Decapolis cities. Multiple settlements show 363-era destruction layers in their archaeological record.

At Petra specifically, the earthquake struck a city that had already been declining slowly since the Roman annexation in 106 CE. The Nabataean kingdom's peak prosperity was 250 years in the past; the major construction era was over; the trade routes that had originally made the city wealthy had partially shifted to alternative paths (the southern Red Sea route via Palmyra, and direct sea routes between Mediterranean ports and Yemen). Petra was still a substantial place — perhaps 10,000-15,000 inhabitants — but it was already past its peak. The earthquake hit a city that lacked the political and economic momentum to rebuild fully.

The damage

The damage pattern at Petra was characteristic of major seismic events striking a city with mixed construction types:

Free-standing built structures. The most damaged. The Great Temple complex, Qasr al-Bint, the colonnaded street infrastructure, the Roman theatre, and the various civic buildings of the lower city all show 363-era damage. Many of the columns visible lying on the ground today (in original sequence, showing they fell as units rather than being later dismantled) date to this collapse.

Water management infrastructure. Significant damage to the dam systems, channels, and storage cisterns. The Nabataean water management network had been the city's critical infrastructure for 400 years; large-scale damage to it would have been catastrophic for daily life. Some sections were rebuilt after 363 but the integrated system probably never recovered to its full Nabataean-era capacity.

Carved monuments. The Treasury, the Monastery, and the major Royal Tombs survived comparatively well because they're carved into the bedrock rather than free-standing. They are part of the cliff, not built on top of it. Some damage occurred — surface cracks, fragmentation of decorative details, displacement of carved elements — but the basic structures remained intact. This is why the carved facades dominate the visible Petra today while the free-standing monuments survive primarily as foundations and collapsed columns.

Residential areas. The Nabataean housing on the slopes around the valley was largely destroyed. Subsequent occupation reduced these areas to small clusters of rebuilt structures rather than the dense urban fabric of the peak period.

What survived

Petra was not destroyed in 363 — the city continued to be inhabited for several more centuries, and significant Byzantine-era construction followed the earthquake. The key surviving elements:

The carved monuments. As noted, the integral-to-cliff structures survived. The Treasury, the Monastery, and the Royal Tombs all remained accessible and visible through the post-363 centuries.

The trade routes. Petra's position on the trade routes between Arabia and the Mediterranean meant that some level of commercial activity continued. The Byzantine-period city was smaller but not abandoned.

The Byzantine Christian community. Petra had a substantial Christian population by the 5th century, building churches on the slopes above the colonnaded street. The Petra Church (discovered with surviving mosaic floors in 1990) is the most prominent surviving Byzantine structure.

The Bedouin community. The local Bedouin tribes who had inhabited the region since before the Nabataean rise continued to live in and around Petra through all the subsequent centuries, eventually becoming the Bdoul community that lived in the caves of Petra until the 1980s. See the Bdoul for that story.

The longer decline

The 363 earthquake didn't immediately end Petra, but it marked the beginning of a slow decline that continued through the next few centuries. Subsequent events deepened the decline:

The 551 CE Beirut earthquake. Another major seismic event causing additional damage to surviving structures.

The Justinianic Plague (541-549 CE). The first major plague pandemic in the Mediterranean world; reduced populations across the entire region, including at Petra.

The Arab conquests of the 630s. Brought political integration into the Caliphate but shifted trade routes northward and reduced Petra's importance.

The 749 CE Galilee earthquake. Devastated the broader region, including Jerash. Whether Petra suffered additional damage in this event is debated, but the timing fits a broader regional collapse.

By the 8th and 9th centuries, Petra had ceased to be a major settlement. The Bedouin population continued to use the caves and rock-cut structures for shelter, but the city as an urban entity was effectively gone. From this point until the 19th-century rediscovery by Burckhardt, Petra existed primarily as a local Bedouin space rather than a known place in the wider world.

What to look at on your visit

Specific 363-era damage visible on a standard Petra visit:

  • Fallen columns at the Great Temple. The drums lying on the ground in original sequence — the columns fell during the earthquake and were never re-erected.
  • Collapsed walls at Qasr al-Bint. The largest free-standing temple at Petra shows extensive 363-era damage. See Qasr al-Bint for monument detail.
  • The colonnaded street. The original Nabataean and Roman-period pavement is mostly buried; what's visible is largely Byzantine-era reconstruction on top of the 363 destruction layer.
  • Cracks and damage to the Royal Tombs. The most visible carved monuments show some surface damage that probably dates to the earthquake.

Frequently asked questions

When was the 363 earthquake?
May 19, 363 CE. The date is firmly established from the historical record — the Roman emperor Julian (the Apostate) died from a battle wound in Mesopotamia in late June 363, and a letter from Cyril of Jerusalem to Julian discussed earthquake damage in the region in the weeks just before Julian's death. The synchronism between the Christian record and the seismic damage at Petra establishes the date with unusual precision for an ancient seismic event.
How bad was the damage?
Severe but not annihilating. The earthquake damaged or destroyed many of Petra's free-standing structures — the Great Temple, Qasr al-Bint, the colonnaded street infrastructure, and substantial portions of the water management system all show damage from this event. The carved facades (Treasury, Monastery, Royal Tombs) survived better because they're integral to the bedrock cliffs rather than free-standing constructions. After 363, Petra continued as a settlement but at significantly reduced scale and prosperity.
Was this the only major earthquake at Petra?
No — Petra sits in a seismically active region (the Dead Sea Transform fault system) and has experienced multiple significant earthquakes through its history. The 363 CE event is the best-documented and most damaging in the historical record, but other major events include the 32 BCE earthquake (predating most of the surviving monuments), the 551 CE Beirut earthquake (which caused additional damage), and the 749 CE Galilee earthquake (which devastated the broader region including Jerash and Amman). The cumulative effect of multiple seismic events explains the current state of the visible ruins.
Did anyone survive at Petra after 363?
Yes — Petra continued to be inhabited through the Byzantine period (roughly 330-636 CE) and into the early Islamic period. The Byzantine-era church on the slopes above the colonnaded street (with surviving 6th-century mosaic floors discovered in 1990) demonstrates continued occupation. But the population was much smaller than during the Nabataean peak, and the city's role shifted from a major trade entrepot to a smaller regional center.
Can you see earthquake damage today?
Yes, throughout the site. The collapsed columns at the Great Temple, the largely-ruined state of Qasr al-Bint, the partly-buried colonnaded street, the displaced stones throughout the lower city — all show seismic damage. Travelers attentive to it can spot specific evidence: column drums lying in original sequence on the ground (showing the columns fell intact rather than being deliberately dismantled), foundations shifted from their original positions, and patterns of collapse consistent with horizontal seismic motion.
