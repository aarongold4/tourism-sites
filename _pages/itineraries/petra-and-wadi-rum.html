ITINERARIES

Petra and Wadi Rum

Jordan's two headline destinations sit 120 kilometers and 2 hours apart, and the right way to combine them is the deciding question for most Jordan trips. Petra first, two days; Wadi Rum after, ideally two nights. Here's how the combined itinerary actually works.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 10-minute read · See our editorial standards

For most travelers, Petra and Wadi Rum are the two non-negotiables of a Jordan trip — and combining them is the deciding question for how the whole itinerary works. They sit 120 kilometers apart, both south of the country, accessible from each other in about 2 hours of driving. They're also dramatically different experiences: Petra is high-energy walking days on uneven terrain at archaeological sites; Wadi Rum is seated jeep tours through dramatic desert landscapes followed by Bedouin camp evenings. Combining them in the right order, with the right number of nights, makes both work better than either does alone.

This page covers the practical logistics: how long each takes, which to do first, how to get between them, and what each experience is actually like once you arrive. It's not a hour-by-hour itinerary like the dedicated Petra two-day plan; it's the framing page that helps you decide how to allocate your Jordan time.

The basic math

The minimum viable Petra + Wadi Rum trip is three days: two full days at Petra, one night in a Wadi Rum Bedouin camp. This is what most package tour operators sell as a 2-day or 3-day combination from Amman, and it works as the compressed version.

The recommended version is four days: two days at Petra, two nights in Wadi Rum. The Wadi Rum second night is what transforms the experience from a jeep tour into a real desert visit — proper sunrise time, stargazing on a moonless night, an unhurried Bedouin camp evening, and the option of a balloon flight (best done on a second morning when you're already in the desert rather than scrambling between camps).

A five- or six-day version adds either: an extra Petra day (for hikers wanting Jebel Haroun or photographers wanting all the light), a Dana Biosphere stop between Petra and Wadi Rum (for hikers who want a different desert experience), or an Aqaba night (for divers).

Why Petra first

The sequencing decision matters more than you'd expect. Petra first, Wadi Rum after, is the right answer for almost everyone. Three reasons:

Energy management. Petra is a walking trip — 8 to 12 kilometers per day on uneven stone, with steep climbs to the Monastery and the high places. You want to do it while fresh, before fatigue starts to compound. Wadi Rum, by contrast, is mostly seated (jeep tours, camel rides, evening at camp), which makes it a better recovery experience.

Logistical flow. Most travelers come from the north (Amman or the Dead Sea) and continue south. Petra is the northern of the two destinations; Wadi Rum is south. Doing them in that order means you're consistently moving south, not backtracking. After Wadi Rum, you can continue to Aqaba (1 hour south) for the airport or to cross to Eilat, rather than driving back up to Petra.

The Wadi Rum night sky. Bedouin camps offer some of the best stargazing in the Middle East — minimal light pollution, dry desert air, and the Milky Way visible from horizon to horizon on a moonless night. After two physically demanding Petra days, the Wadi Rum night is the recovery moment. Doing it in the other order — Wadi Rum first, exhausting yourself in Petra after — wastes both experiences.

Getting between them

Three main options.

JETT bus. A daily scheduled service connects Petra and Wadi Rum directly. The current 2026 schedule is roughly: Petra → Wadi Rum at 17:00 (arrives 18:30), Wadi Rum → Petra at 09:00 (arrives 10:30). Fare is around 22 JOD ($30 USD) one-way. The timing works well for late-afternoon arrival in Wadi Rum — you reach camp in time for dinner and the sunset jeep tour.

Camp-arranged transfer. Most Wadi Rum Bedouin camps will arrange transfer to or from Petra for guests. Typical cost: 30–50 JOD per car (so cheaper than JETT for groups of 3+). Pickup at your Wadi Musa hotel, drop at the Wadi Rum Visitor Centre or directly at the camp. Arrange this when you book your camp stay, ideally a week ahead.

Rental car or hired driver. A rental car gives you the flexibility to leave Petra when you want, stop at the Shobak Castle ruins on the way (a 30-minute detour and well worth it), and reach Wadi Rum on your own schedule. The drive is straightforward — 120 km south on the Desert Highway, 2 hours. Parking at the Wadi Rum Visitor Centre is free but you'll need to leave the car there overnight; most camps will pick you up from the Visitor Centre in a 4WD.

What Wadi Rum is actually like

For travelers who've only read about Wadi Rum, a quick orientation. Wadi Rum is a 720-square-kilometer protected desert area, roughly 60 km north of Aqaba, characterized by enormous sandstone and granite rock formations rising out of red sand. The area is administered jointly with the local Zalabia Bedouin tribe, who run most of the tourism — jeep tours, Bedouin camps, camel treks, and the major experiences. Lawrence of Arabia campaigned here during the Arab Revolt of 1916–1918, which produced the book and the David Lean film, both of which made the area internationally famous. More recently, much of the Mars footage in *The Martian* and several scenes in *Dune* were filmed here.

The typical first-night experience at a Wadi Rum camp:

  • 14:00–16:00: Arrive at the Visitor Centre, meet your camp guide, transfer to the camp by 4WD (typically 30–45 minutes into the desert).
  • 16:00–19:00: Jeep tour through the major rock formations — Lawrence's Spring, the Khazali Canyon petroglyphs, the Mushroom Rock, the natural rock bridges. Sunset at one of the high viewpoints (Um Sabatah is a common choice).
  • 19:30: Dinner at camp — traditional Zarb, slow-cooked underground in a sand oven, meat and vegetables. Eaten communally with other camp guests around low tables.
  • 20:30–22:00: Evening around the fire. Bedouin tea, sometimes traditional music or storytelling. Stargazing if the moon is right.
  • 23:00: Sleep in a Bedouin tent — rugs and thick blankets, no electricity in basic camps, comfortable beds in mid-range and luxury camps.

The second day adds: 06:00 sunrise option (worth it), longer jeep tour with stops at less-visited formations, a camel trek for an hour or two if you want it, and the option of returning to camp for another night or continuing onward to Aqaba or Petra.

Choosing a Wadi Rum camp

Three rough tiers.

Basic Bedouin camps ($30–60 USD per person per night). Standard tents on a sand floor, shared bathrooms, communal dining. Authentic but rustic. Includes dinner and breakfast and typically the basic jeep tour. Examples: Bedouin Whispers, Wadi Rum Bedouin Camp.

Mid-range camps ($80–150 USD per person per night). Private tents with proper beds, attached bathrooms in newer camps, better dinners, more comfortable common areas. Most travelers should look here. Examples: Wadi Rum Sky Tours camp, Memories Aicha.

Luxury "bubble" camps ($200–500 USD per person per night). Transparent-roof dome tents for stargazing from your bed, full bathrooms, restaurant-quality meals, often with hot tubs. The Instagram version of Wadi Rum. Examples: Sun City Camp, Magic Camp.

For most travelers, the mid-range camps produce the best balance of comfort and authentic experience. The luxury camps are remarkable but expensive; the basic camps are authentic but uncomfortable for less experienced campers.

What to do beyond the basics

A second night in Wadi Rum opens up:

Hot-air balloon flight at sunrise. Several operators run balloon flights over the desert in the early morning hours. Cost is typically 120–180 JOD ($170–250 USD) per person. The flight lasts about an hour and offers the best view of Wadi Rum available. Worth doing once; book through your camp or in advance.

Rock climbing. Wadi Rum is a world-class rock climbing destination, with hundreds of routes on the granite walls. Mostly trad climbing on long routes; a few sport routes have been bolted by Bedouin climbers in recent years. Most travelers won't have the experience for this, but if you do, hire a local guide and bring your own gear.

Walking trails. Several marked routes for travelers who want to hike independently. The most accessible is the Burdah Rock Bridge route (3–4 hours, moderate difficulty). Maps are available at the Visitor Centre; a local guide is recommended for anything more demanding.

Sleeping outside. Many camps offer the option of sleeping under the stars rather than in a tent — mattress and blankets on the sand, away from camp lights. On a moonless night, this is one of the great sleeping experiences anywhere.

The full four-day itinerary

For travelers extending to four days, here's the recommended sequence:

Day 1: Petra Day 1. Standard two-day Petra plan Day 1. Sleep in Wadi Musa.

Day 2: Petra Day 2. Day 2 from the two-day plan. Optionally Petra by Night this evening if Sun–Thu. Sleep in Wadi Musa.

Day 3: Travel + Wadi Rum first night. Morning departure from Wadi Musa (camp transfer or JETT bus). Arrive Wadi Rum by mid-afternoon. Jeep tour, sunset, dinner, evening at camp.

Day 4: Wadi Rum second day + onward. Optional sunrise balloon flight, second jeep tour or hike, lunch at camp, transfer to Aqaba (1 hour) for flight out, or continue to your next destination.

Frequently asked questions

How far apart are Petra and Wadi Rum?
About 120 km, roughly 2 hours by road. The drive south along the Desert Highway is straightforward and well-signed. JETT runs a daily bus connection (22 JOD, ~1.5 hours), and most Wadi Rum Bedouin camps can arrange transfer to or from Petra (typically 30–40 JOD per car).
Should I do Petra first or Wadi Rum first?
Petra first, almost always. The Petra visit is more physically demanding (8–12 km walking per day on uneven ground), so doing it while fresh makes sense. The Wadi Rum visit is mostly seated (jeep tours, Bedouin camp dinners) and works well as a recovery experience. The other reason: Wadi Rum camps offer the best stargazing nights in the region, and you'll appreciate them more after the high-energy Petra days.
How many days for both?
Three days is the minimum to do both justice (2 days Petra, 1 night Wadi Rum), and a real four-day version (2 days Petra, 2 nights Wadi Rum) is meaningfully better. Three days produces a satisfying visit; four days transforms it. The Wadi Rum second night is what gives you a proper jeep tour day, a full evening at camp, and the early morning balloon ride or sunrise hike if you want them.
What about adding the Dead Sea, Amman, or Aqaba?
For a typical seven-day Jordan trip: Amman 1 day, Dead Sea 1 night, Petra 2 days, Wadi Rum 1 night, return to Amman or continue to Aqaba 1 day. The Dead Sea works either between Amman and Petra (heading south) or as a final stop heading back north. Aqaba is mostly skippable unless you're a diver — it's a port city, not a tourist destination.
Can I do Petra and Wadi Rum as a guided tour?
Yes — most tour operators in Amman offer 2-day or 3-day Petra + Wadi Rum packages. Typical pricing: $400–650 USD per person for a 2-day package (Petra by Night included), $550–900 USD for 3 days with Wadi Rum overnight. The premium covers transport, accommodation, guides, and meals. For travelers who want zero logistics, this is the cleanest option; for travelers comfortable with independent travel, doing it yourself is dramatically cheaper.
What should I expect at a Wadi Rum Bedouin camp?
A typical Wadi Rum camp experience: arrive in late afternoon, take a 3–4 hour jeep tour through the major rock formations (Lawrence's Spring, Khazali Canyon, the Mushroom Rock, sunset at Um Sabatah), return to camp for a traditional Zarb dinner (meat and vegetables cooked in an underground oven), evening around the fire, sleep in a Bedouin tent (rugs, blankets, no electricity in cheaper camps), early breakfast, optional sunrise camel ride. Camps range from basic ($30–50 USD/person) to luxury "bubble" tents with private bathrooms ($150–300 USD/person).
