Who built the pyramids

This is the rare ancient mystery that archaeology has actually solved. We've excavated the builders' city, read their graffiti, analyzed their bones, and — since 2013 — read the logbook of a crew that hauled Khufu's stone. Meet them.

Last reviewed June 6, 2026◆9-minute read

Photo: kallerna · CC BY-SA 3.0 · Wikimedia Commons

For most of modern history the question had two competing answers: the slave armies of Herodotus and Hollywood, or — in the louder corners of the 20th century — somebody else entirely, because surely ancient Egyptians couldn't have done this. Both answers dissolved under excavation. Since the 1990s, archaeology south of the Sphinx has uncovered the city of the people who built the pyramids; since 2013, we've been able to read the work diary of one of their crews. The real answer is more interesting than either myth: the pyramids were built by Egypt itself — a young state mobilizing paid, organized, well-fed labor on a scale no one had attempted before.

The city of the builders

South of the Sphinx, beneath what was long dismissed as empty desert, excavations led by Mark Lehner's teams uncovered a purpose-built settlement from the reigns of Khafre and Menkaure: dormitory galleries that slept workers by the dozen, administrative buildings, and an industrial food infrastructure — bakeries with bread molds by the thousand, breweries, and corrals and bone deposits showing cattle, sheep, and goats slaughtered at a rate sufficient to feed thousands of people meat regularly. That detail matters: in the Old Kingdom, regular beef was elite provisioning. The state was not starving its workforce; it was fueling it.

The logistics implied by the site are themselves the achievement. Grain, cattle, fish, and fuel flowed in from estates across Egypt; copper tools were issued, dulled, and re-sharpened by on-site smiths; and the whole apparatus scaled up and down with the building season. The pyramid, seen from the workers' city, looks like what it was — the visible output of the ancient world's most sophisticated supply chain.

The crews and their names

The workforce was organized in named gangs with a structure Egyptologists can read in the monuments themselves, because the crews left graffiti on the stones they set — team names painted in hidden chambers and on blocks never meant to be seen. The names are workplace swagger, four and a half thousand years old: gangs named for their loyalty to the king, marked on quarry blocks and relieving chambers as a record of which team set what. Inside the Great Pyramid's relieving chambers, gang graffiti naming Khufu remains some of the most direct evidence tying the monument to its king.

The picture from the graffiti and the administrative evidence: a permanent core of skilled builders, masons, and surveyors, supplemented by rotating levies of laborers serving the state for stretches — a labor tax paid in muscle, housed and provisioned while serving. Compulsory in the way taxation is compulsory, but compensated, organized, and a world away from chattel slavery.

The bones and the honor

West of the workers' city lies their cemetery — modest tombs for laborers, larger ones for overseers, built within sight of the kings' pyramids. The skeletons tell a hard, human story: spines compressed by lifetimes of load-bearing, arthritis, and — critically — healed fractures and even successful amputations, evidence of real medical care. And the burials themselves are the argument: these people were interred with dignity, with titles like "overseer of the side of the pyramid" recorded for posterity, beside the most sacred ground in Egypt. No society buries slaves like that.

The papyri: a foreman's diary

In 2013, at Wadi al-Jarf on the Red Sea coast, archaeologists found the oldest inscribed papyri ever discovered — including the logbook of an official named Merer, a foreman whose crew spent part of Khufu's 27th regnal year ferrying fine Tura limestone — the casing stone — by boat along canals to the pyramid's harbor at Giza. The diary records the rhythm in administrative deadpan: load at Tura, sail, deliver, return, repeat, with rations accounted. It is as close as we will ever come to standing next to the construction: a middle manager's paperwork from the largest building project in the world, and the final, documentary nail in every alternative theory.

Why the myths persist

The slave narrative persists because it arrived early (Herodotus), was reinforced by religious tradition and cinema, and flatters a modern intuition that monumental ancient labor must have been coerced misery. The lost-civilization and ancient-alien narratives persist for a darker reason: they begin from disbelief that Bronze Age Africans organized this — a premise the workers' city, the graffiti, and Merer's logbook refute in detail. Giza is not an unexplained anomaly. It is one of the best-documented construction projects of the ancient world, and the documentation points one direction: Egyptians built it, on payroll, and were proud enough to sign it.

Seeing the evidence at Giza

The workers' cemetery is a ticketed area on the plateau (the specialist tier in our fees guide), and the excavated city itself is an active research zone rather than a public attraction — but the story is visible everywhere once you know it: in the gang graffiti reproduced in museum displays, in the sheer logistics the site implies, and in the Grand Egyptian Museum's Khufu galleries. Merer's logbook, entry by entry

The Wadi al-Jarf papyri deserve a closer look, because nothing else in archaeology lets you ride along on Khufu's project. Inspector Merer led a phyle — a forty-man boat crew — and his journal records their rotation in administrative deadpan: load Tura limestone at the quarries on the east bank; sail with the current to "Akhet-Khufu" (Horizon of Khufu — the Great Pyramid's own name, in a worker's hand); unload at the harbor by the construction zone; return upriver; repeat, roughly two to three round trips per ten-day week. Entries name his superior — Ankhhaef, Khufu's half-brother, known independently from his famous bust in Boston — log ration deliveries of bread and beer, and date themselves to the 27th year of Khufu's reign, late in the project, when the casing Merer ferried was being fitted high on the nearly finished monument. The find's location seals its strangeness: the papers were archived at a Red Sea harbor the same crews operated, 200 kilometers from Giza — the pyramid workforce doubling as the state's expedition corps, exactly as the rotating-labor model predicts.

What the workers' bodies tell us

The cemetery on the plateau's southern edge adds the biological chapter. The hundreds of worker and overseer burials excavated there since 1990 show skeletons that worked brutally hard — arthritic vertebrae, robust muscle attachments, the wear of lives spent hauling — and were cared for accordingly: healed fractures set straight, evidence read by the excavators as splinting and treatment; at least one amputation survived by years; and burials with the dignity of tomb superstructures, false doors, and titles ("overseer of the side of the pyramid," "director of workers"), facing the monuments they built. Their bones held bread-and-beer staple diets enriched by the cattle the delivery records promise. None of it is the biography of expendable slaves; all of it is the profile of a hard, organized, valued workforce — the same story the bakeries, the rosters, and Merer's matter-of-fact entries tell from their own angles, which is what makes the consensus a consensus.

The answer, finally

So: who built the pyramids? Egyptians — tens of thousands of them across two decades per giant, organized in named crews, fed from state bakeries and ranches, treated when injured, buried with honor in sight of their work, and supervised by middle managers whose paperwork survives. It is the best-documented construction workforce of the ancient world, and the only mystery left in the question is why the myth ever outcompeted an answer this good.

Frequently asked questions

Were the pyramids built by slaves?

No. The excavated workers' city and cemetery at Giza show organized, paid labor: rotating crews housed and fed by the state, supplied with bread, beer, and remarkable quantities of meat, given medical care — healed fractures appear in the skeletons — and buried with honor beside the king's monument. Slaves received none of that. The slave story traces to Herodotus, writing two thousand years after construction, and to later religious tradition; the ground itself says otherwise.

How many workers did it take?

Current estimates, grounded in the workers' village excavations and construction logistics, put the workforce around 20,000-30,000 at peak — a permanent skilled core supplemented by rotating crews — rather than the 100,000 of Herodotus's account. Smaller, better-organized, and better-fed than the legend.

Could the pyramids be built today?

Yes, trivially, with modern machinery — the interesting version of the question is whether we could match the precision with their tools, and experimental archaeology keeps answering yes: copper tools, dolerite pounders, sleds, ramps, levers, and water-slicked tracks reproduce every documented effect. The mystery is not how it was possible but how it was organized.

What about the alternative theories?

Aliens, Atlanteans, and lost civilizations all share one feature: zero supporting evidence at a site that is, ironically, one of the most evidence-rich on Earth. We've found the workers' homes, bakeries, breweries, medical care, graffiti, payrolls, and graves — and in 2013, the harbor logbooks of a crew that delivered Khufu's casing stone.
