HISTORY

Aretas IV

The greatest Nabataean king, who ruled from 9 BCE to 40 CE — and almost certainly the patron behind the Treasury at Petra. A 49-year reign that transformed a wealthy trading post into one of the ancient world's monumental capitals.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 8-minute read · See our editorial standards

Aretas IV Philopatris (literally "Aretas, lover of his people") is the historical figure most directly associated with the Petra that survives today. His 49-year reign from approximately 9 BCE to 40 CE coincided with the Nabataean kingdom's peak — maximum territorial extent, peak trade wealth, and the burst of monumental construction that produced the Treasury, the Monastery, the major Royal Tombs, and most of the other carved facades that define Petra in the modern imagination. For travelers visiting Petra without historical context, knowing about Aretas IV transforms what you're looking at: nearly every major monument you see was probably commissioned during his lifetime.

This page covers what we know (and don't know) about Aretas IV, his political and military reign, the construction program at Petra, his complicated relationship with Rome, and his connection to one of the better-attested episodes in the New Testament — the escape of the apostle Paul from Damascus. The framing throughout: this is the figure standing behind the visible Petra.

The historical sources

Nabataean kings left remarkably few inscriptions identifying themselves by name with specific buildings, which is the main reason individual monuments at Petra can't always be definitively attributed. What we know about Aretas IV comes from several converging sources:

Greek and Latin historians. The Jewish historian Josephus (writing in the late 1st century CE) discusses Aretas IV in connection with his daughter's marriage to Herod Antipas, the Roman tetrarch of Galilee. The Greek geographer Strabo (writing around 7 BCE - 23 CE, during Aretas's reign) describes the Nabataean kingdom favorably. Roman historians mention him in passing in the context of Roman-Nabataean diplomacy.

Coinage. Aretas IV minted extensive coinage with Nabataean inscriptions naming him explicitly. The coins establish his reign dates with high precision and confirm his title "Philopatris."

Inscriptions. A handful of dedicatory inscriptions from Petra, Hegra (the Nabataean city in modern Saudi Arabia), and other sites name Aretas IV in dating formulas ("in the year X of Aretas the king") — useful for absolute chronology even when they don't identify specific buildings.

The New Testament. Paul's letter to the Corinthians (2 Corinthians 11:32-33) mentions "the governor under King Aretas" guarding Damascus while Paul escaped over the city walls in a basket. This places Aretas IV briefly controlling Damascus around 36-39 CE — the only New Testament reference to a named Nabataean ruler.

Archaeology. The systematic excavation of Petra (continuing today) has filled in details that the textual record doesn't supply — construction chronologies, demographic data, the scale of the water management infrastructure.

The reign

Aretas IV came to power around 9 BCE under unclear circumstances. He was likely the son of the previous king (Obodas III, ruled circa 30-9 BCE) or possibly a more distant relative; the Nabataean royal succession isn't fully documented. The Roman emperor Augustus initially refused to recognize Aretas's accession — the Romans expected confirmation rights over client kings — but eventually accepted him after several years of diplomatic friction.

The 49-year reign that followed (9 BCE to 40 CE) was the longest of any Nabataean king and coincided with the peak of the kingdom's wealth and influence:

Trade prosperity. The Nabataeans controlled the major caravan routes carrying frankincense, myrrh, and spices from southern Arabia (modern Yemen and Oman) north to Mediterranean ports. Under Aretas IV, this trade reached peak volume, making the kingdom enormously wealthy. The frankincense was used in religious ritual across the Roman Empire and the Hellenistic East; the Nabataeans were the indispensable middleman.

Territorial extent. The Nabataean kingdom under Aretas IV stretched from Damascus in the north (briefly, around 36-39 CE) to Hegra in the south (now Mada\'in Salih in Saudi Arabia, a site that preserves Nabataean monuments in similar style to Petra). The eastern frontier extended into the Hauran (modern southern Syria); the western frontier reached the Sinai Peninsula.

Political sophistication. Aretas IV maintained diplomatic relations with both Rome and the Parthian Empire (Rome's eastern rival), conducted independent military campaigns (notably against Herod Antipas in 36 CE), and ran a sophisticated bureaucracy at home. He married his daughter Phasaelis to Herod Antipas around 7 BCE, then went to war with Herod after he divorced her around 36 CE — winning the war but eventually backing off from territorial expansion due to Roman pressure.

The construction program at Petra

The visible Petra is largely a product of Aretas IV's reign. Earlier Nabataean kings had built impressive structures, but the burst of monumental construction that produced the carved facades most travelers come to see falls primarily in the period from roughly 1 BCE to 40 CE — squarely within Aretas IV's reign.

The Treasury (Al-Khazneh). The architectural style, the carved details, and the scale all point to construction during Aretas IV's reign. The leading scholarly interpretation is that the Treasury was built as his royal tomb or as a major dynastic memorial — possibly for his predecessor Obodas III, possibly for himself, possibly for the broader royal lineage. The Hellenistic-influenced architectural elements (broken pediment, columned facade, sculptural niches) reflect the Nabataean kingdom's deep cultural engagement with Greek and Roman aesthetic traditions.

The Monastery (Ad-Deir). Mid-1st century CE, also probably commissioned during the late Aretas IV reign or shortly after. The largest carved facade at Petra (48 meters wide, 47 meters tall), originally a religious site rather than a tomb.

The major Royal Tombs. The Urn Tomb, the Silk Tomb, the Corinthian Tomb, and the Palace Tomb were all likely carved during the Aretas IV period or his successors. The carved facades cluster on the eastern cliff above the lower city, forming a deliberate royal-necropolis display.

The colonnaded street and civic infrastructure. The main paved street of Petra (the "cardo maximus" by analogy with Roman city planning) was constructed or expanded during Aretas IV's reign. The Great Temple complex, Qasr al-Bint, and the broader civic infrastructure follow the same pattern.

The water management system. The dams, channels, cisterns, and underground conduits that made Petra liveable in the desert reached their peak sophistication during Aretas IV's reign. See Nabataean water management for the detail on how this engineering enabled the city.

The Damascus episode and Paul

One of the few specific historical events associated with Aretas IV outside Nabataean sources comes from the New Testament. In 2 Corinthians 11:32-33, Paul writes: "In Damascus the governor under King Aretas guarded the city of the Damascenes in order to seize me, but I was let down in a basket through a window in the wall and escaped from his hands."

This places Aretas IV briefly controlling Damascus around 36-39 CE — the only documented period when a Nabataean king held the city. The political backstory is complex: after Aretas's daughter Phasaelis fled Herod Antipas (her husband, who divorced her to marry Herodias), Aretas IV attacked Herod and defeated him in battle in 36 CE. The Roman emperor Tiberius ordered Roman intervention to punish Aretas, but Tiberius died in 37 CE before the campaign was launched. Caligula, the new emperor, withdrew the order. During this brief window of Roman inattention, Aretas IV apparently extended Nabataean control to Damascus, leading to the situation Paul describes.

The episode is historically significant because it provides the only firmly-dateable cross-reference between Nabataean and Roman/Christian history for Aretas IV's reign. It also illustrates the political reach of the late Aretas-era Nabataean kingdom.

The end

Aretas IV died in 40 CE after a 49-year reign — extraordinarily long by ancient-world standards. He was succeeded by his son Malichus II, who ruled until 70 CE. The kingdom continued for another 65 years under Malichus II and then Rabbel II (70-106 CE), but the great construction era was already past by the time Aretas IV died. His successors maintained the trade routes and the city, but didn't commission new monumental architecture on the scale of the Aretas IV period.

The Roman annexation in 106 CE, under Emperor Trajan, formally ended the independent Nabataean kingdom. Petra continued as a major Roman provincial city for another 200 years and remained populated through the Byzantine period before declining after the earthquakes of 363 CE and 551 CE. But the Petra that survives in the imagination — the carved facades, the monumental tombs, the Treasury at the end of the Siq — is fundamentally the Petra Aretas IV built.

Where to see Aretas IV today

Nearly everywhere at Petra. Specifically:

  • The Treasury (Al-Khazneh) — almost certainly his commission, possibly his actual tomb
  • The Monastery (Ad-Deir) — late-period work that grew out of the Aretas IV building program
  • The major Royal Tombs — the royal necropolis his reign created
  • The colonnaded street and lower city civic core — the urban infrastructure he expanded
  • The Jordan Museum in Amman — the best collection of Aretas IV coins and inscriptional material, useful for visual context

For visitors who want a deeper sense of the figure behind the architecture, the small museum at the Petra Visitor Centre includes contextual material on the Nabataean kings.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Aretas IV?
The greatest Nabataean king, who ruled from approximately 9 BCE to 40 CE — a 49-year reign that coincided with Petra's peak. Under his rule the Nabataean kingdom reached its maximum territorial extent (from Damascus in the north to the Hejaz in the south), Petra was transformed from a wealthy trading post into a monumental ceremonial capital, and the iconic Treasury (Al-Khazneh) was almost certainly commissioned and completed. He's the king behind the Petra we visit today.
Did Aretas IV actually build the Treasury?
Almost certainly yes, though the inscription evidence is indirect. The Treasury's architectural style, scale, and the broader pattern of Aretas-era construction at Petra all point to his reign as the commissioning period (roughly 9 BCE to 40 CE). The current scholarly consensus is that the Treasury was built as either his royal tomb or a major dynastic memorial. The 1st-century-CE timing fits the carved decoration, the architectural details, and the wealth required for such a project.
What was the kingdom like under Aretas?
Wealthy, sophisticated, and at the peak of its political reach. The Nabataeans controlled the incense and spice trade routes from Arabia to the Mediterranean, which generated extraordinary wealth — Petra at this period was probably one of the richest cities per capita in the ancient world. The Aretas-era kingdom built monumental religious architecture (the Khazneh, the Monastery, the major Royal Tombs), maintained a professional army, conducted independent diplomacy with Rome and Parthia, and ran a sophisticated water management infrastructure that supported a population of perhaps 20,000-30,000 in the desert.
Why is he called Aretas IV and not just Aretas?
Four Nabataean kings named Aretas (Hellenized form of the Nabataean name Haritat) ruled in succession from the 2nd century BCE through the 1st century CE. The historical record distinguishes them by numbers: Aretas I (around 168 BCE), Aretas II (around 100 BCE), Aretas III (around 84-62 BCE, who briefly held Damascus), and Aretas IV Philopatris (9 BCE-40 CE). Aretas IV's full title was "Aretas, lover of his people" — Philopatris in Greek.
What happened after he died?
The kingdom continued for another 65 years before Roman annexation in 106 CE. Aretas IV was succeeded by his son Malichus II (40-70 CE), then by Rabbel II (70-106 CE), the last independent Nabataean king. Construction at Petra continued under both successors but at a reduced pace — the monumental peak was already past. When Rabbel II died, the Roman emperor Trajan annexed the kingdom, converting it into the Roman province of Arabia Petraea. The Nabataean royal line ended, though the population and culture continued under Roman rule.
