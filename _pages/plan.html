Plan your Tbilisi visit

Georgia is one of the easiest countries in the region to visit — visa-free for most travelers, affordable, safe, and welcoming. The practical layer below clears the remaining questions before you land.

Last reviewed: June 7, 2026

The headline is that the barriers are low. Citizens of more than ninety countries enter visa-free for up to a year — one of the most generous policies anywhere — and arrival is uncomplicated: no registration, no paperwork, a stamp and you're in. The currency is the lari, cards work nearly everywhere in the capital, the city is statistically among the safer capitals you can visit, and the tourism infrastructure has matured fast without losing the improvisational warmth that makes Georgia Georgia. What follows is the practical layer — when to come, what things cost, and the handful of local customs worth knowing in advance.

When to come

Tbilisi's sweet spots are May–June and September–October: warm, mostly dry, terraces open, mountains accessible, and the city at its sociable best. October adds the rtveli grape harvest in Kakheti and, most years, the Tbilisoba city festival. July and Augustare hot — afternoons regularly uncomfortable in the gorge the Old Town sits in — and locals respond by living at night, which has its own appeal if you adopt the rhythm. Winter is cold, occasionally snowy, and quietly wonderful: the baths make sense, prices drop, Gudauri's ski season runs, and the supra moves indoors where it belongs. The trade-off is mountain day-trip reliability, covered in the day trips cluster. The full month-by-month breakdown lives on the season page below.

Money, in one honest paragraph

Tbilisi remains genuinely affordable by European standards, though it's no longer the rounding-error-cheap destination of a decade ago. Eating out is the standout value — a generous khinkali dinner with wine costs what a starter does in Western Europe — and transport is almost free in practice: city buses and the metro cost small change, and Bolt rides across the center rarely strain anyone's budget. Accommodation spans guesthouse-cheap to design-hotel-reasonable. Tipping runs modestly: many restaurants add a service charge to the bill (check before doubling up), and rounding up or leaving around ten percent where it isn't added is appreciated, never demanded. Exact current figures — and the ATM and currency-conversion traps — live on the money page, where they're re-verified each review pass.

Getting around and staying connected

Inside the city, your tools are your feet, the two-line metro, the modern bus fleet, and Bolt — the ride-hailing app that has effectively replaced street taxis for visitors and removed the haggling that used to define them. Contactless bank cards tap directly onto buses and metro gates, so there's no ticket ritual to learn. For connectivity, local SIMs from the major operators (Magti, Silknet/Geocell brands) are cheap and sold at the airport and in every district, and eSIM activation has become the path of least resistance for short stays — the dedicated page (coming to this cluster) compares current packages.

Culture notes that earn their space

Three things smooth a first visit. Churches are living places of worship, not museums: shoulders and knees covered, headscarves for women inside (usually provided at the door), and quiet during services. Hospitality is structural — if you're invited to eat or drink, refusing outright can land harder than you intend; accepting a token amount is the graceful out. The supra, Georgia's formal feast with its tamada toastmaster, has real rules — the food and wine cluster explains them — but the only rule a guest must know is that toasts are drunk with wine, never beer, and never half-heartedly.

One modern note: Tbilisi sees periodic political demonstrations, overwhelmingly centered on Rustaveli Avenue near Parliament. They're a feature of civic life, rarely affect visitors beyond road closures, and the safety page below covers how to read the situation like a local rather than a headline.

The practical essentials

Visas and entry. Georgia is one of the easiest countries in the region to enter: citizens of around a hundred countries — the EU, UK, US, Canada, Australia, the Gulf, and many more — get a full year visa-free, simply by arriving. Always confirm your own nationality against the official list before you fly, but for most readers the border is a formality. Connectivity. Tourist SIMs from the operators (Magti, Geocell, Cellfie) are cheap and sold at the airport and in city shops; eSIM activation is the lowest-friction option for short stays, and wifi is near-universal in cafes and hotels. Money is covered in depth on the money page — short version: the lari, cards almost everywhere, a cash float for markets and the bath.

Language. Georgian is its own language family with its own beautiful, non-Latin alphabet — you will not decode signs by guessing. Russian is widely understood, especially by older people; English is common among younger Tbilisians and ubiquitous in tourism. Learning to read a few letters and saying gamarjoba (hello) and madloba (thank you) goes a genuinely long way. Power is European 220V with the standard two-pin (Type C/F) sockets, so EU travelers need nothing and others need a simple adapter.

Etiquette and rhythm. Tbilisi runs late — dinner after nine, nightlife later — and rewards an unhurried pace. Churches expect covered shoulders and knees and headscarves for women, with wraps usually provided at the door. Hospitality toward guests is close to a national value, so accept the offered coffee, fruit, or chacha graciously. The three pages below go deep on the questions that actually move a trip — when to come, what it costs, and whether it's safe — and the answers are reassuring on all three counts.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a visa?

Probably not. Citizens of more than ninety countries — including the US, UK, EU member states, Canada, Australia, and most of the wider region — can enter Georgia visa-free and stay up to a full year. Other nationalities may qualify via the 90-day rule for holders of valid US, EU/Schengen, UK, Canadian, Japanese, or GCC visas and residence permits, or through the official e-visa portal at evisa.gov.ge. Rules change; verify against official sources close to travel.

What currency is used, and do cards work?

The Georgian lari (GEL). Card acceptance in Tbilisi is excellent — contactless works in supermarkets, restaurants, city buses, and the metro — but keep some cash for markets, small guesthouses, and rural day-trip stops. ATMs are everywhere in the center; the money page covers fees and the dynamic-currency-conversion trap.

Is English widely spoken?

In tourism, yes — hotels, tours, and central restaurants operate comfortably in English, and younger Georgians often speak it well. Russian remains the most common second language among older generations. Outside the tourist core, translation apps bridge the gaps, and Georgians are famously patient with attempts at “gamarjoba” (hello) and “madloba” (thank you).

What should I pack that people forget?

Shoes with real grip — Old Town cobbles and marble-smooth sidewalks are slippery even dry. A scarf or shawl for church visits. Layers in spring and autumn, when days and evenings can be twenty degrees apart. Swimwear is not needed for the sulfur baths (private rooms are bathed nude or in underwear; the page explains), but flip-flops are welcome.

Is tap water drinkable?

Tbilisi tap water comes from mountain sources and is generally considered safe to drink; most visitors drink it without issue. Bottled and famous Georgian mineral waters (Borjomi above all) are cheap and everywhere if you prefer.
