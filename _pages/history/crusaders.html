HISTORY

The Crusader interlude

Between 1115 and 1189, European Crusaders held the Petra region as a southern frontier outpost of the Kingdom of Jerusalem. Two ruined castles survive — Al-Wu'ayrah and Al-Habis — and they're one of the most overlooked elements of Petra's historical layers.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 6-minute read · See our editorial standards

The Crusader period at Petra is a small but distinctive episode in the site's long post-Nabataean history. For roughly 75 years between 1115 and 1189, European Crusader forces occupied the broader Petra region as part of the southern frontier of the Kingdom of Jerusalem. They built two castles in the area, established small garrisons, and used the region's geography for strategic defense against the Egyptian forces of the Fatimid caliphate and later Saladin's Ayyubid empire. The episode ended with Saladin's reconquest of the Levant after the Battle of Hattin (1187) and the broader Crusader retreat from the region.

For visitors interested in the layered history of Petra, the Crusader period is a useful reminder that the site wasn't simply abandoned between the Nabataean-Roman-Byzantine centuries and the modern era. People kept coming through, fortifying defensible positions, and using the geography for whatever purposes their era required. This page covers what happened during the Crusader interlude and what survives today.

The setting

By the early 12th century when the Crusaders arrived, Petra had been declining for centuries. The Byzantine Christian community of the 5th-6th centuries had largely dispersed; the 7th-century Arab conquest had brought political change but accelerated the broader regional decline; the 8th and 9th centuries saw the city's population reduced to small clusters of Bedouin and rural settlers. The monumental architecture was abandoned, the trade routes were dead, the urban infrastructure was crumbling.

The First Crusade (1095-1099) established the Kingdom of Jerusalem and several smaller Crusader states across the Levant. The Kingdom's territory eventually extended from Beirut in the north through Jerusalem to the Gulf of Aqaba in the south — encompassing the Petra region as the southern frontier of the Crusader state. Petra's geographic position on the routes between Jerusalem, Egypt, and the Red Sea made it strategically relevant despite the long-decayed city itself.

The castles

Al-Wu'ayrah (Crusader Castle)

The larger and better-preserved of the two Crusader castles at Petra. Al-Wu'ayrah sits on a hilltop about 1 km north of central Petra, between the Wadi Musa town area and Little Petra. The castle was built around 1115-1116 under the orders of Baldwin I, the first Crusader king of Jerusalem, as part of a network of southern frontier fortifications.

The surviving structures include:

  • Defensive curtain walls following the contours of the hilltop
  • Foundations of an interior keep (the main residential structure)
  • The remains of a small chapel, identifiable by the apse foundation
  • Storage rooms and small outbuildings
  • A water cistern (essential for the relatively waterless hilltop position)
  • Access stairs and entry passages

The total fortified area is modest — perhaps 1,500-2,000 square meters — reflecting the small garrison size (probably 50-100 troops during peak occupation). The castle was never intended as a major military base; it functioned as a observation post, customs station, and emergency refuge.

Al-Habis Castle

The smaller second castle sits on a steep rocky peak directly within the Petra basin, just south of Qasr al-Bint and Wadi Musa. The position is dramatic — a near-vertical climb up the rock face from the lower city — and the surviving structures are correspondingly modest: small fortified walls along the summit, foundations of internal structures, evidence of limited residential use.

Al-Habis served as a forward observation post and potential emergency refuge, more than as a sustained garrison position. The difficulty of accessing the summit (requiring climbs that limit both supply and large-force defense) made it strategically limited even by Crusader-period standards.

Other minor Crusader sites

A handful of smaller fortified positions and watchtowers existed across the broader region but have left only fragmentary remains. Most weren't permanent garrisons but seasonal or emergency positions.

What the Crusaders did

The Crusader presence at Petra functioned at three levels:

Frontier defense. The main strategic role. The castles monitored movement through the southern Levantine corridor — Egyptian raids north toward Jerusalem, Arabian trade caravans, pilgrim movements toward the Red Sea. The garrisons couldn't have stopped a major military force but provided warning and limited delay against smaller threats.

Trade route taxation. The limited remaining trade through the region (mostly small-scale local commerce by this period, plus occasional Hajj pilgrim traffic moving toward Mecca via the Red Sea coast) was taxed at the Crusader positions. The revenue was modest but contributed to the broader Crusader state's frontier income.

Pilgrim protection. Christian pilgrimage to biblical sites continued throughout the Crusader period, including pilgrim traffic moving through the Petra region en route to or from Mount Sinai and other southern destinations. The Crusader garrisons provided protection to these pilgrims against bandits and hostile local forces.

The Bedouin and local interactions

The Crusader presence intersected with the local Bedouin and rural populations in complicated ways. Some Bedouin tribes allied with the Crusaders for trade and protection purposes; others remained hostile; most maintained pragmatic neutrality, providing services (water, food, intelligence) to whoever paid for them at any given moment.

The Bedouin communities living in and around Petra during this period — predecessors of the modern Bdoul community though not necessarily continuous in lineage — provided much of the local labor and intelligence supporting the Crusader garrison operations. The relationship was transactional rather than ideological; the Bedouin didn't share the Crusaders' religious motivations but engaged with them as the temporary local power.

The end

The Crusader period at Petra ended within the broader collapse of Crusader power in the southern Levant during the 1180s. The major turning points:

Saladin's rise (1171-1187). The Kurdish military commander Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub — known to history as Saladin — established the Ayyubid sultanate over Egypt by 1171 and over Syria by 1183. His unified Muslim state had the military resources to systematically challenge Crusader holdings across the Levant.

The Battle of Hattin (July 4, 1187). Saladin's decisive victory over the main Crusader field army at Hattin in Galilee. The Crusader military strength was essentially destroyed in a single day; the subsequent collapse of garrison after garrison across the Levant was inevitable.

The fall of Jerusalem (October 2, 1187). Saladin's recapture of Jerusalem ended the central Crusader political authority. Outlying positions like Petra-area castles became militarily and politically untenable.

The fall of Al-Wu'ayrah (1189). Saladin's forces took Al-Wu'ayrah Castle in 1189, about two years after Hattin. Al-Habis fell around the same time. The Crusader period at Petra ended cleanly with these surrenders; the garrisons were typically allowed to withdraw under terms rather than being slaughtered.

What came after

After 1189, Petra and the surrounding region returned to the slow post-Byzantine decline that the Crusader presence had briefly interrupted. The Bedouin population continued; small-scale agriculture and trade continued at reduced volume; the major monuments continued their long slow weathering. The Crusader castles were not reused at scale by subsequent occupants — Saladin's forces dismantled the fortifications partially to prevent their use as future Crusader bases.

The next significant period of attention to Petra would be the 19th-century European rediscovery (see Burckhardt and the 19th-century explorers) — a 600-year gap during which the Petra region was essentially absent from international historical attention.

Visiting the Crusader sites

Al-Wu'ayrah Castle

Location: On the hilltop accessed via the road north from Wadi Musa toward Little Petra. The castle is signposted but not heavily promoted; many visitors miss it entirely.

Access: Short drive from Wadi Musa (10 minutes) or 30-minute walk. A 10-15 minute hike up from the parking area reaches the castle ruins.

Cost: Free to visit; the site is on the standard Petra access permit area but not specifically ticketed.

Time to budget: 30-45 minutes to walk through the ruins and consider the position. Combine with a Little Petra visit for an efficient half-day off-the-main-Petra-route trip.

Al-Habis Castle

Location: On the rocky peak in the Petra basin, accessed from near Qasr al-Bint in the lower city.

Access: Requires a steep climb up the rock face — about 20-30 minutes of moderate scrambling. Not recommended for visitors uncomfortable with exposed climbing.

Cost: Free; within the regular Petra entry.

Time to budget: 60-90 minutes round trip from the lower city. The view from the summit is spectacular even setting aside the Crusader ruins.

Frequently asked questions

Were Crusaders actually at Petra?
Yes, briefly. European Crusader forces occupied Petra and the surrounding region from approximately 1115 to 1189, about 75 years. They built two castles in the area — Al-Wu'ayrah on the hill north of central Petra, and Al-Habis on a small peak within the Petra basin itself — and used the region as part of their frontier defense system against Egyptian forces under Saladin. The Crusader presence ended after Saladin's victories culminating in the Battle of Hattin (1187) and his subsequent reconquest of the Levant.
Can I see the Crusader castles today?
Yes, both partially. Al-Wu'ayrah Castle sits on a hilltop about 1 km north of central Petra, accessible via a short hike from Wadi Musa or Little Petra. The ruins include defensive walls, foundations of internal buildings, and a small chapel, though the structures are heavily decayed. Al-Habis Castle on the rocky peak within Petra is more difficult to reach (requires climbing up the rock face from the lower city) and even more fragmentary. Neither is on the standard Petra tourist route; visitors specifically interested in Crusader history seek them out.
Why were Crusaders in such a remote location?
Strategic position on the route between the Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem and the Red Sea. The Kingdom controlled the Levant from Jerusalem and various coastal cities; the southern frontier extended through Petra-area territory toward the Gulf of Aqaba. The castles guarded against Egyptian counter-attacks from the south and protected pilgrimage and trade routes through the region. The location was also a useful customs post for taxing the limited remaining trade through what had once been Petra's main caravan routes.
What was the Crusader period like for Petra?
A small interlude in the longer post-Byzantine decline. By the time the Crusaders arrived (around 1115), Petra had been substantially declined for centuries — population was probably under 5,000, the city's monumental architecture was largely abandoned, and the Nabataean trade routes were essentially dead. The Crusaders didn't significantly rebuild or repopulate the area; they used the existing geography (defensible cliffs, the existing road network, the Bedouin population for limited support) to build their fortified positions and otherwise left the broader area to its slow decline.
Did the Crusaders affect Petra's monuments?
Minimally. The Crusaders built new fortifications on previously unbuilt sites (the hilltops where the castles sit) rather than modifying or reusing the Nabataean monuments in the main valley. Some evidence suggests Crusader-era use of certain carved caves and tombs for shelter or storage, but no major intervention in the Treasury, Monastery, Royal Tombs, or other headline monuments. The Crusader presence is more historically interesting than architecturally consequential for Petra itself.
