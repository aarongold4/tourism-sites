Practical

Plan Your Trip

Visas, denars, buses, burek — the practical layer, fact-checked so you don't have to.

North Macedonia is an easy country to travel — easier than its low profile suggests. Entry is visa-free for most Western travelers, English is widely spoken by anyone under forty, cards work in cities, and the intercity bus network is genuinely good. The friction points are small and predictable; this cluster exists to remove them in advance.

The essentials in one pass

Getting there

Skopje International Airport (SKP) is the main gateway, heavily served by low-cost carriers — Wizz Air alone connects dozens of European cities, often at fares under €50. Ohrid's small St. Paul the Apostle Airport (OHD) adds seasonal routes. Overland, buses link Skopje with Sofia, Thessaloniki, Tirana, Pristina and Belgrade; there is no useful international passenger rail at present.

Getting around

Intercity buses are the workhorse — frequent, cheap (Skopje–Ohrid around €8–14) and reliable; note the small station entry fee (about 50 denars) at major terminals. Domestic rail is sparse and slow; treat it as a curiosity, not transport. Rental cars run €25–35 a day and unlock the wine country, monasteries and national parks — roads are decent, mountain driving slow, and an International Driving Permit is recommended alongside your license.

Money

The currency is the Macedonian denar (MKD), pegged to the euro at roughly 61.5 MKD = €1 for over two decades — so prices are stable and mental math is easy (100 MKD ≈ €1.60). Cards are widely accepted in cities and hotels; carry cash for bazaar stalls, village tavernas, monasteries and rural fuel stops. ATMs are everywhere in towns; euros are often quoted for hotels but pay denars day-to-day.

Safety and health

North Macedonia is a low-crime destination by any European measure; standard pickpocket awareness in crowded bazaar areas is the extent of normal precaution. Tap water is safe in most of the country including Skopje and Ohrid. The genuine hazards are mundane: aggressive driving habits, summer heat in the Vardar valley, and underestimating mountain weather on hikes.

When to come

May–June and September–October are the all-around sweet spots — warm, green or golden, uncrowded. July–August is lake season (and festival season in Ohrid) at the cost of heat and the year's peak crowds. December–March is for Mavrovo skiing and moody, cheap city breaks.

Eating

The food is the Balkans at its most generous: tavče gravče (baked beans in a skillet, the national dish), ajvar in autumn, burek for breakfast, grilled meats everywhere, Ohrid trout (choose farmed — the wild lake population is protected), shopska salad under a snowfall of sirenje cheese, and Vranec red to wash it down. Eating out is startlingly cheap — a full taverna dinner with wine rarely passes €15 a head.

Connectivity and the practical pocket

SIM and eSIM: local prepaid SIMs from the main operators are cheap and sold with passport at official shops; travel eSIMs covering North Macedonia now cost a few euros per GB and skip the queue — note the country sits outside EU roaming, so EU plans typically charge here. Wifi is fast and free in virtually every café and hotel. Plugs: standard European two-pin (Type C/F), 230V — UK and US visitors need only the usual adapter. Time: Central European Time, with EU summer-time changes. Tap water: good in the cities; mountain spring fountains are a point of local pride. Emergencies: 112 nationwide, with pharmacies plentiful and capable for the small stuff.

What to pack, honestly

The list is short and specific: real walking shoes (Ohrid\'s polished cobbles and fortress climbs are the trip\'s main terrain), water shoes for the gravel beaches, a modesty layer for churches and mosques (covered shoulders/knees; scarves often provided), a genuine sun kit June–September (the valley heat and shadeless ridges mean it), one warm layer year-round for lake evenings and any mountain plan, and a cash habit — small denar notes solve more daily friction than any gadget. Winter adds proper footwear and, for drivers, the legal winter-tire question. Everything forgettable is buyable in Skopje for less than at home.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a visa for North Macedonia?

Citizens of the EU, UK, US, Canada, Australia and many other countries enter visa-free for short stays — typically up to 90 days. Check current rules for your passport before travel, and ensure six months of passport validity.

What currency does North Macedonia use?

The Macedonian denar (MKD), long pegged at roughly 61.5 to the euro. Cards work widely in cities; keep cash for markets, villages and monasteries.

Is English spoken in North Macedonia?

Widely among younger people and in tourism — hotels, restaurants and tours in Skopje and Ohrid operate comfortably in English. A few Macedonian pleasantries go a long way in villages.

Is North Macedonia in the EU or Schengen?

No — it is an EU candidate country but not a member, and not in Schengen. Time spent here does not count against your Schengen 90/180 allowance, which makes it a favorite reset stop for long-term travelers.

Will my EU roaming plan work in North Macedonia?

Usually not at EU rates — the country is outside the EU roaming zone, so most plans charge extra here. The fixes are a cheap local prepaid SIM (passport required at official shops) or a travel eSIM activated before arrival.

What power plugs does North Macedonia use?

Standard European Type C/F two-pin sockets at 230V — the same as the rest of continental Europe. UK, US and other travelers need only their usual European adapter.
