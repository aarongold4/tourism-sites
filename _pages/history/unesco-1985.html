HISTORY

UNESCO World Heritage designation

On December 6, 1985, UNESCO inscribed Petra on the World Heritage List — the moment after which Petra became formally an internationally protected cultural site. Here's what changed, what it means, and why the designation matters for the site you visit today.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 6-minute read · See our editorial standards

Petra's December 6, 1985 inscription as a UNESCO World Heritage Site is one of the most consequential events in the site's modern history. The designation formalized Petra's status as an internationally significant cultural property, brought systematic protection frameworks to the site, and catalyzed the broader changes that have shaped how visitors experience Petra today. The 1985 inscription is less famous than the 2007 New Seven Wonders vote but more institutionally significant — UNESCO World Heritage status carries weight that the popular Seven Wonders designation doesn't.

This page covers what the inscription actually involved, what changed at Petra in the years following 1985, and how the UNESCO framework continues to shape the site's management today. The framing throughout: the institutional foundation underlying the visible modern Petra is the 1985 designation more than any other single event.

The inscription itself

UNESCO's World Heritage Committee inscribed Petra during its 9th session, held in Paris from December 2-6, 1985. The inscription added Petra to the World Heritage List under several criteria from the UNESCO framework:

Criterion (i): Sites that "represent a masterpiece of human creative genius." The Treasury, the Monastery, and the broader Nabataean rock-cut architecture qualify directly.

Criterion (iii): Sites that "bear a unique or at least exceptional testimony to a cultural tradition or to a civilization which is living or which has disappeared." The Nabataean kingdom is precisely such a vanished civilization preserved in its monumental built heritage.

Criterion (iv): Sites that "are an outstanding example of a type of building, architectural or technological ensemble or landscape which illustrates a significant stage in human history." Petra's combined rock-cut and free-standing architecture, with the integrated water management system, illustrates the Nabataean cultural synthesis at a specific historical moment.

The inscription text cited Petra's "outstanding universal value" — UNESCO's foundational concept for World Heritage status, meaning cultural importance transcending national or regional significance. The designation placed Petra among the cohort of sites considered globally important for shared human heritage.

What UNESCO World Heritage status involves

What it does

  • International recognition. Formal acknowledgment that the site has outstanding universal value, making it part of humanity's shared cultural heritage rather than purely a national asset.
  • Protection obligations. The host country commits to preserving the site according to international conservation standards.
  • Technical assistance. UNESCO provides expertise on conservation, management, and visitor infrastructure design.
  • Financial assistance. Limited UNESCO funding for specific conservation projects, and increased access to international funding from other sources (governments, foundations, academic institutions) drawn to World Heritage sites.
  • Monitoring framework. Periodic UNESCO review of the site's condition and management practices.
  • Tourism credibility. The World Heritage label provides international tourism marketing value and contributes to visitor confidence.

What it doesn't do

  • Transfer ownership. The site remains entirely under Jordanian property law and sovereignty.
  • Grant immunity. UNESCO can recommend protection but cannot directly enforce conservation against host country decisions.
  • Provide major funding. Direct UNESCO funding for protection is relatively modest; most conservation work funds come from national budgets and international donors.
  • Prevent visitor access. World Heritage status doesn't restrict visitation; it shapes how visitation is managed.

What changed at Petra after 1985

The Bdoul resettlement

The most controversial of the post-1985 changes. The Bedouin community (the Bdoul) who had lived in Petra's caves and rock-cut tombs for centuries were resettled to a new village called Umm Sayhoun, about 3 km north of the Visitor Centre. The resettlement happened in coordination with the World Heritage inscription — UNESCO requirements for reducing continuous habitation impact on the monuments were a major driver, though Jordanian government priorities were also involved.

The resettlement was complicated and not universally welcomed. Most Bdoul families accepted the move (modern housing, infrastructure, continued tourism work access); some resisted; the cultural cost of leaving ancestral living spaces remains acknowledged. See the Bdoul page for the full context.

The Petra Archaeological Park

The formal establishment of the Petra Archaeological Park provided regulatory framework for site protection. Boundaries were defined, ticketing systems established, visitor flow patterns designed. The Park is now administered through the Petra Development and Tourism Region Authority (PDTRA), which coordinates conservation, visitor management, and economic development for the broader area.

The Visitor Centre and infrastructure

The modern Visitor Centre at the Petra entrance was developed in the years following the 1985 inscription. The complex includes:

  • Ticketing and tour-booking services
  • The Petra Museum with archaeological exhibits
  • Restrooms and basic visitor services
  • Restaurant and shop facilities
  • Information and signage in multiple languages

The Visitor Centre represents the institutional commitment to professional visitor management that the World Heritage status required.

Trail markings and conservation infrastructure

The post-1985 period included systematic trail marking, conservation work on the most-visited monuments, dam reconstruction (notably the Siq dam, rebuilt after the 1963 flash flood that killed 23 French tourists), and ongoing structural stabilization of damaged structures. The trail network — clearly marked routes from the entrance through the major monuments — was designed to balance visitor access with monument protection.

International archaeological partnerships

The World Heritage framework made international archaeological partnerships easier to organize. The major modern excavation projects (Brown University's Great Temple work, ACOR's Petra Church discovery, the various German and French projects) all benefited from the institutional framework the UNESCO designation provided. See modern archaeology at Petra for the detail.

Tourism growth

Petra's annual visitor numbers grew substantially after 1985. Pre-inscription visitation was modest (estimated 50,000-100,000 annually); by the mid-1990s, the site was hosting 200,000-300,000 annually; by 2010 (after the 2007 New Seven Wonders boost), the number exceeded 1 million annually. The World Heritage status contributed significantly to this growth, though it wasn't the only factor.

The ongoing protection framework

The PDTRA

The Petra Development and Tourism Region Authority is the primary management body for the broader Petra region. Established in 2009 to coordinate the previously fragmented authority structure, the PDTRA oversees:

  • Conservation work and monument stabilization
  • Visitor management and ticketing
  • Local community engagement (particularly with the Bdoul community)
  • Tourism development planning for Wadi Musa and surrounding areas
  • International partnership coordination

UNESCO monitoring

UNESCO conducts periodic monitoring of Petra's condition through the State of Conservation reporting process. Reports cover the major risk factors (climate, tourism pressure, development pressure), the conservation responses, and ongoing challenges. The reports are publicly available through UNESCO's online portal and represent the most authoritative assessment of the site's current status.

Current challenges

Several ongoing concerns affect Petra's protection:

Climate change. Increased temperature variation and changing rainfall patterns are causing accelerated erosion on the sandstone monuments. Long-term conservation strategies are adapting to these changes.

Flash flooding. Petra's geography (the Siq narrows, the wadi system) makes the site susceptible to catastrophic flooding. The November 2018 flood killed 12 tourists and caused damage to the site; conservation efforts include improved early warning systems and additional flood control infrastructure.

Tourism pressure. Visitor numbers exceeding 1 million annually create wear-and-tear pressure on the most-visited monuments. Active management balances access with preservation.

Development pressure. Hotel construction, road expansion, and other infrastructure development around the Petra archaeological zone creates buffer-zone concerns. Recent decades have seen tighter zoning enforcement to limit inappropriate development near the site.

Conservation of the carved facades. The Treasury, Monastery, and other carved monuments experience continuous weathering from temperature variation, wind erosion, salt accumulation, and other natural processes. Ongoing conservation work attempts to slow these processes; complete prevention isn't possible.

Petra alongside other Jordanian World Heritage Sites

Petra was Jordan's first World Heritage Site. Subsequent Jordanian inscriptions:

  • Quseir Amra (1985). An 8th-century Umayyad desert castle northeast of Amman, inscribed the same year as Petra. Famous for the frescoes covering the interior walls.
  • Um er-Rasas (2004). An archaeological site east of Madaba with Byzantine and Umayyad mosaics.
  • Wadi Rum (2011). The desert landscape south of Petra, inscribed as both natural and cultural heritage.
  • Baptism Site (2015). Bethany Beyond the Jordan, the traditional site of Jesus's baptism, inscribed for religious-historical significance.
  • As-Salt (2021). The historic Ottoman-era town near Amman, inscribed for its preserved 19th-century architecture and tolerant religious heritage.

Jordan now has six UNESCO World Heritage Sites, an unusually high concentration for a country its size. The combined sites support Jordan's broader cultural heritage tourism strategy.

Frequently asked questions

When did Petra become a UNESCO World Heritage Site?
December 6, 1985. UNESCO's World Heritage Committee inscribed Petra during its 9th session, held in Paris. The inscription cited Petra's outstanding universal value as one of the world's most precious cultural properties and recognized it for criteria covering its exceptional architecture, cultural significance, and historical importance. Petra was Jordan's first World Heritage Site; Wadi Rum followed in 2011 as Jordan's second.
What does World Heritage status actually mean?
Three things. (1) International recognition of the site's outstanding universal value — meaning it has cultural importance transcending national borders. (2) An obligation on the host country (Jordan, in this case) to preserve and protect the site according to international standards. (3) Access to UNESCO technical and financial assistance for conservation and management. The status doesn't transfer ownership or sovereignty — Petra remains entirely Jordanian property under Jordanian law — but it creates international accountability for the site's care.
What changed at Petra after 1985?
Several significant changes. (1) The Bdoul Bedouin community living inside Petra was relocated to the new village of Umm Sayhoun, a controversial decision driven by UNESCO requirements to reduce continuous habitation impact on the monuments. (2) The Petra Archaeological Park boundaries were formally established with regulatory protection. (3) Visitor infrastructure was systematically upgraded — the modern Visitor Centre, marked trails, signage, and conservation protocols. (4) Major international archaeological projects became easier to organize through the World Heritage framework. (5) International tourism grew substantially as the UNESCO designation increased global recognition.
Why specifically 1985?
Several factors converged. The Jordanian government had been building the case for World Heritage status through the late 1970s and early 1980s as part of broader cultural protection initiatives. The UNESCO World Heritage Committee meeting in Paris in late 1985 was the natural inscription opportunity. The Bdoul resettlement to Umm Sayhoun had been planned in coordination with the World Heritage process — the resettlement happened in 1985 in parallel with the inscription, treating the two changes as connected components of the broader site protection initiative.
Are there ongoing concerns about Petra's protection?
Yes. Climate change is causing increased erosion on the sandstone monuments. Flash flooding has caused damage on multiple occasions (most notably November 2018 when 12 tourists were killed in a Siq flood). Tourism volume creates wear-and-tear pressure on the most-visited monuments. Development around the site (hotel construction, road expansion) creates buffer-zone concerns. The Jordanian authorities and UNESCO monitor these issues continuously; conservation work is ongoing.
