The Incense Road

The trade that made Petra wealthy. Frankincense and myrrh from southern Arabia, moved north through Nabataean territory to Mediterranean ports — and the toll revenues that funded the Treasury, the Monastery, and the rest of the visible monumental city.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 8-minute read · See our editorial standards

Petra exists because of the Incense Road. The wealth that built the Treasury, the Monastery, and the rest of the monumental city didn't come from local agriculture (there isn't enough water for it), local crafts (the city's craft production was modest by ancient standards), or local mining (the region has no major mineral resources). It came from controlling the chokepoint where the overland trade routes from southern Arabia connected to the Mediterranean world. For roughly 600 years (700 BCE - 300 CE), the Nabataeans charged transit fees on the caravans moving frankincense, myrrh, and other Arabian luxury goods north through their territory — and that toll revenue built the city.

This page covers what the trade actually was, how the Nabataeans controlled it, what they sold and to whom, and how the trade's decline ended Petra's monumental era. The framing throughout: this is the economic foundation that explains why Petra exists at all.

The goods

The Incense Road carried four primary luxury commodities from southern Arabia and the broader Indian Ocean trade network to Mediterranean markets:

Frankincense. An aromatic resin tapped from the bark of the Boswellia tree, which grows in specific climates in southern Arabia (modern Yemen and Oman) and the Horn of Africa (modern Somalia). Frankincense was the principal incense burned in religious ritual across the ancient Mediterranean — Roman temples, household shrines, and elite funerals all consumed it in vast quantities. The Roman Empire at its peak consumed an estimated 3,000 tons annually, with most of it transiting Nabataean territory.

Myrrh. A similar aromatic resin from the Commiphora tree, also native to southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa. Less abundant than frankincense, and used for both religious ritual and embalming/cosmetic purposes. The wealth-and-status implications of myrrh appear in the New Testament as one of the three gifts the magi bring to Bethlehem — alongside gold and frankincense, marking the cultural-economic prominence of the Arabian aromatic trade.

Spices. Pepper, cinnamon, ginger, and other spices reaching the Mediterranean from India and the Far East via the Arabian sea ports. The Nabataeans handled the overland leg from the Gulf of Aqaba ports up through their territory to Mediterranean exit points. Pepper alone became economically significant enough that the Roman state at one point demanded part of its tribute from conquered territories in pepper rather than coin.

Other luxury goods. Silk from China (reaching Arabia via Indian Ocean routes), pearls from the Persian Gulf, exotic woods, ivory, and gold. The Incense Road's traffic was diverse beyond the resins, though frankincense was the volume leader and the economic anchor.

The geography

The trade ran north from the Hadhramaut region (southern Arabian Peninsula, the modern Yemen-Oman borderlands) through the western edge of the Empty Quarter, across the Hejaz region, to the Gulf of Aqaba — about 2,400 kilometers of caravan travel through extreme desert. From the Gulf of Aqaba, the route turned northwest through Nabataean territory to the Mediterranean: either to Gaza on the southern Levantine coast (the most common terminus), or to Egyptian ports like Alexandria via the Sinai Peninsula.

The total journey from southern Arabia to Mediterranean ports took 60-90 days of caravan travel, with multiple waystations, water resupply points, and toll collection sites along the way. Petra sat near the final stretch — about 130 km north of Aqaba — and served as the major Nabataean administrative center for the trade rather than as a transit point for the caravans themselves (most caravans probably bypassed the city center to use the more direct route through the surrounding desert).

The route had been used for centuries before the Nabataeans rose to dominate it — references to frankincense trade appear in Egyptian sources from the 14th century BCE, and the Hebrew Bible references the Queen of Sheba's wealthy visit to Solomon in roughly the 10th century BCE, likely a reflection of southern Arabian trade contacts. The Nabataean period (roughly 4th century BCE through 2nd century CE) was the peak of overland trade volume, but the trade itself was much older.

How the Nabataeans controlled the trade

The Nabataean wealth from the Incense Road came from controlling chokepoints rather than from producing or selling the goods. Three mechanisms:

Water-source ownership. The Arabian caravans needed water at predictable intervals — every 2-3 days for the animals, more frequently in summer. The Nabataeans developed an extensive network of wells, cisterns, and waystations along the trade routes, providing reliable water access in exchange for transit fees. Caravans that didn't pay couldn't reliably access water; over the long desert stretches, this was effectively a refusal-of-service that no caravan operator could accept.

Toll collection. At controlled chokepoints — narrow valleys, river crossings, mountain passes — the Nabataeans charged transit fees. The pricing was structured to extract significant revenue without destroying the trade entirely: caravans paid, but at rates that still allowed them to profit on the goods they were carrying.

Protection services. The Nabataeans provided armed protection against bandits and rival raiding parties, in exchange for tribute or formal alliance arrangements. Caravan operators preferred the Nabataean fee structure to the chaos and risk of unprotected routes, even when the toll amounts were significant.

The combined revenue streams produced extraordinary wealth concentrated in the small Nabataean elite. The 1st-century BCE Greek geographer Strabo described the Nabataean kingdom as one of the wealthiest places he had encountered, with the people living in luxury more typical of major Mediterranean cities than of desert peripheries.

The Petra connection

The trade explains the existence and the wealth of Petra specifically. The city's monumental construction program — the Treasury, the Monastery, the major Royal Tombs, the colonnaded street, the water management infrastructure — was funded by the toll and tribute revenues from the Incense Road. The dates of construction align with the dates of peak trade volume:

  • 4th-3rd century BCE: Nabataean expansion and consolidation. Petra becomes the political capital. Modest construction.
  • 2nd-1st century BCE: Trade peaks under Augustus's pacification of the Mediterranean. Petra wealth accumulates.
  • 9 BCE - 40 CE (Aretas IV's reign): The Treasury, Monastery, and major Royal Tombs constructed during the period of maximum trade wealth.
  • 40-106 CE (later Nabataean kings): Construction continues at reduced pace as trade competition increases.
  • 106-200 CE (Roman period): Civic construction continues under Roman administration, but the monumental Nabataean program is over.

The fact that Petra's peak monumental construction overlapped precisely with the Incense Road's peak revenue period isn't coincidence — it's cause and effect.

What ended the trade

Three factors combined to end the overland Incense Road over the 1st-3rd centuries CE:

Roman annexation. When Trajan annexed the Nabataean kingdom in 106 CE, the trade continued but the toll revenues now went to the Roman imperial treasury rather than to the Nabataean royal court. The Petra elite lost the wealth concentration that had funded monumental construction. See the Roman annexation for the full story.

Direct sea routes. Greco-Roman sailors discovered the Indian Ocean monsoon patterns in the 1st century CE, opening direct sea routes between Mediterranean ports (via the Red Sea and the Egyptian Suez region) and southern Arabia/India. The sea routes were faster and cheaper than the overland caravans, gradually displacing the older trade pattern. By the 3rd century CE, the overland route handled a small fraction of its earlier volume.

Christianization. The conversion of the Roman Empire to Christianity in the 4th century reduced religious demand for incense — Christian liturgy used much less than the polytheistic Roman religion had, and the elite funeral practices that consumed enormous quantities of incense in the Roman world shifted to Christian burial practices that didn't require the same materials. The market for the principal Incense Road commodity collapsed over a few generations.

By the late Byzantine period (5th-6th centuries CE), the overland Incense Road was effectively gone. Petra continued to be inhabited but at a fraction of its peak population and wealth.

Where to see the trade legacy today

The most direct legacy is Petra itself — every visible monument was funded by trade revenues. Specifically:

  • The Treasury and the Royal Tombs — the most visible monuments, all funded by Aretas-era trade revenues
  • The Monastery — the largest carved monument, built during peak prosperity
  • The colonnaded street and civic infrastructure — the urban fabric supported by trade wealth
  • The water management system — the engineering investment enabled by sustained revenue surpluses

For travelers wanting to see other Incense Road sites: Avdat and Mamshit in the Israeli Negev preserve Nabataean caravan waystations with significant intact structures. Hegra (Mada'in Saleh) in Saudi Arabia preserves Nabataean monuments comparable in style to Petra. The Hadhramaut region in eastern Yemen has surviving frankincense-producing operations and trade-era architecture, though tourism access is currently limited by political conditions.

Frequently asked questions

What was the Incense Road?
The overland trade network that moved frankincense, myrrh, and spices from southern Arabia (modern Yemen and Oman) to the Mediterranean world, primarily between roughly 700 BCE and 200 CE. The route ran north through the Arabian Peninsula along established caravan paths, with the Nabataeans controlling the critical final stretch from the Gulf of Aqaba through Petra to Mediterranean ports like Gaza and Alexandria. Petra became wealthy because it sat at the chokepoint where the long Arabian routes connected to the Mediterranean market.
What's frankincense, and why was it valuable?
An aromatic resin from the Boswellia tree, native to southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa. It was the principal incense burned in religious ritual across the ancient Mediterranean — the Roman Empire alone consumed an estimated 3,000 tons annually at peak demand, used in temple worship, household devotions, and the cremation rites that featured prominently in Roman elite funerals. Frankincense had no functional substitute and the trees grow only in specific regions, which gave the Nabataeans and the broader Arabian trade network something close to a global monopoly on supply.
How did the Nabataeans control the trade?
Through a combination of geographic position, water-source ownership, and military capacity. The Arabian caravan routes converged at the northern end of the peninsula, where the Nabataean territory blocked access to Mediterranean ports. Caravans had to either use Nabataean-controlled wells and waterways or take much longer alternative routes through the open desert. The Nabataeans charged transit fees and toll payments at controlled chokepoints, provided water and protection to compliant caravans, and conducted occasional military operations against rival routes. The arrangement made the kingdom enormously wealthy without requiring direct participation in the producing or selling end of the trade.
When did the trade end?
Gradually, between roughly 100 and 300 CE. Three factors combined: (1) the Roman annexation of the Nabataean kingdom in 106 CE shifted control of the trade to Roman imperial structures; (2) the development of direct sea routes between Mediterranean ports and southern Arabia/India largely bypassed the overland caravans; (3) the rise of Christianity in the 4th century reduced the religious demand for incense (the Christian church used much less than the polytheistic Roman religion had). By the Byzantine period, the overland Incense Road was a minor secondary route rather than the lifeline that had made Petra wealthy.
Can you see traces of the trade at Petra today?
Mostly indirectly. The monumental architecture is the most visible legacy — the wealth that built the Treasury, the Monastery, and the Royal Tombs came from trade revenues. More specifically, you can see caravan stops and waystations along the Nabataean road network in the broader region (the most preserved examples are at Avdat and Mamshit in the Israeli Negev), inscriptions naming Nabataean caravan officials at Hegra in Saudi Arabia, and warehouse/storage structures at the lower city of Petra that probably handled goods in transit.
