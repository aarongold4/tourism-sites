The Abanotubani sulfur baths

The hot springs are why Tbilisi exists, and bathing in them is the most distinctive thing you can do in the city — a living tradition, not a recreation. Here's exactly how it works.

The brick domes at the Old Town's southern end are roofs: the bathhouses sit below street level, fed by hot, sulfur-rich springs that have been in continuous use since before the city had a name — the name, in fact, comes from them. Pushkin and Dumas took these waters and said so in writing; the marble plaque quoting Pushkin still hangs at the entrance of the bathhouse he praised. The smell is real but milder than the warnings suggest, the heat is serious, and the after-effect — a boneless, scrubbed-new calm — is the entire point.

The district holds roughly a dozen working houses in a short radius. Chreli Abano (the Orbeliani Baths), behind its spectacular blue-tiled Persian facade, is the iconic and most tourist-ready option: online booking, polished rooms, room-service tea, and prices to match. The traditional houses around it — Bathhouse No. 5, Gulo's, the Royal Baths, and their neighbors — trade polish for patina and undercut it substantially, with domed rooms that have steamed for generations. There is no wrong answer; there's only the polish-versus-patina dial.

How a session runs

Book or walk in, pay for a private room by the hour, and you get a changing area, a shower, and a tiled chamber with a sunken pool of spring water arriving genuinely hot. Soak in intervals — fifteen or twenty minutes, then the cool shower, then back — and if you've ordered the kisa, the mekise arrives mid-session to scrub you on the warm slab with cheerful violence. Order tea or cold water to the room where offered; hydration is not optional in water this hot. One hour is the standard booking and the right one. Afterward, the only sensible itinerary item is dinner at walking pace, which is why every plan in our itineraries cluster puts the bath at the day's end.

Choosing and timing

Pick Chreli Abano for a first bath if you want frictionless and beautiful; book a day or two ahead in season. Pick a traditional house if atmosphere outranks amenity — walk the lane, look at a room before committing, and carry cash, since card machines get temperamental below street level. Evenings after a walking day are the classic slot and the busiest; weekday afternoons are the quiet one. Winter is the connoisseur's season — emerging scalded and serene into cold air is the full version of the experience. Bring flip-flops, a towel if you'd rather not rent, and nothing valuable. The waterfall gorge behind the bath lane makes the perfect pre-soak stroll; the Old Town guide maps it.

The history under your feet

You are bathing in the reason the city exists. The legend has King Vakhtang Gorgasali's falcon tumbling into a hot spring here in the fifth century, prompting him to found a capital on the spot and name it for the warm water — tbili, “warm” — and whether or not a bird was involved, the springs genuinely predate and explain the city. The brick domes you see at street level are just the roofs; the bathhouses sit below, fed by the sulfur-rich water that has run here continuously since before Tbilisi had a name. Pushkin and Dumas took these waters and wrote about them, and the marble plaque quoting Pushkin still hangs at the entrance of the house he praised. Soaking here is the closest a visitor comes to touching the founding directly.

That lineage is worth carrying into the water, because it reframes the whole experience. This isn't a spa novelty bolted onto a tourist district — it's the city's original infrastructure, still serving its first purpose, with the neighborhood (Abanotubani literally means “bath district”) named for the function. The faint sulfur smell that the warnings overstate is simply the springs being honest about what they are. Combined with the cool gorge walk to the waterfall just behind the bath lane and the fortress overhead, the district is less a checklist stop than the literal source of everything else in this guide.

Frequently asked questions

What does a sulfur bath cost?

As of our last review: public (shared, single-sex) bathing runs roughly 5–10 GEL; private rooms run from about 50 GEL per hour at simpler houses to 100–200+ GEL at the famous ones, with Chreli Abano’s rooms starting around the low hundreds. The kisa scrub adds 10–25 GEL, paid in cash directly to the attendant. Towel and slipper rental is a few lari where not included.

Private room or public bath?

First-timers: private. The jump in price over public is small in absolute terms and the experience is completely controlled — your own pool, shower, and changing space, with treatments brought to you. Public bathing is the cultural deep end: cheap, single-sex, nude, and sociable, best attempted once you know you like the water.

Do I wear a swimsuit?

In private rooms it’s your call — many bathe nude, many wear swimwear; for the kisa scrub you’ll be asked to strip down regardless. In public sections, nude is the norm. Note the sulfur water can stain fabrics, so don’t bring your best suit. Flip-flops are non-negotiable everywhere — floors are hot and slick.

What is the kisa scrub actually like?

You soak until soft, then lie on a warm slab while a mekise scrubs you head to toe with a rough mitt — vigorous to the point of comedy, with visible rolls of dead skin as the proof of work. It’s the signature treatment and worth doing once; pay the mekise directly in cash and round up if they earned it (they did).

Do I need to book?

Chreli Abano takes online bookings and needs them a day or more ahead in season and on weekends. Most other houses are walk-in, with evening waits possible. An hour is the standard session and genuinely enough — the water is hot.
