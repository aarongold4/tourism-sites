HISTORY

The Nabataeans

The Arab civilization that built Petra — nomadic traders who became regional rulers, peaked in the 1st century BCE, and were absorbed into Rome in 106 CE. Here's what we know about who they were and what they accomplished.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 9-minute read · See our editorial standards

The Nabataeans were the people who built Petra. That's a useful starting answer, but it conceals the more interesting question: who were they, and how did a nomadic Arab tribe come to construct one of the great architectural achievements of the ancient world? The honest answer is that we know less than we would like — the Nabataeans left almost no literature, and most of what we know comes from Greek and Roman sources looking in at them from outside. But the broad outline of their history is clear, and the specific things they accomplished — particularly in water engineering and trade-route control — are increasingly well-understood from a century of archaeological excavation at Petra and the surrounding sites.

This page covers the long story: where they came from, how they rose to regional power, what life in their capital was like, how the kingdom ended, and what happened to the population afterward. For the dramatic 19th-century rediscovery of their abandoned capital, see the Burckhardt rediscovery page.

Origins

The Nabataeans first appear in historical sources around the late 4th century BCE, when they are described as nomadic camel-herding Bedouin in the Arabian peninsula. The Greek historian Diodorus Siculus, writing in the 1st century BCE but drawing on earlier sources, describes a Greek military expedition against them in 312 BCE — already, at this early date, the Nabataeans are wealthy enough to be worth attacking, and they manage to repel two successive Greek armies through a combination of guerrilla warfare and detailed knowledge of the desert terrain.

Over the following two centuries, the Nabataeans gradually transition from purely nomadic to semi-settled life, with Petra emerging as their de facto capital and trading center. By 168 BCE the first historically attested king, Aretas I, is reigning over a recognized kingdom. The Greek name "Petra" (rock) appears in this period as a translation of the Nabataean name for the city, which appears to have been Raqmu in their own language.

The expansion: Aretas III

The Nabataean kingdom reached its greatest territorial extent under King Aretas III (86–62 BCE), known as "Philhellene" — the friend of the Greeks. Aretas III briefly held Damascus from 84 to 72 BCE, controlling trade routes that stretched from southern Arabia to Syria and giving the Nabataean economy access to the eastern Mediterranean. This expansion alarmed the Romans, who had recently consolidated their hold on the eastern Mediterranean. In 64 BCE, the Roman general Pompey arrived in Syria and quickly forced the Nabataeans into a tributary relationship — paying tribute to Rome to retain nominal independence.

The next several decades were uneasy. The Nabataeans paid tribute to Rome but maintained their internal autonomy. They engaged in occasional military skirmishes (most notably losing significant territory to Herod the Great's invasion in 31 BCE). They also continued to expand and refine their control of the long-distance trade in incense, spices, and silk — the economic foundation of their kingdom.

The peak: Aretas IV

The reign of Aretas IV (9 BCE to 40 CE) is the golden age of Nabataean civilization. He took the throne name "rym 'mh" — "he who loves his people" — and his 49-year reign produced the most prosperous and culturally creative period in the kingdom's history. Most of the major monuments at Petra were built during this time, including:

  • The Treasury (Al-Khazneh) — traditionally identified as Aretas IV's mausoleum, though the identification isn't certain
  • The Royal Tombs cluster — though the Urn Tomb specifically may be linked to his successor Malichus II
  • The Garden and Pool Complex — an elaborate hydraulic showpiece in the lower city, recently re-excavated
  • The High Place of Sacrifice and most other cultic infrastructure
  • The colonnaded street and surrounding civic architecture

Petra under Aretas IV had a population estimated at 20,000–25,000 people — substantial for any 1st-century city, remarkable for one in a desert environment. The city housed a cosmopolitan mix of Nabataean nobility, traders from across Arabia and the Mediterranean, Greek-speaking architects and artists, and the slaves and servants who supported the elite. The architectural style is distinctively Hellenistic-influenced: Corinthian columns, broken pediments, classical proportions — combined with distinctively Nabataean elements like the crow-step cornices and the aniconic religious symbolism.

One unusual feature: Aretas IV's queens appear on Nabataean coins alongside him. The Nabataeans accorded women a higher legal and social status than most contemporary Mediterranean cultures, with queens functioning as co-rulers in inscriptions and currency. His successive queens Huldu and Shaqilat both appear on issued coinage. Whether this reflects underlying gender norms or specific political choices is debated.

The end of the kingdom

After Aretas IV's death in 40 CE, the Nabataean kingdom continued for another 66 years under three more kings — Malichus II, Rabbel II, and a final brief period under another Aretas. The kingdom remained nominally independent but increasingly under Roman influence; Nabataean military forces fought alongside the Romans during the Jewish revolt of 66–70 CE.

The last Nabataean king, Rabbel II (70–106 CE), reportedly struck an arrangement with the Romans: they would not invade during his lifetime, but on his death the kingdom would be annexed peacefully. When he died in 106 CE, the Emperor Trajan annexed Nabataea without significant resistance and reorganized it as the Roman Province of Arabia Petraea. Petra remained the provincial capital for a time, then was gradually overshadowed by Bosra (Bostra) to the north as the new administrative center.

The slow decline of Petra

Roman Petra continued to function as a significant city for several centuries. The colonnaded street was renovated in Roman style. New civic buildings were added. The local cult of Dushara was gradually replaced by Roman religious practice, and by the 4th century CE, Christianity had taken hold — Petra became a bishopric, and several of the Nabataean tombs (most notably the Urn Tomb in 446 CE) were converted into Christian churches.

The decline came mainly from three sources: changing trade routes that gradually bypassed Petra in favor of sea routes, two major earthquakes (363 CE and 551 CE) that damaged the water infrastructure and many of the monumental buildings, and the general decline of the Byzantine economy. By the 8th century, Petra was no longer a significant city. By the 11th century, it was effectively abandoned, with only the local Bdoul Bedouin tribe living in some of the caves and tombs.

What we still don't know

Despite a century of archaeological work, much about the Nabataeans remains uncertain. We don't know with confidence what their early religion looked like before Hellenistic contact. We have only fragmentary evidence of how their trade routes actually operated — what the caravans carried, how they were taxed, who organized them. The political structure of the kingdom is poorly understood; we know the kings' names from coinage but not the underlying tribal or administrative structure. And the question of how a nomadic Bedouin culture transitioned to monumental urban architecture in just two centuries remains one of the great puzzles of ancient Near Eastern history.

The Nabataeans left almost no literature of their own — no surviving histories, no philosophical or religious texts beyond short inscriptions, no biographical material. Almost everything we know about their internal life comes either through Greek and Roman writers looking in from outside, or through the archaeological record. New excavations at Petra in the last 25 years (particularly the Garden and Pool Complex and the upper terrace of the Great Temple) have produced significant new evidence, but the broad picture is still incomplete.

The Bdoul connection

The Bdoul Bedouin tribe — who lived in the caves and tombs of Petra until being relocated to the nearby village of Umm Sayhoun in 1985 to facilitate UNESCO World Heritage protection — claim descent from the original Nabataean inhabitants. The claim has some genetic support, though the matter is complicated by 1,500 years of intervening migration and intermarriage. What is unambiguous: the Bdoul have an unbroken cultural relationship with the Petra landscape that significantly predates the modern archaeological understanding. Many of the older Bdoul guides at Petra can name and describe monuments that aren't in any of the formal English-language guidebooks, drawing on family knowledge passed down through generations.

Frequently asked questions

Who were the Nabataeans?
The Nabataeans were an Arab people who originated as nomadic Bedouin in the Arabian peninsula and gradually settled in the area around Petra from about the 4th century BCE onward. By 168 BCE they had established a recognized kingdom; by the 1st century BCE they controlled an enormous swath of trade routes from Damascus to Mecca; and by 106 CE they had been absorbed into the Roman Empire as the Province of Arabia Petraea. Their distinctive culture combined Arab linguistic and tribal traditions with Hellenistic architectural and artistic borrowings.
When did the Nabataean kingdom rise and fall?
The conventional dates are 168 BCE (the reign of the first historically attested king, Aretas I) to 106 CE (Trajan's Roman annexation). Within that 274-year span, the kingdom peaked under King Aretas IV (9 BCE to 40 CE), who built most of the major monuments at Petra including the Treasury. After Roman annexation, Petra continued as an important regional center for several centuries before being progressively reduced by earthquakes — particularly the 363 CE and 551 CE quakes — and eventual abandonment by the 11th century.
What language did they speak?
The Nabataeans spoke an early form of Arabic in daily life and used Aramaic as their written and administrative language. Their distinctive Nabataean script — a stylized form of Aramaic written from right to left — is one of the direct ancestors of the modern Arabic script. Most of the surviving Nabataean inscriptions at Petra are short memorial or dedicatory texts. The Nabataeans appear not to have produced significant literature; their cultural record comes through architecture, coins, and references in Greek and Roman sources rather than from their own written works.
What made the Nabataeans wealthy?
Control of the long-distance trade in incense (frankincense and myrrh) and spices from southern Arabia and India to the Mediterranean and Mesopotamia. Petra sat at the crossroads of these routes, and the Nabataeans developed a sophisticated system of taxation, water management, and caravan support that made overland trade reliable. They also engineered remarkable water capture and storage infrastructure that supported a city of 25,000 in an arid environment — terraced fields, hidden cisterns, ceramic-pipe aqueducts, and dam systems against flash floods.
Were they polytheistic?
Yes. The chief Nabataean god was Dushara (literally "the Lord of the Mountain," sometimes spelled Dhushara or Dusares), often associated with the sun and represented aniconically as a rectangular block of stone (a betyl) rather than as a human figure. The chief goddess was Al-Uzza, associated with fertility and the morning star Venus. Lesser gods included Allat, Manat, and Hubal — names that later appeared as pre-Islamic Arabian deities. The Nabataean approach to representing gods through aniconic stone blocks rather than figural sculpture was distinctive and influential.
Where did the Nabataeans go after the kingdom ended?
Mostly nowhere — they stayed in place and gradually integrated into the wider Roman, Byzantine, and eventually Arab populations of the region. There was no "fall" in the dramatic sense; the kingdom's political structure ended in 106 CE, but the people, language, and culture persisted for centuries. By the 7th century, with the rise of Islam, Nabataean identity as a distinct ethnic group had effectively merged into the broader Arab population of the region. The modern Bdoul Bedouin of the Petra area claim descent from the original Nabataeans, a claim partly supported by genetic studies.
