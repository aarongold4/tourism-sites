HISTORY

Byzantine Petra

The Christian centuries — Petra from the 4th to the 7th century, after the Roman Empire became Christian and before the Arab-Islamic conquest. Mosaic-floored churches, carbonized papyrus archives, and the gradual decline of a city that had once been one of the wealthiest places in the ancient world.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 7-minute read · See our editorial standards

The Byzantine period at Petra is the least-discussed era of the city's main historical arc — overshadowed by the dramatic Nabataean monumental period before it and by the post-636 Islamic decline that followed. But the Byzantine centuries (roughly 330-636 CE) are surprisingly substantial. Petra remained populated, building churches, supporting a regional bishopric, maintaining trade connections, and preserving cultural life at a level that the post-Islamic Petra never matched. The most significant single archaeological discovery in modern Petra — the Petra Church with its mosaic floors and carbonized papyrus scrolls — is a Byzantine-era site.

This page covers what Byzantine Petra was, the Christian community that lived there, the major archaeological discoveries from the period, and how the Byzantine arc connects the Nabataean past to the Islamic-period present. The framing throughout: this is the last centuries when Petra was a real city, and the period worth more attention than most travelers give it.

The setting

The Byzantine period at Petra begins with the political and religious changes of the early 4th century CE: Constantine's conversion to Christianity (around 312), his recognition of the Christian church as a legitimate imperial institution (Edict of Milan, 313), the Council of Nicaea (325), and the founding of Constantinople as the new imperial capital (330). The Roman Empire's center of gravity shifted east, and what historians call the Byzantine Empire — the eastern continuation of Rome — governed Petra for the next 300 years.

Petra in the early 4th century was still a substantial provincial city, despite the long decline from its Aretas-era peak. Population estimates of 10,000-15,000 are typical for the early Byzantine period, with the city serving as a regional administrative center, a continuing trade entrepot (at reduced volume), and a stop on the Roman road network connecting the Levant to the Red Sea ports.

The political framework was Roman provincial administration in continuity with the post-106 period — the same basic governance structures, the same imperial taxation, the same legal system, the same military organization. What changed was the religious dimension and the cultural orientation: Christianity gradually displaced the older pagan traditions, and the city's cultural connections shifted increasingly toward Constantinople and the Christian east rather than toward Rome and the western Mediterranean.

The Christian community

Christianity at Petra has a continuous history from the 4th century onward, attested by:

The bishop's record. Petra had its first known bishop, Asterius, attending the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE. The presence of a bishop at a major ecumenical council indicates an established Christian community by the early 4th century. Subsequent bishops attended later councils — the records confirm a continuous episcopal tradition through the Byzantine period.

The church building program. Multiple Byzantine-era churches have been identified at Petra. The largest and best-preserved is the Petra Church (discussed below); others include smaller chapels and shrines distributed across the city. The construction dates concentrate in the 5th and 6th centuries, suggesting active community investment in religious infrastructure during these periods.

The pilgrimage tradition. Christian pilgrimage to the Holy Land was a major cultural movement in the Byzantine period, and Petra (as a regional Christian center near major pilgrimage routes) participated in this tradition. Pilgrim accounts from the 4th-6th centuries reference visits to the region.

The funerary record. Byzantine Christian burials and tomb inscriptions throughout the city document the community's daily presence — names, dates, family relationships, occupational identifications, religious affiliations.

The Petra Church

The most significant Byzantine-era archaeological site at Petra is the Petra Church, discovered in 1990 on the slope above the colonnaded street and excavated by the American Center of Oriental Research through the 1990s and early 2000s. The site is a major basilica dating to the late 5th and early 6th centuries CE.

Key features:

The architecture. A three-aisled basilica approximately 26 meters long, with an apse at the eastern end (the standard Byzantine church orientation, toward Jerusalem), narthex at the western end, and side aisles. The structure was built using earlier Nabataean and Roman stones as spolia (reused construction material) — visible in the foundations and lower walls.

The mosaic floors. Three substantially intact mosaic floors are preserved in the side aisles. The most famous shows personifications of the four seasons surrounded by animals, birds, and decorative borders — a high-quality Byzantine mosaic comparable to better-known examples at Jerash and Madaba. The mosaics are visible at the site (under a protective covering structure that allows visitor viewing).

The carbonized papyrus scrolls. The most extraordinary find. During the church's destruction in a 6th-century fire, dozens of papyrus scrolls in the church's archive were carbonized (preserved by the fire rather than destroyed by it). The carbonized scrolls record church business, real estate contracts, family records, and personal documents from 6th-century Petra — papers that wouldn't survive in any other normal preservation context. The scrolls have been painstakingly conserved and read by specialists, providing a unique window into daily life at Byzantine Petra.

The destruction. The church was destroyed by fire in the 6th century, possibly in connection with the broader regional disruptions of that period (the Justinianic Plague of 541-549, ongoing seismic activity, political instability). The destruction preserved the site exceptionally well — the collapsed roof sealed the mosaic floors and the archive scrolls until the modern excavation.

The Blue Chapel and other churches

The Petra Church isn't the only Byzantine-era religious site. Several other churches and chapels have been identified across the city:

The Blue Chapel. A smaller church near the Petra Church, named for the blue-painted columns and capitals that decorate the interior. The columns reuse Egyptian granite from earlier Roman or Nabataean construction. Less elaborate than the main Petra Church but well-preserved and accessible to visitors.

The Ridge Church. A church on the ridge above the main valley, less visited but with surviving architectural elements.

The Urn Tomb conversion. The Urn Tomb (the largest of the major Royal Tombs at the eastern end of the lower city) was converted into a Christian church in the Byzantine period. The Greek inscription above the entrance dates the conversion to 446-447 CE, naming the bishop Jason who consecrated the new church. This conversion is the most direct piece of evidence for the Byzantine reuse of older Nabataean monuments — a Nabataean royal tomb becoming a Christian church.

The broader Byzantine fabric

Beyond the explicitly religious architecture, Byzantine Petra continued the broader urban life of the post-Nabataean city:

The colonnaded street. Continued in use through the Byzantine period, with some maintenance and minor modifications. The Byzantine paving probably layered over the older Nabataean and Roman surfaces.

Residential areas. The Byzantine population lived in modified versions of earlier Nabataean and Roman housing, with some new construction in the post-363-earthquake reconstruction periods.

Trade continuation. While the Incense Road's overland traffic had declined substantially, Petra continued to serve as a regional trade center for the immediate surrounding territory. The trade was smaller-scale and less wealthy than the Nabataean peak but still significant for the city's economy.

Cultural production. The carbonized papyrus archives suggest active literacy and document-based legal and commercial culture. The mosaic art demonstrates continued artistic production. The community wasn't in absolute decline through the early Byzantine period — it was simply at a smaller scale than the earlier peak.

The decline within the period

The Byzantine centuries were a decline period in aggregate, though not a continuous one. Major events that affected the city:

The 363 CE earthquake. Caused major damage to the surviving Nabataean-Roman infrastructure. Reconstruction continued for centuries but never restored the full pre-earthquake city. See the 363 earthquake page.

The 551 CE Beirut earthquake. Additional damage to the surviving fabric, accelerating decline.

The Justinianic Plague (541-549 CE). The first major plague pandemic in the Mediterranean world reduced populations across the region, including at Petra. Recovery was slow and incomplete.

The Sassanid invasions (early 7th century). Brief Persian control of the region disrupted Byzantine administration before the Arab conquest replaced both powers.

By the time of the Arab conquest in 636 CE, Petra was substantially smaller and less wealthy than at the start of the Byzantine period. The city had perhaps 3,000-5,000 inhabitants — still a working town, but greatly reduced from the peak.

The Arab conquest and the end

The Battle of Yarmouk in 636 CE ended Byzantine control of the broader Levant; the Battle of al-Qadisiyyah in 637 ended Sassanid Persian control of Mesopotamia; by 640, the entire region was incorporated into the new Arab Caliphate. Petra transitioned from Byzantine to Arab-Islamic rule with relatively little immediate disruption — the Christian population continued, the administrative structures changed but daily life remained recognizable.

The longer-term decline came over the following centuries. By the 9th-10th centuries, Petra had ceased to be a working city; the Christian community had largely converted or migrated; the trade routes had shifted to other paths. The Crusader interlude (12th-13th centuries) briefly reintroduced Christian European presence to the region but didn't reverse the broader decline. By Burckhardt's rediscovery in 1812, Petra was inhabited only by Bedouin communities and known to the outside world primarily through fragmentary references in older sources.

What to look at on your visit

The most accessible Byzantine sites at Petra:

  • The Petra Church. On the slope above the colonnaded street, marked with signage. The mosaic floors are visible under the protective covering. Allow 15-20 minutes.
  • The Blue Chapel. Near the Petra Church, the smaller church with blue-painted columns.
  • The Urn Tomb interior. Climb the staircase to the Urn Tomb at the southern end of the Royal Tombs. The Greek inscription noting the 446-447 CE Christian conversion is visible above the entrance.
  • The papyrus scroll displays. The Petra Museum at the Visitor Centre includes information panels and reproductions of the carbonized papyrus scrolls. The originals are conserved at the American Center for Oriental Research in Amman.

Frequently asked questions

What's the Byzantine period at Petra?
The Christian era of Petra, roughly 330-636 CE. After Constantine moved the Roman capital to Constantinople in 330 and Christianity became the imperial religion, the eastern Roman empire (Byzantium) governed Petra for the next 300 years. During this period the city remained populated, the older pagan religious sites were gradually closed, and Christian churches were built — most prominently the Petra Church discovered in 1990, which preserves intact 6th-century mosaic floors.
Was Petra still a major city in this period?
Smaller than before but still significant. The Aretas-era peak population of 25,000-30,000 had declined to perhaps 8,000-15,000 by the early Byzantine period, with further decline through the 5th and 6th centuries. The city served as a regional bishopric (Petra had its own bishop), a continuing administrative center, and a stop on the gradually-declining trade routes. The 363 CE earthquake had damaged substantial portions of the urban fabric, but reconstruction continued through the 5th and 6th centuries.
What's the Petra Church?
A major 5th-6th century Byzantine basilica on the slope above the colonnaded street, discovered in 1990 and excavated through the early 2000s. The site preserves three intact mosaic floors (the most famous shows personifications of the seasons surrounded by animals and birds), wall fragments with frescoes, and a substantial number of carbonized papyrus scrolls that record church business, contracts, and personal documents from the 6th century. The carbonized scrolls are unusual — paper documents from this period rarely survive in any context.
When did Christianity arrive at Petra?
Gradually, with the broader Christianization of the Roman Empire in the 4th century. Petra had its first known bishop (Asterius) attending the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE, suggesting an established Christian community by the early 4th century. Through the 4th and 5th centuries, Christianity grew from a minority religion to the dominant tradition; by the 6th century, Petra was a thoroughly Christian city with multiple churches and an active religious life. Pagan practice continued at remote sites for longer but had largely ended by the 5th century.
When did the Byzantine period end?
636-637 CE, with the Arab-Islamic conquest. The Arab armies defeated the Byzantine forces at the Battle of Yarmouk (636) and subsequently incorporated the broader Levant into the new Arab caliphate. Petra and the surrounding region transitioned from Byzantine to Arab-Islamic rule with relatively little disruption — the Christian population continued for several more centuries before gradually converting to Islam. The major changes were political and administrative rather than civilizational.
