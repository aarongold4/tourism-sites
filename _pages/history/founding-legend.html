History

The founding of Tbilisi

A king, a hunt, and a hot spring: the founding legend is folklore, but the truth inside it — that Tbilisi exists because of its warm waters — is written into the city's name and still steaming under the Old Town.

Last reviewed: June 7, 2026

Every Georgian child knows the story. In the fifth century, King Vakhtang Gorgasali is hunting in the densely wooded gorge of the Mtkvari when his bird — a falcon in some versions, a pheasant in most — brings down its quarry, and both tumble into a steaming spring. The king, struck by the warm water bubbling out of the earth, declares that a city shall rise on the spot and names it for the discovery: tbili, the Old Georgian word for "warm." Tbilisi — "the warm place."

It is a founding myth doing what founding myths do: compressing a slow truth into a single vivid afternoon. The archaeology is clear that people had used this site long before Vakhtang — the combination of defensible gorge, river crossing, and thermal springs was always going to draw settlement. What the fifth-century king and his son Dachi actually did was raise an existing town into the capital of the kingdom of Iberia, shifting power downriver from older Mtskheta. The legend keeps the part that matters and discards the bureaucracy: the springs are the reason, and the name is the receipt.

The king on the cliff

Vakhtang Gorgasali — the epithet derives from the wolf-head (gorg-) helmet he supposedly wore into battle — became one of the most mythologized figures in Georgian history, a warrior-king later canonized as a saint. His grand equestrian statue stands beside the Metekhi church on its cliff above the river, sword at his side, surveying the city the legend says he willed into being. It is one of the Old Town's fixed reference points, and knowing whose horse you're looking at turns a photo stop into a thread back fifteen centuries.

The deeper payoff of the founding story is what it does to the sulfur baths. They are not a quaint survival or a spa novelty; they are the city's original infrastructure, the literal reason for its existence, in continuous service since before the name was coined. Bathing in Abanotubani is the closest a visitor comes to touching the founding directly — the same warm water, the same gorge, fifteen hundred years on.

Reading it into the rest of the city

The founding is chapter one of a long story the rest of this cluster carries forward: the golden age and the centuries of sackings, the Persian and Russian eras, and the Soviet decades whose marks are still legible in the streets. For the sweep of it, start at the history overview; for the chapter most visible on a walk today, read Soviet Tbilisi (publishing soon). And then go stand in Abanotubani, where the whole thing started with a king, a bird, and a cloud of sulfurous steam.

Where the legend lives in the city

The founding story isn't just a tale — it's mapped onto places you can still stand in. The hot springs that supposedly cooked Vakhtang's falcon-struck pheasant still run beneath the brick domes of Abanotubani, and soaking there is the most direct way a visitor touches the legend. A bronze statue of King Vakhtang Gorgasali on horseback presides over the Metekhi cliff above the river, the traditional site of his palace, gazing across at the Old Town he is said to have willed into being. And the shift the legend dramatizes — capital moving downriver from ancient Mtskheta to the warm-springs site of Tbilisi — is a real historical transition you can retrace in a single short day trip.

Hold the legend lightly as history and fully as meaning. Tbilisi almost certainly existed as a settlement before Vakhtang's fifth-century reign, and the falcon-and-pheasant tale has the tidy shape of myth rather than chronicle — but its truth is in what it encodes: a city defined from birth by its warm water, its strategic crossroads position, and a founder-king who became a national symbol of independence. That origin sets up everything that follows, from the medieval golden age to the Russian and Soviet centuries, and the full sweep is laid out in the history overview. Read the legend first, and the layered city overhead suddenly has a first chapter.

Frequently asked questions

Did a pheasant really found Tbilisi?

It’s a legend, not a chronicle entry. The traditional story has King Vakhtang Gorgasali hunting in the wooded gorge in the fifth century when his falcon (a pheasant in many tellings) falls into a hot spring; struck by the warm water, he founds a city on the spot. The detail that matters is true even if the bird isn’t: the springs are why the city exists, and the name proves it.

What does the name Tbilisi mean?

It comes from the Old Georgian tbili, “warm” — a direct reference to the sulfur hot springs that the founding legend credits with the site’s selection and that still feed the bathhouses of Abanotubani today. The older European name Tiflis is the Persian-mediated form of the same word.

How much of the legend does archaeology support?

The pheasant is folklore; the antiquity is real. Excavations show the site was settled well before Vakhtang’s fifth-century date — the gorge’s springs and river crossing made it valuable long before any king made it a capital. What Vakhtang and his son Dachi did was elevate an existing settlement into the kingdom’s capital, which the legend compresses into a single hunting trip.

Who was King Vakhtang Gorgasali?

A fifth-century king of Iberia (eastern Georgia), later canonized, and one of the most mythologized figures in Georgian history — “Gorgasali” refers to the wolf-head helmet he supposedly wore. The grand equestrian statue near the Metekhi church on the cliff is his, and it remains a fixture of the Old Town skyline.

Where can I see the founding story in the city today?

In Abanotubani, where the springs that started it all still steam under their brick domes; at the Metekhi cliff, where Vakhtang’s statue surveys the river; and across at Mtskheta, the older capital he succeeded. Reading the founding first makes all three land harder.
