Day Trips

Day trips from Tbilisi

Tbilisi may be the best day-trip base in the Caucasus: a 5,000-meter peak, a UNESCO ancient capital, and the world's oldest wine region all sit within a few hours' drive — in three different directions.

The geography is almost too convenient. Drive north from Tbilisi and the road climbs into the High Caucasus, ending beneath Mount Kazbek. Drive east and the hills open into Kakheti, where families have made wine in buried clay vessels for longer than recorded history. Drive west and within half an hour you're in Mtskheta, the ancient capital where Georgia adopted Christianity in the fourth century. Each direction is a full, distinct day — and choosing between them is the central planning decision of most Tbilisi trips.

This cluster compares the routes honestly and then, on each route's own page, gets into the operator-level detail: which tours run it well, what a private driver costs, and how to do it independently where that's realistic.

North: Kazbegi and the Military Highway

The Georgian Military Highway is the day trip people fly to Georgia for. The historic road north toward the Russian border stacks its highlights generously: the fortress-monastery of Ananuri on its reservoir, the climb past the ski town of Gudauri, the Russia–Georgia Friendship Monument balcony hanging over a gorge, and the crossing of the Jvari Pass before descending to Stepantsminda — still universally called Kazbegi. Above the town, the fourteenth-century Gergeti Trinity Church sits on its ridge against the glaciated bulk of Mount Kazbek, and the image does not disappoint in person.

It's a long day — expect twelve hours door to door with stops — and the road itself is half the experience, so a window seat matters. Group tours run daily year-round; 4x4 transfers cover the final climb to the church, though the walk up is a rewarding hour-plus for the fit. Weather rules everything here: see the FAQ below before booking a winter date.

West: Mtskheta, the ancient capital

Mtskheta is the easy one — barely thirty minutes from Tbilisi, and so close that it works as a half day, an add-on to other routes, or even a long lunch excursion. It was Georgia's capital for nearly a millennium and remains its spiritual heart: the town's great Svetitskhoveli Cathedral and the hilltop Jvari Monastery, staring at each other across the confluence of two rivers, anchor a UNESCO World Heritage listing. Jvari's view over the meeting rivers is the single best panorama within easy reach of the capital.

Because it's so close, Mtskheta is also the day trip most worth doing without a tour: a Bolt or taxi there, a wander, lunch, and a ride back is simple and cheap, and the dedicated page maps it step by step.

East: Kakheti, the cradle of wine

Kakheti is Georgia's wine region — which is to say, the oldest wine region on earth, where the qvevri method of fermenting in buried clay vessels is recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage. A classic day loops through Sighnaghi, the fortified hill town gazing across the Alazani Valley to the Caucasus wall, and the wine capital Telavi, with tasting stops at family cellars in between. The amber wines — whites fermented on their skins to a deep gold — are the signature pour and unlike anything in Western Europe.

The format question matters more here than anywhere: Georgia's zero-tolerance driving law means tastings require a tour, a hired driver, or unusual restraint. Wine-focused small groups, generalist day tours with token tastings, and private cellar itineraries are genuinely different products; the Kakheti page separates them.

The offbeat option: Uplistsikhe and Gori

West past Mtskheta, the cave city of Uplistsikhe — inhabited from before classical antiquity into the Middle Ages — spreads its carved halls and tunnels across a riverside ridge. It pairs naturally with Gori, whose Stalin Museum preserves, with almost no editorial framing, the dictator's childhood home, personal railway carriage, and a museum frozen in the Soviet gaze. The combination makes the region's most thought-provoking day, and tours routinely bundle it with Mtskheta on the return.

Further-flung classics — the David Gareja monastery complex on the Azerbaijani border, Vardzia's cliff monastery, and Borjomi's spa forests — push past the comfortable day-trip radius and join this cluster in later phases as overnight-worthy extensions.

Frequently asked questions

What’s the single best day trip from Tbilisi?

For drama, Kazbegi — the Military Highway is among the great mountain drives, and Gergeti Trinity Church against the peak is Georgia’s defining image. For ease, Mtskheta — thirty minutes away and half a day well spent. For food and wine, Kakheti. If you have two day-trip days, do Kazbegi and Kakheti and see Mtskheta on the way to one of them; many tours combine it.

Group tour, private driver, or marshrutka?

Group tours are the cheapest organized option and fine for the classic routes. A private driver-guide — including app-based services like GoTrip that fix the price in advance — costs more but sets your own pace, and splits well between three or four people. Marshrutkas (shared minibuses) are the local way and very cheap, but they leave from out-of-center stations on their own schedule and suit travelers with more time than money.

Is Kazbegi doable in winter?

Often, but never guaranteed. The Military Highway climbs over a high pass that closes temporarily in heavy snow, and tours re-route or cancel accordingly — usually announced the evening before. From roughly November to March, book Kazbegi early in your stay so a weather day can slide, and treat Kakheti or Mtskheta as the fallback.

Can I do wine tastings if I’m driving?

Georgia enforces a zero-tolerance drink-driving limit, so a self-drive Kakheti wine day doesn’t work unless one of you genuinely doesn’t taste. This is the day trip where a tour or hired driver earns its cost — every format below solves the problem differently.

How far ahead should day trips be booked?

Group departures on the classic routes run daily and rarely sell out more than a few days ahead outside May-June and September-October peaks. Well-reviewed private guides and small-group wine tours are the exception — those deserve one to two weeks of lead time once your dates are set.
