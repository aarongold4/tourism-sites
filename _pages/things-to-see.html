THINGS TO SEE

The plateau, and the triangle beyond it

Giza is the headline, but the Old Kingdom's full story is told across four sites within an hour of each other: Giza, Saqqara, Memphis, and Dahshur. Here's everything worth your time at each.

The Giza plateau holds the three famous pyramids — Khufu, Khafre, Menkaure — plus the Sphinx, the reconstructed solar boat of Khufu, the workers' cemetery, and a panoramic desert viewpoint that produces the photograph everyone came for. That alone fills a morning. But the pyramids at Giza were the culmination of a century of trial and error, and the trials are still standing: the Step Pyramid at Saqqara, and the Bent and Red Pyramids at Dahshur, where the engineering story becomes visible.

This cluster covers every major sight across the four-site triangle, with the practical layer — which tombs are open, which interior tickets are worth it, where the crowds concentrate and when — that turns a checklist into a plan.

Reading the plateau in one paragraph

Everything at Giza belongs to three generations of one family, built in sequence across about seventy-five years: Khufu's Great Pyramid first (c. 2560 BC), then his son Khafre's — higher ground, steeper angle, the surviving white cap, and the Sphinx carved from his quarry — then his grandson Menkaure's, a tenth the volume with the finest statuary of the age. Each pyramid anchored a complex: mortuary temple against its east face, causeway running downhill, valley temple at the desert's edge where the Nile's floodwaters once arrived. Around them spread the cemeteries of the court, the queens' pyramids, the buried boats, and — south of the Sphinx — the excavated city of the workforce that built it all. Walk it east to west and you walk up the family tree.

How to spend your hours

With three hours (the standard plateau visit): Great Pyramid base and its surviving casing stones, one interior if booked, the panoramic viewpoint for the nine-pyramid composition, then downhill through Khafre's valley temple to the Sphinx enclosure. With a full day: add the walk to Menkaure's quiet end and the Tomb of Meresankh III, then give the afternoon to the Grand Egyptian Museum at the plateau's edge — the boat, the Tutankhamun galleries, and the statuary that came off this ground. With two days: the second morning belongs to Saqqara and Dahshur, thirty minutes south, where the pyramid form was invented and debugged — arguably the better archaeological day, and certainly the quieter one. The orderings, timed, are in itineraries.

Interiors: a buying guide in brief

Every interior is a separate ticket on the tiered system (current prices). The Great Pyramid (1,500 EGP, ~150/day cap) is the name and the Grand Gallery; the second pyramid on rotation — Khafre or Menkaure, 200-280 EGP — is the value play with near-identical chamber atmosphere; the Step Pyramid's interior at Saqqara is the historical one; and the Red Pyramid at Dahshur is, for connoisseurs, the best climb in Egypt at the friendliest price. Pick by knees, budget, and crowd tolerance — the dedicated pages below make each case honestly.

What's deliberately not on this list

A few standbys of older guidebooks have moved or closed: the boat-shaped Solar Boat Museum beside the Great Pyramid closed when the ship moved to GEM in 2021 (the full story), private vehicles were banned from the plateau in 2025 in favor of the free shuttle loop, and the interior-access rotation means any specific "you can enter Khafre" claim ages fast. Every page in this cluster carries a review date for exactly that reason.

The photography hour-by-hour

The plateau faces its photographers east and south, and the light writes the schedule. 7-9am: warm side-light on the east faces, long shadows, the Sphinx's face lit — the window for everything close-up. Midday: vertical glare flattens the monuments; shoot details, casing textures, and people instead. 2-4pm: the panoramic viewpoint's hour — sun behind you looking northeast, the nine-pyramid stack modeled in warm light — and the Sphinx in silhouette. The classic compositions: casing stones up the Great Pyramid's north face at dawn; Khafre rising behind the Sphinx from the enclosure's southeast; the full panorama with camels from the desert overlook. Drone flying is prohibited; interior photography policies rotate — ask at the ticket point, not after.

An honest skip list

Time is the scarce ticket, so: the Workers' Cemetery ticket (the story is better read than seen — here); a second camel ride (one at the viewpoint is the experience; two is a billing strategy); midday hours on the plateau generally (trade them for GEM's air conditioning); and Memphis as a standalone trip (essential as the two-day plan's lunch-stop history lesson, thin as a destination). Nothing on the main pages of this cluster makes the skip list — that's why they're the main pages.

Crowd patterns, by day and season

Daily: the bus fleets land 9:30-10:30 and lift 2:30-3:30 — the two quiet windows bracket them. Weekly: Fridays and Saturdays add domestic visitors; Sunday-Wednesday mornings run lightest. Seasonally: December-January and Easter are maximum load; May and September are the shoulder-season sweet spots; summer is hot and correspondingly thin. Saqqara and Dahshur run a fraction of Giza's density on every axis — the structural argument for the two-day program.

If you have X hours: the ranked allocation

The cluster's contents, compressed into an allocation table you can hold in your head. Three hours (a layover, a cruise window): Giza alone, on the compressed route — Great Pyramid base, viewpoint, Sphinx — with one interior only if the clock is ahead. A full day: the plateau morning plus GEM afternoon, the classic pairing the geography hands you. Two days: add the southern triangle — Saqqara, Memphis, Dahshur — which is where sightseeing becomes education and crowds become memory. Three days: add the Tahrir museum and the medieval city, completing the set. Beyond three, you're choosing between depth (second passes, Meresankh, the workers' story) and breadth (Alexandria, Luxor) — both covered in their pages. The ranking embedded in that table is deliberate: Giza first because it's why you came, Saqqara-Dahshur second because it's the trip's best surprise, museums woven through because Cairo's afternoons demand shelter, and everything else after.

Tickets across the sites: the bundling reality

Each site in this cluster tickets separately — Giza, Saqqara, Memphis, and Dahshur are four gates, four (card-first) transactions — and the "combo tickets" sold by third-party platforms are convenience resales with markup, not official bundles; the inclusive passes that do exist (the Cairo Pass tier, when offered) only pencil out for travelers hitting many sites across several days, and their terms change often enough that this site points you to the official platforms rather than printing a verdict that will stale. The stable advice: pre-buy Giza online the night before (capacity tickets are the scarce asset), buy the southern sites at their gates the day of (they don't sell out), and budget the full multi-site, multi-interior program at roughly 2,000-3,000 EGP per person at recent prices — still among the cheapest world-class sightseeing on Earth.

Closures and rotation: why pages here carry dates

The monuments are under permanent conservation, and access rotates accordingly: the second and third pyramids alternate their interiors, tombs at Saqqara open and close by season, restoration scaffolding migrates, and ticket tiers get redrawn most years. This is normal and mostly good — the rotation is why the Bent Pyramid's interior exists to visit at all, closed for 65 years and reopened in 2019 — but it means any guide's specifics decay. The system this site uses against decay: every operational claim carries the page's review date, the official platform gets the final word in every practical paragraph, and reader corrections (welcome here) trigger re-verification. Read the dates the way you'd read a weather forecast's timestamp.

One perfect detail per site

Beyond each page's full treatment, a single detail to hunt at every stop — the connoisseur's scavenger list. At Khufu: the surviving casing stones at the north base, fitted so finely the joints barely admit paper — run a respectful eye, not a hand. At Khafre: the summit casing through binoculars at the day's first light, when the original surface glows. At Menkaure: the unfinished granite courses by the entrance, chisel-dressed mid-stroke for forty-five centuries. At the Sphinx: the tail — curled around the right haunch, unseen by almost everyone who photographs the face. At Saqqara: the bundled-reed columns of Djoser's entrance colonnade, stone pretending to be plants because architecture had no stone traditions yet. At Dahshur: the exact course where the Bent Pyramid's angle breaks — engineering humility you can point to. At Memphis: Ramses's cartouches on the colossus's shoulder and belt, royal branding at eye level from the gallery. Small things; they turn a second look at each site into a first look.

Pairing the sites with their objects

The cluster's last organizing trick: every site here has a museum half, and seeing them as pairs completes both. Khufu's pyramid pairs with his boat and his ivory miniature; Khafre's complex pairs with the diorite enthronement his valley temple yielded; Menkaure's pyramid pairs with the triads and the dyad that made his name; the workers' city pairs with Merer's papyri (displayed periodically) and the Tahrir bakery models; Saqqara pairs with the Imhotep Museum at its own gate; and Memphis pairs with everything — the capital's scattered furniture filling rooms across Cairo. The practical version of this principle is the museum strategy page; the experiential version is simpler: visit the monument and its objects within a day of each other, in either order, and each makes the other specific. The plateau gives the scale; the cases give the faces.

The sites, in a sentence each

The cluster's index, compressed: Khufu is the scale, Khafre is the composition, Menkaure is the craftsmanship, the Sphinx is the face, the boat is the proof, Meresankh is the color, the viewpoint is the photograph, Saqqara is the education, Dahshur is the solitude, and Memphis is the footnote that explains the rest. Ten sentences, ten pages, one plateau and its southern triangle — and the ranking embedded in the order is the one this site would defend at any dinner table.

Frequently asked questions

Can you go inside the pyramids?

Yes — the Great Pyramid (Khufu) is open to visitors with a separate interior ticket, and typically one of the other two pyramids rotates open as well. The interior is a steep, cramped, hot climb to a bare granite chamber: historically extraordinary, physically demanding, and not for the claustrophobic. Whether it's worth the extra ticket is a genuine judgment call we cover in the Great Pyramid guide.

Is Saqqara worth it if I have already seen Giza?

Saqqara is arguably the better archaeological experience. The Step Pyramid of Djoser is the world's oldest monumental stone building, the recently opened tombs hold the most vivid reliefs in Egypt, and crowds are a fraction of Giza's. If you have a second morning, spend it there.

How long do you need at the Giza plateau?

Three to four hours covers the plateau properly: the three pyramids, the panoramic viewpoint, the Sphinx, and the Great Pyramid interior if you choose it. A rushed visit can be done in 90 minutes; a photography-heavy morning can fill five hours.
