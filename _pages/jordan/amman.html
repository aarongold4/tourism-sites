JORDAN

Amman

Jordan's capital city — and the practical starting point for most Jordan trips. Roman ruins, Ottoman-era souks, modern restaurants, and a base for day trips to Jerash, the Dead Sea, and Madaba. Here's how to make Amman work.

Last reviewed June 4, 2026 · 10-minute read · See our editorial standards

Amman is the city most travelers spend the least time in, and most regret not spending more. The reasons travelers underweight it are real — it's not visually iconic like Petra, doesn't have the photographic drama of Wadi Rum, and the layout (hills, sprawl, traffic) doesn't reward casual wandering the way Jerusalem or Marrakech do. But Amman is one of the better Middle Eastern capital cities for travelers who give it time: the Citadel and Roman Theatre genuinely repay a visit, the downtown souks are working markets rather than tourist setups, the food scene punches above the city's profile, and the position makes Amman the practical hub for the rest of Jordan.

This page covers Amman as travelers actually use it: a one-to-two-day stop on the way to Petra, possibly with day trips north to Jerash or south to Madaba and the Dead Sea. The framing throughout: give Amman 24-48 hours and you'll like it more than you expected to.

What to actually see

The Citadel (Jabal al-Qal'a)

The hilltop archaeological site at the city's geographical and historical center. The Citadel has been continuously occupied for 7,000 years, with layers of Bronze Age, Iron Age, Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, and early Islamic structures visible. The main features today: the Temple of Hercules (6 columns standing of the 2nd-century Roman temple), the Umayyad Palace complex (8th-century, partially reconstructed with the distinctive dome), and the Byzantine Church. The Jordan Archaeological Museum on the Citadel grounds (small but worthwhile, 1 JOD extra) holds the Ain Ghazal statues — some of the oldest large-scale human sculptures ever found, dating to 7,250 BCE.

Entry is 3 JOD, free with Jordan Pass. Allow 90 minutes. The Citadel offers the best panoramic view of central Amman — the white limestone buildings extending across the hills in every direction. Best in late afternoon when the light is good for photographs and the temperatures are manageable.

The Roman Theatre and Hashemite Plaza

Directly visible from the Citadel below — the 6,000-seat Roman theatre carved into the north slope of Jabal al-Joufah in the 2nd century CE. Two small museums flank the theatre (Folklore Museum and Popular Traditions Museum), each 1 JOD. The Hashemite Plaza in front of the theatre is downtown Amman's main public space — busy in the evenings with families, food vendors, occasional public performances. Walking distance from the Citadel (steep downhill, easier going down than up).

Downtown (Al-Balad)

The old Ottoman-era heart of Amman, immediately around the Roman Theatre. The souks here are working markets, not tourist constructions — vegetable, spice, gold, and textile markets all functioning for local customers. Walk down King Faisal Street, through the Bukhariyya market for spices, past Habibah Sweets (the knafeh institution, get the cheese version), to the Grand Husseini Mosque. The contrast between the modern white-limestone neighborhoods on the surrounding hills and the older downtown is what most travelers find memorable about Amman.

Rainbow Street and Jabal Amman

A hillside neighborhood with cafes, boutique shops, art galleries, and the famous "Wild Jordan" gift shop (Jordanian crafts, the Royal Society for the Conservation of Nature retail outlet). The street ends at the First Circle, with views back over downtown. Best for a slow afternoon walk — coffee at one of the cafes, browsing the shops, dinner at Sufra (upscale traditional Jordanian) or Beit Sitti (cooking-class restaurant where you can eat what you make).

The Jordan Museum

The country's national museum, in the Ras al-Ain neighborhood. The collection covers Jordan from prehistory through the modern era, with major Dead Sea Scrolls fragments (genuinely rare, on rotating display), the Ain Ghazal statues, and Nabataean artifacts including some carved pieces from Petra. Entry is 5 JOD. Worth 90 minutes to 2 hours if you've got any interest in the regional archaeology.

Day trips from Amman

Amman's position makes it the natural base for several major Jordanian attractions reachable as half- or full-day trips.

Jerash (north, 50 km, 1 hour)

The Roman archaeological site — the country's second-most-significant after Petra. A half-day trip works well. See the Jerash page for the full breakdown.

The Dead Sea (west, 55 km, 1 hour)

The lowest point on Earth's land surface (-430m below sea level), with the famous floating experience. Several public and private beaches; the Movenpick Dead Sea or Hilton Dead Sea resorts allow day passes for $40-80 USD including beach access, pool, lunch. Worth a half-day; full-day if you want spa time. See the Dead Sea page.

Madaba and Mount Nebo (south, 30-35 km, 45 minutes)

Madaba is famous for the 6th-century mosaic floor map of the Holy Land at St. George's Church — the oldest surviving map of the region. Mount Nebo (a few kilometers further) is the traditional spot where Moses saw the Promised Land before dying, with a small church and panoramic view across the Jordan Valley. Both together work as a half-day trip.

The Desert Castles (east, 30-70 km, varies)

A loop of 7-8 Umayyad-era hunting lodges and palaces in the eastern desert — Qasr Amra (UNESCO-listed for its 8th-century fresco paintings, the only major surviving Umayyad palace interior decoration), Qasr Kharana, Qasr al-Azraq (where T.E. Lawrence based winter operations during the Arab Revolt). Full-day trip with a hired driver (60-80 JOD for the day). Niche but rewarding for travelers interested in early Islamic architecture.

Getting around Amman

Amman is a hill city with sprawling layout — walking between districts is rarely practical. Three transport options:

Taxis: The default. Yellow taxis are metered (insist on the meter — "addad" — when you get in); most cross-city trips run 2-5 JOD. White service taxis (shared, fixed routes) are cheaper but require knowing the system. Uber and Careem operate in Amman with similar fare levels to metered taxis and easier for non-Arabic-speakers.

Walking: Within neighborhoods (downtown, Rainbow Street, the Citadel area) walking works. Between neighborhoods it usually doesn't — the hills are steep and the distances are deceptive on a map.

Rental car: Available but generally not recommended for Amman city stays. Traffic is heavy, parking is limited, and Jordanian driving culture (assertive merging, loose lane discipline) is intimidating to first-timers. Save the rental car for the inter-city driving (Amman to Petra to Wadi Rum).

Where to stay

Three areas with distinct character:

Downtown (Al-Balad). Closest to the Citadel, Roman Theatre, and souks. More authentic, busier, occasionally noisier. Budget hotels start around $30 USD/night; mid-range $60-90 USD/night. The Boutique Hotel and Sydney Hotel are well-reviewed local options.

Jabal Amman / Rainbow Street. The boutique zone. Walking distance to Rainbow Street restaurants and cafes, short taxi to downtown. Mid-range $80-130 USD/night, boutique $120-200 USD/night. The Boutique Hotel Amman, Caravan Hotel, and Toledo Amman are consistently recommended.

Abdoun / Sweifieh / Shmeisani. Upscale modern Amman. International hotel brands (Four Seasons, Fairmont, Marriott, Sheraton, W). $200-400+ USD/night. Better for travelers prioritizing comfort and amenities over location near the historical sites.

For first-time visitors, Jabal Amman is the right call. Walking distance to the best cafes and restaurants, easy taxis to the historical sites, and a meaningful "stay in a neighborhood" experience rather than a generic hotel district.

The realistic 2-day Amman itinerary

For travelers giving Amman 48 hours:

Day 1. Morning at the Citadel (90 min) and the Jordan Museum (90 min). Lunch at a downtown restaurant near the Roman Theatre. Afternoon walking the downtown souks. Evening drink and dinner on Rainbow Street.

Day 2. Day trip — either Jerash + Ajloun in the north, or Madaba + Mount Nebo + Dead Sea in the south. Either combination is roughly 8-10 hours including travel. Return to Amman for dinner.

This is enough Amman for most travelers heading on to Petra. Travelers wanting more depth (the desert castles, multiple day trips, slower museum time) can stretch to 3-4 days comfortably.

Frequently asked questions

How many days do I need in Amman?
One full day is enough for most travelers — the Citadel, Roman Theatre, downtown souks, and a meal at one of the modern restaurants. Two days adds a half-day at the Jordan Museum, the Rainbow Street neighborhood walking exploration, and a slower pace at the markets. Three+ days is for travelers using Amman as a base for Jerash, Madaba, the Dead Sea, and Mount Nebo — all reachable as half-day or day trips from the capital.
Where should I stay in Amman?
Three areas work well. Downtown (Al-Balad) puts you walking-distance to the Citadel, Roman Theatre, and souks — atmospheric, busier, more "real" Amman. Jabal Amman / Rainbow Street area is the boutique-hotel zone with cafes, restaurants, and a 10-minute taxi to downtown — recommended for first-timers. Abdoun / Sweifieh area is upscale modern Amman with the international hotel brands (Four Seasons, Fairmont, W) — quieter, more suburban, longer taxi to attractions. For most travelers, Jabal Amman is the sweet spot.
What's the airport transfer situation?
Queen Alia International Airport (AMM) is 35 km south of central Amman — 30-40 minutes by taxi in normal traffic. The fixed-rate official airport taxi is 22 JOD into the city; some drivers attempt to charge 30-40 JOD if you don't insist on the fixed rate. The Sariyah Airport Express bus runs every 30-60 minutes for 3.5 JOD to the Tabarbour bus station (then taxi to your hotel), much cheaper if you don't mind the longer journey. Most Amman hotels can arrange airport pickup for around 25-30 JOD if you book in advance.
Is Amman safe for tourists?
Yes, very. Jordan ranks among the safer countries in the Middle East and Amman specifically has minimal street crime, pickpocketing, or violent incidents affecting tourists. The usual urban precautions apply (don't leave valuables visible in cars, women should dress modestly in conservative neighborhoods downtown, avoid demonstrations or protests when they occur), but Amman is genuinely one of the easier Middle Eastern capitals for travelers. Solo travelers including women report consistently positive experiences.
Should I learn any Arabic for Amman?
Helpful but not necessary. English is widely spoken in tourist areas, hotels, and most restaurants. Basic phrases that help: "shukran" (thank you), "ahlan wa sahlan" (hello/welcome), "wein el..." (where is the...), "bikaam?" (how much?). Taxi drivers often have limited English but can navigate by major landmarks. Hand gestures and Google Translate fill any gaps. Most travelers get by comfortably without studying Arabic.
What's the food scene like in Amman?
Amman is one of the better Middle Eastern food cities — both traditional Levantine cuisine and a strong modern restaurant scene. Must-try traditional: mansaf (Jordan's national dish, lamb on rice with fermented yogurt sauce, at Sufra or any traditional restaurant), maqluba (rice and meat layered upside-down), knafeh (the sweet cheese pastry, best at Habibah in downtown). Modern: Beit Sitti (cooking-class restaurant), Sufra (upscale traditional), Hashem (downtown street-food institution, falafel and ful). Budget travelers eat well at hashem-style places for 3-5 JOD per meal.
