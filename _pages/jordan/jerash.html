JORDAN

Jerash

Jordan's other major archaeological site — and one of the best-preserved Roman provincial cities anywhere in the world. The Oval Plaza, South Theatre, and 800 meters of colonnaded street, 50km north of Amman. Here's how to do it right.

Last reviewed June 4, 2026 · 9-minute read · See our editorial standards

Jerash is the second great archaeological site in Jordan, and the one most travelers skip — usually because they're focused on Petra, occasionally because they assume "more Roman ruins" is redundant after the Pompeii or Ephesus they've already seen. Both assumptions sell Jerash short. The site is exceptionally well-preserved (better than Pompeii in several respects), distinctively positioned (you walk through a working Roman city rather than visiting individual buildings), and uniquely accessible — 50km north of Amman, easy half-day trip, free with the Jordan Pass you already have. For travelers spending more than 4 days in Jordan, Jerash earns a half-day.

This page covers what the site actually is, how to plan the visit, the monuments worth your time (and the ones that don't repay attention), and how Jerash fits into a wider Jordan trip. The framing throughout: Jerash is a sleeper hit. Most travelers leave more impressed than they expected to be.

What Jerash actually is

Jerash was a major city of the Roman Decapolis — a confederation of ten Hellenized cities established after Pompey's conquest of the region in 63 BCE. Originally called Gerasa, the city flourished from roughly the 1st through 6th centuries CE, reaching peak prosperity in the 2nd century under Roman emperor Hadrian (who visited in 129-130 CE and commissioned the triumphal arch at the city's southern gate). The site went into decline after the 749 CE Galilee earthquake, was abandoned for centuries, and was rediscovered in 1806 by the German explorer Ulrich Jasper Seetzen.

Archaeological excavation began in the 1920s and continues today. What you see when you visit is one of the most complete examples of a provincial Roman city anywhere — the layout intact, the major civic buildings standing, the colonnaded streets walkable. Unlike Pompeii (frozen by a specific catastrophe) or Ephesus (vast but partially reconstructed), Jerash gives you a working sense of how a Roman provincial city was actually organized — religious, civic, and residential zones, with major monuments visible from each other across the site.

The major monuments

Hadrian's Arch and the Hippodrome

The site entrance brings you in through Hadrian's Arch — a 13-meter triumphal arch built in 129 CE to mark the emperor's visit. Adjacent to it sits the Hippodrome, a 245-meter chariot-racing track that once held 15,000 spectators. The Hippodrome was partially reconstructed in the 1990s and now hosts a daily reenactment of Roman chariot racing and legionary drill demonstrations (entry typically included; check schedule on arrival). Skippable for serious archaeology travelers, fun for families.

The Oval Plaza (Forum)

The single most photographed feature of Jerash, and unique in Roman architecture — a 90 by 80 meter oval (not the standard rectangular forum) surrounded by 56 intact Ionic columns. The plaza sits at the junction of the cardo maximus and the road from the south gate, functioning as the city's commercial and ceremonial heart. The shape is unusual; no other Roman city had an oval forum of this scale. Spend 20-30 minutes here.

The cardo maximus

The main north-south colonnaded street running 800 meters through the city. Still surfaced with the original Roman paving stones, you can see the chariot ruts worn into the limestone — the visible wear from centuries of traffic, frozen in place. The cardo is lined with columns (mostly intact), commercial shopfronts on both sides, and the major civic buildings opening onto it. Walking the full length of the cardo, end to end, gives you a sense of Jerash's scale that no single monument can replicate.

The South Theatre

The larger of Jerash's two theatres (the other, the North Theatre, is smaller and less visited). The South Theatre seats 3,000, with full acoustic functionality intact — you can hear a coin drop on the stage from the back row, demonstrated daily by site guides. Climb to the upper seating for the best view back over the city. In summer, the theatre is one of the only shaded spots on the site.

The Temple of Artemis

Jerash's principal religious building, dedicated to the city's patron goddess. Eleven of the original twelve columns remain standing, each 12 meters tall. The temple sits on an elevated platform with a long stairway approach — the procession route from the cardo to the sanctuary, designed for ceremonial use. Worth the 15-minute walk and climb.

The Temple of Zeus and the lower temenos

Across the cardo from the Temple of Artemis, the Temple of Zeus sits on a higher platform with a more dramatic stairway approach. Less of the temple itself remains intact compared to Artemis, but the platform and the views back over the Oval Plaza make it worth the climb.

The Nymphaeum

A 2nd-century public fountain along the cardo, dedicated to the water nymphs. Two stories tall, with carved decoration still visible, the Nymphaeum was the main public water-display structure of the city — a flex of Jerash's engineering and water-management capacity. The basin at the front was once filled by an aqueduct; the architecture is what survives.

The honest visit plan

Most travelers visit Jerash as a half-day trip from Amman, leaving the city around 08:00 and back by 14:00. The realistic schedule:

  • 08:00: Depart Amman (private taxi 25-35 JOD, or rental car).
  • 09:00: Arrive Jerash, enter through Hadrian's Arch.
  • 09:00-09:30: Hadrian's Arch, Hippodrome (skim or skip).
  • 09:30-10:00: Oval Plaza — the photograph moment.
  • 10:00-11:00: Cardo maximus — walk the full length, stopping at the Nymphaeum and the major intersections.
  • 11:00-11:30: Temple of Artemis (climb to the top, look back at the cardo).
  • 11:30-12:00: South Theatre — climb to upper seats, listen to the acoustic demonstration.
  • 12:00-12:30: North Theatre and any monuments you skipped (optional).
  • 12:30-13:00: Lunch at the on-site restaurant or in Jerash town.
  • 13:00: Return to Amman.

For travelers willing to do a fuller day, add the Ajloun Castle (12th-century Ayyubid fortress, 35 km from Jerash, 90-minute visit) on the return trip to Amman. Many private drivers can be hired for a Jerash + Ajloun combination day for 60-80 JOD total, which works out cheaper per person than two separate trips.

Getting to Jerash

Private taxi from Amman: 25-35 JOD one-way, 50-60 minutes. The most common option. Most Amman hotels can arrange this in 30 minutes. Negotiate round-trip with waiting time (60-80 JOD total) so you have the same driver for the return.

Rental car: Straightforward drive. Route 35 north from Amman, well-signed. Parking at the site is free. About 50 km, 50-60 minutes depending on traffic.

JETT bus: JETT runs daily service from Amman's Abdali station to Jerash (typically 08:00 departure, returns mid-afternoon). Cost is about 8-12 JOD round-trip. The cheapest option but ties you to a fixed schedule.

Guided tour from Amman: Day tours from Amman covering Jerash + Ajloun + sometimes Umm Qais run $50-90 USD per person. Worth it for travelers who want a guide explaining the monuments — Jerash benefits from interpretation more than Petra does.

What Jerash isn't

Worth saying honestly to set expectations:

It's not Petra. Different culture, different scale, different experience. Jerash is a Roman city; Petra is a Nabataean ceremonial center. Travelers who expect Petra-level emotional impact at Jerash usually leave disappointed; travelers who expect a really good Roman archaeological site usually leave delighted.

It's not Pompeii. Pompeii has the immediacy of catastrophic preservation — the bodies, the carbonized loaves, the political graffiti frozen mid-sentence. Jerash is the slower-decline version: a city that emptied out over centuries, was rediscovered, and now reads as architecture rather than as snapshot-in-time.

It's not for everyone. If you've already seen multiple Roman archaeological sites, Jerash is incrementally rather than revelationally interesting. If Jerash would be your first major Roman site, it's a fantastic introduction.

Combining with the rest of Jordan

Jerash fits naturally into a 5-7 day Jordan itinerary as a half-day from Amman. The typical sequence: arrive Amman, Jerash + Ajloun day trip, Madaba/Mount Nebo day trip, drive south to Petra, two days at Petra, Wadi Rum overnight, return north or continue to Aqaba. See the seven-day Jordan itinerary for the full plan.

For travelers on shorter trips (3-4 days), Jerash is usually the first thing to cut — Petra and Wadi Rum take priority. For trips of 6+ days, Jerash should be on the list.

Frequently asked questions

How far is Jerash from Amman?
About 50 km north of Amman — roughly 50 minutes to 1 hour by car depending on traffic. Jerash is the easiest major Jordanian attraction to reach from the capital, making it a popular half-day trip. The route is on Route 35 north (the King's Highway extension) or Route 15 via the Desert Highway connector. Both are well-signed.
How much does Jerash cost to enter?
10 JOD for non-Jordanian visitors as of 2026. The site is also included free in the Jordan Pass (all three tiers — Wanderer, Explorer, Expert). For most travelers who are visiting Petra and using a Jordan Pass anyway, Jerash entry is essentially free, which makes the half-day trip easy to justify.
How long do I need at Jerash?
A focused visit covering the major monuments takes 2.5 to 3 hours. A thorough visit with rest stops and the small museum takes 4 to 5 hours. The site is large — about 1.5 km from end to end — and the walking is on uneven Roman paving stones in full sun. Most travelers underestimate the time, plan for 2 hours, and then either rush or get tired. Three hours minimum is realistic.
Can I do Jerash as a day trip from Petra?
Technically yes, practically no. Jerash is 280 km north of Petra (3.5 hours by car each way), which makes a same-day round trip a 12+ hour exhausting affair with maybe 3 hours on site. The better play is to combine Jerash with Amman — visit Jerash in the morning, Amman Citadel and downtown in the afternoon, and either return to Amman or continue south to Petra. See the seven-day Jordan itinerary for how this fits.
What's the highlight of Jerash?
The Oval Plaza (or Forum) is the most iconic single feature — a 90×80m oval surrounded by 56 Ionic columns, unique in Roman architecture. The South Theatre (3,000 seats, with the famous acoustic effect where you can hear a coin drop on the stage from the back row) is the most experiential. The cardo maximus (colonnaded main street, 800m of intact Roman paving with chariot ruts) gives you the best sense of walking through an actual Roman city. If you only have 90 minutes, hit those three in that order.
When should I visit Jerash?
Spring (March-May) and autumn (September-November) are the comfort sweet spots — moderate temperatures, manageable sun. Summer is hot (35-40°C with no shade on the main paths), so plan a 06:30 entry and aim to be off the site by 10:00. Winter is cold (8-15°C daytime) but generally clear and uncrowded; bring layers. The annual Jerash Festival in July brings concerts and theatre performances to the South Theatre — worth the experience if your visit overlaps.
