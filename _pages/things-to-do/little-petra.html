WHAT TO SEE

Little Petra (Siq al-Barid)

A smaller, quieter Nabataean site eight kilometers north of the main Petra entrance — free to enter, walkable in under an hour, and home to the only substantially surviving Nabataean wall paintings.

Little Petra — Siq al-Barid in Arabic, "the cold canyon" — sits about eight kilometers north of the main Petra Visitor Centre, near the village of Beidha. It is administered separately, has no entrance fee, and gets perhaps a tenth of the visitors that the main site sees. For travelers on a two-day or longer Petra visit, it makes one of the most rewarding half-day add-ons in Jordan: a small, walkable site that has most of the architectural elements of the main Petra at a manageable scale, plus one significant feature that the main site doesn't have — the painted Biclinium, with surviving 1st-century Nabataean wall paintings.

The contemporary archaeological consensus is that Little Petra was a Nabataean caravan staging post: the place where merchants arriving from the north (from Damascus, Bosra, and the trade routes through what is now Syria and Israel) would have rested, watered their camels, and been received with formal hospitality before being admitted to Petra proper. The site's smaller scale and dense concentration of triclinium spaces (carved feasting chambers) fit this interpretation. Petra was the destination; Little Petra was the front desk.

What you'll see

The site itself is a single slot canyon about 350 meters long, narrower than the main Siq but with the same general geometry. The walls reach perhaps 50 meters high, and the canyon floor is wide enough to walk three or four abreast comfortably. Along both sides of the canyon, several Nabataean facades and chambers are cut into the rock:

The triclinium chambers. Three or four large rectangular rooms cut into the rock, each with benches carved around three sides. These were almost certainly Nabataean feasting rooms — the bench arrangement is the classical Greek/Roman triclinium design, used for reclining banquets. The biggest of them could have seated 20 or more guests at a single meal.

The painted Biclinium. One of the upper-level chambers on the eastern side, accessible by a short flight of steps. The ceiling and upper walls retain substantial portions of original 1st-century Nabataean wall paintings — vines, flowers, birds, and small Hellenistic figures (putti or cherubs) painted in red, yellow, and blue pigments. This is the only substantially surviving example of Nabataean wall painting anywhere, and worth the trip to Little Petra on its own. Bring a flashlight; the chamber is dim and details are hard to see without one.

The water channels. Like the main Siq, the walls of Little Petra retain Nabataean water-management infrastructure. The channels are smaller and more eroded than those in the main site, but several are still visible.

The viewing platform. At the far end of the canyon, a set of carved steps leads up to a small viewing platform overlooking the surrounding hills. Worth the five-minute climb.

The Neolithic site at Beidha

A 5-minute walk from the Little Petra entrance brings you to one of the most important Neolithic archaeological sites in the Middle East: Beidha, an agricultural village dating to roughly 7,000 BCE. The site is partly reconstructed (the lower courses of several houses have been rebuilt to show the original layout) and is genuinely worth the additional time. Beidha is one of the oldest agricultural villages ever excavated, and visiting it after Petra produces an interesting time-depth — 9,000 years of continuous human presence in this small valley, of which the Nabataean monumental architecture is barely the middle.

Getting there

Three options. A taxi from Wadi Musa is the simplest — 10–15 JOD one-way, about 15 minutes by road. Most drivers will wait for you for an additional 5–10 JOD per hour and bring you back; arranging a round trip in advance is the easiest plan. Some Wadi Musa hotels can organize this directly.

The second option, for travelers with rental cars, is to drive yourself — the road is paved and well-signed, and Little Petra has a small parking lot at the entrance.

The third — and most interesting for hikers — is the back-route trail from Little Petra to the Monastery in the main Petra site. This 6-kilometer trail enters Petra from the north and emerges at the back of the Monastery plateau, skipping the 800-step climb. It takes 3 to 4 hours and is one of the great walking routes in the region. The catch: you exit at the Monastery, which means you still have to walk the full length of Petra and back through the Siq to get out (or hire a Bedouin guide to walk you in the opposite direction). For most travelers, the taxi-and-walk approach is the better choice; for serious hikers, the back-route trail is one of the trip's high points.

When to visit

A half-day on either Day 2 or Day 3 of a multi-day Petra visit. Late afternoon is the more rewarding time — the canyon faces east and gets nice late-afternoon light on the western wall, and the temperature is more comfortable than midday. Bring a flashlight for the painted Biclinium.

Frequently asked questions

Where is Little Petra?
About 8 kilometers north of the main Petra Visitor Centre, near the village of Beidha. The site is officially called Siq al-Barid ("the cold canyon") and is sometimes also called Beidha after the nearby Neolithic settlement. Most visitors reach it by taxi from Wadi Musa (10–15 JOD one-way, about 15 minutes) or by tour vehicle as part of a half-day add-on.
Is Little Petra free?
Yes. Unlike the main Petra site, Little Petra has no entrance fee. The site is open daylight hours and is administered separately from the main archaeological park. No tickets, no Jordan Pass requirement, no booth.
Is it worth visiting?
For two-day or three-day Petra visitors, yes. Little Petra has all the elements of the main site at a smaller scale — a slot canyon entrance, carved facades, ancient water channels, and Nabataean ritual triclinium spaces — and you can walk the entire site in under an hour. It works particularly well as a quieter half-day add-on after a busy day at the main site. The painted Biclinium, with surviving 1st-century Nabataean wall paintings, is unique to Little Petra and worth the trip on its own merits.
How long do I need at Little Petra?
1 to 2 hours. The site is a single slot canyon about 350 meters long, with tomb facades and triclinium chambers on either side. Walking the canyon and climbing the steps to the upper-level facades (including the painted Biclinium) takes about 45 minutes. Most travelers also visit the nearby Neolithic site of Beidha as part of the same trip, which adds another 30–45 minutes.
What was Little Petra for?
Most likely a Nabataean caravan staging post — the place where merchants and their goods arrived from the north before being processed and admitted into Petra proper. The carved triclinium spaces would have housed feasts for visiting traders, and the smaller scale of the architecture suggests it was a working logistics hub rather than a ceremonial site. The relationship between Little Petra and Petra is roughly analogous to a major medieval city and its caravanserai.
Is the painted Biclinium really visible?
Yes, though it requires looking carefully. The painted ceiling is on the upper level of one of the chambers on the eastern side of the canyon, accessed by a short flight of steps. The paintings — vines, flowers, birds, and small figures of putti or cherubs — are 1st-century Nabataean work and the only substantially surviving examples of Nabataean wall painting anywhere. The colors are faded but the details are visible with a flashlight (which most visitors do not bring, and which is necessary for full appreciation).
