GETTING THERE

How to get to Petra from Eilat

The Wadi Araba border crossing logistics, the 130 JOD in combined fees that catches travelers off guard, the realistic timing for a day trip versus an overnight, and the honest version of which one you should actually do.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 9-minute read · See our editorial standards

The Eilat-to-Petra crossing is one of the more interesting border experiences in the Middle East — a short walk between two countries that are visible from each other but operate completely different visa, currency, and logistics regimes. It is also one of the most common ways travelers reach Petra, particularly from Israel-focused trips that add Petra as a one- or two-day extension. The crossing itself is straightforward but slow, the fees are substantial, and the math of "day trip vs. overnight" works out very differently than most travelers expect.

This page covers what you actually need to know: which border to use, what the fees cost, how long the crossing takes, what to do on the Jordanian side, and whether the day-trip version of this is genuinely worth doing or whether you should bite the bullet and overnight in Wadi Musa.

The Wadi Araba / Yitzhak Rabin crossing

The crossing sits about 5 km north of central Eilat, easily reached by taxi (10–15 minutes, ~50 NIS). On the Israeli side, it's called the Yitzhak Rabin Border Terminal; on the Jordanian side, the Wadi Araba crossing. The two terminals are connected by a 200-meter walkway through no-man's-land. You exit Israeli customs, walk the strip, and enter Jordanian customs — three distinct processes.

Border hours:

  • Sunday–Thursday: 06:30 to 20:00
  • Friday: 08:00 to 20:00
  • Saturday: Closed (Israeli Shabbat)
  • Closed on: Yom Kippur, eves of Rosh Hashanah, Passover, and Sukkot (Israeli holidays)
  • Hours may shift during Ramadan on the Jordanian side

Always check both governments' official sources before traveling, particularly if your trip falls near a holiday weekend. The Saturday closure catches a meaningful share of travelers — you cannot do Petra as a Saturday day trip from Eilat regardless of how you plan it.

The fees, in detail

Israeli exit fee: Approximately 110 NIS (~$30 USD, ~22 JOD) per person, collected at the Israeli side before you can exit. Payable in shekels, US dollars, or by credit card.

Jordanian entry visa: 60 JOD (~$85 USD) per person at the Wadi Araba crossing. Note this is higher than the standard 40 JOD visa-on-arrival fee at Amman or Aqaba airports — the land border charges a premium. Payable in JOD, US dollars, or credit card.

Or: The Jordan Pass. If you've purchased a Jordan Pass before arriving and you plan to stay at least two nights in Jordan, the pass waives the visa fee entirely. The pass costs 70/75/80 JOD depending on tier and includes Petra entry. For travelers planning to overnight in Wadi Musa, the Jordan Pass is almost always the right financial choice. See the Jordan Pass page for the full math.

Combined cost for a day trip without Jordan Pass: Roughly 130 JOD ($185 USD) per person in border fees, plus 90 JOD ($128 USD) for Petra entry (the non-accommodated rate), plus transport. For a couple, the day-trip total runs $700+ before factoring in tour operators or private transport — comparable to the cost of two nights at a mid-range Wadi Musa hotel with the standard 50 JOD entry rate.

On the Jordanian side

Once you've cleared Jordanian customs, you exit into a small parking area where Jordanian taxi drivers wait. Three onward options:

Direct private taxi to Petra: 80–100 JOD for the car (up to 4 passengers). Roughly 2 hours travel time. The cleanest option — agree the fare at the border, ride directly to your Wadi Musa hotel or to the Petra Visitor Centre.

Shared taxi to Petra: 25–35 JOD per person, pools 3–4 travelers as they emerge from the border. Faster than the bus, cheaper than a private taxi for solo or paired travelers.

Taxi to Aqaba, then JETT bus to Petra: 25 JOD taxi to central Aqaba + 22 JOD JETT bus + waiting time. Cheapest in absolute terms but slow (3+ hours total) and the bus schedule may not match your border-crossing arrival time. Most travelers don't bother with this option.

If you've booked through a tour operator from Eilat, your Jordanian driver will be waiting in the parking area with a sign showing your name. This is the smoothest option for first-timers.

The day trip — honest version

A day trip from Eilat to Petra is possible but compressed. The realistic schedule:

  • 05:30: Wake, taxi to Yitzhak Rabin border
  • 06:30: Border opens, begin crossing
  • 08:00: Clear Jordanian side, taxi to Petra
  • 10:00: Arrive at Petra Visitor Centre, buy 90 JOD ticket
  • 10:15–15:00: Visit Petra (4 hours 45 minutes on site)
  • 15:30: Begin taxi back to border
  • 17:30: Arrive at border
  • 19:00: Cross back into Israel
  • 19:30: Back in Eilat

That's 14 hours door-to-door for under 5 hours on site. You skip the Monastery (no time), the Royal Tombs in good afternoon light (you've left before 14:00), and Petra by Night entirely. You pay the higher 90 JOD non-accommodated entry rate. If anything goes wrong — the morning border queue is longer than expected, a flat tire, an issue at the gate — you risk missing the 20:00 border closure.

The alternative: stay one or two nights in Wadi Musa. The math runs roughly as follows. Day trip: 130 JOD border fees + 90 JOD Petra ticket + ~80 JOD round-trip transport = 300 JOD per person, with 5 hours on site. Two-night extension: 130 JOD border fees one-way + 50 JOD two-day Petra ticket + ~150 JOD round-trip transport + 200 JOD/night × 2 mid-range hotel = roughly 580 JOD per person, but with a real Petra experience including Monastery, Petra by Night, and morning light at the Treasury. For an extra 280 JOD (~$400 USD), you transform a partial-day visit into the version of Petra worth flying internationally for.

With a Jordan Pass, the math gets even better: the 80 JOD Jordan Expert pass (3-day Petra) plus zero visa fee at the border (since you're staying 2+ nights) saves you roughly 70 JOD per person versus paying separately.

Tour operators that handle the crossing

For travelers who want the whole logistics package handled, several Israeli and Jordanian operators run organized Petra day trips from Eilat. Typical pricing: $180–280 USD per person for a day trip, $350–500 USD per person for a one-night version.

Worth knowing: the day-trip operators handle the border crossing administratively, but you still pay the 110 NIS Israeli exit fee and the 60 JOD Jordanian visa yourself (the tour fare typically covers the Petra entrance, transport, guide, and lunch, but not the government fees). Confirm what's included before booking. We compare the major operators on the Petra tours from Eilat page.

The Sabbath problem

The Wadi Araba border is closed Saturdays for Israeli Shabbat. This catches travelers whose flights into Eilat land Friday night and who want to do Petra on Saturday — it isn't possible. The workaround is to do Petra on Sunday or to fly into Eilat earlier in the week.

The same closure applies to the King Hussein/Allenby crossing near the Dead Sea, though that crossing is more relevant for Jerusalem-to-Petra travelers than Eilat-to-Petra travelers.

Frequently asked questions

Which border crossing do I use from Eilat to Petra?
The Wadi Araba border crossing (called Yitzhak Rabin Border Terminal on the Israeli side, Wadi Araba on the Jordanian side). It sits about 5 km north of central Eilat and connects directly to Aqaba on the Jordanian side. This is the only one of Jordan's three Israel border crossings that's practical for Petra trips — the others (Jordan River/Sheikh Hussein in the north, Allenby/King Hussein near Jerusalem) are far less convenient.
What are the border crossing hours?
Sunday through Thursday 06:30 to 20:00; Friday 08:00 to 20:00; closed Saturday (Israeli Shabbat) and on several Israeli national holidays per year (notably Yom Kippur and the eves of Rosh Hashanah, Passover, and Sukkot). The hours can shift during Ramadan on the Jordanian side. Check both governments' official sources before traveling, and plan to arrive at the crossing at least 60 minutes before closing time.
How much does the border crossing cost?
Israeli exit fee: around 110 NIS (~$30 USD) per person. Jordanian entry visa: 60 JOD (~$85 USD) per person — unless you have a Jordan Pass, which waives the visa fee IF you stay at least two nights in Jordan. The combined cost of crossing one-way without a Jordan Pass is roughly 130 JOD ($185 USD) per person, which is one of the reasons we strongly recommend extending Petra trips to two nights and using a Jordan Pass.
How long does the border crossing take?
Plan for 90 minutes to 2 hours, particularly in the morning when most day-trippers cross. The process: exit Israel (passport check, exit fee, customs), walk across the no-man's land between the two terminals (about 200 meters), enter Jordan (passport check, visa fee, customs, sometimes a baggage scan). It can move faster in afternoon and weekday off-peak times. Build at least 2 hours into your itinerary for the crossing alone, more if you're crossing on a holiday or in summer peak season.
Can I do Petra as a day trip from Eilat?
Yes, but with significant trade-offs. The day-trip math: 90 minutes border crossing each way (3 hours total), 2 hours each way from the border to Petra (4 hours total), leaves you about 4–5 hours on site. You pay the 90 JOD non-accommodated entry fee instead of 50 JOD. You miss the Monastery, the afternoon light, and Petra by Night. You're back at the Eilat border at 22:00 if everything goes smoothly. For travelers who can possibly extend to one or two nights in Jordan, the experience is dramatically better and the math is similar (the Jordan Pass plus a budget Wadi Musa hotel often comes out cheaper than the day-trip premium).
Is it worth getting a tour operator to handle the crossing?
For first-timers, yes. Tour operators that pick up at your Eilat hotel and handle the border crossing typically charge $180–280 USD per person for a one-day Petra trip. The premium gets you: a Jordanian driver waiting at the border (so you skip the chaotic taxi negotiation), a guide at Petra (so you skip hiring one there), and a smoother re-entry process. Worth it if you're a one-time visitor; less worth it if you're doing the route regularly or want to overnight in Jordan.
