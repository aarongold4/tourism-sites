Mother Teresa & Skopje

The saint of Calcutta was born by the Vardar — and Skopje keeps her doorstep.

HomeHistory & CultureMother Teresa & Skopje

On 26 August 1910, in an Ottoman trading city on the Vardar, Anjezë Gonxhe Bojaxhiu was born to a prosperous Albanian Catholic family — grocer-merchant father, devout mother, a parish life centered on the Sacred Heart church. Eighteen years later she left for Ireland, then Calcutta, and the world came to know her as Mother Teresa. Skopje, which lost her church to the 1963 earthquake and her to history, keeps the doorstep faithfully.

The Memorial House

Opened in 2010 on Macedonia Street — near the vanished church of her baptism, its floor plan traced in the plaza — the Memorial House is deliberately modest: ground-floor exhibits of photographs, letters, her sari and rosary among relics and replicas; a glass-and-stone chapel above where mass is still said. Entry is free, the visit takes well under an hour, and its quietness lands harder than monumentality would, two minutes from the Alexander fountain's bombast.

The trail through town

Completists can collect the rest: the memorial plaque marking her birthplace site, her statue near the Stone Bridge approach, and the feast-day observances each September 5 (her death anniversary and feast) and August 26 (her birthday), when the city's Catholic minority and civic Skopje briefly share a calendar. The 2016 canonization made her St. Teresa of Calcutta; Skopje's signage adopted the title with proprietary speed.

The shared inheritance

Her memory is gracefully multi-flagged — Albanian by family, Skopje-born, Indian by life's work — and the Memorial House handles the layers without forcing a verdict, much as she did. As a visit it pairs naturally with the square district and the City Museum's stopped clock: three small rooms of the century, two minutes apart — the saint, the earthquake, and the city that absorbed both.

Frequently asked questions

Was Mother Teresa Macedonian?

She was born in Skopje in 1910 — then an Ottoman city — to an Albanian Catholic family, left at 18 and became a citizen of India. North Macedonia, Albania and India all honor her with cause; she answered the question herself: "By blood I am Albanian, by citizenship an Indian... as to my heart, I belong entirely to the Heart of Jesus."

Is the Memorial House worth visiting?

Yes — small, free and genuinely affecting: 30–40 minutes among personal effects, photographs and letters, with a chapel above. It stands on Macedonia Street near the site of the Sacred Heart church where she was baptized, destroyed in the 1963 earthquake.

What are the opening details?

Central location steps from Macedonia Square, free entry (donations welcome), generally open daily with shorter weekend hours — verify times locally, as they shift. Modest dress for the chapel floor.
