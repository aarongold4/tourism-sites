WHAT TO WEAR

What to wear at Petra

Trail runners or light hiking shoes, layers for the temperature swings, and modest-but-not-strict dress for the cultural context. Here's the practical breakdown by season and time of day.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 6-minute read · See our editorial standards

Petra dressing has two competing constraints: the physical demands of the site (12 km of walking on uneven stone, 800 steps to the Monastery, temperature swings from cold mornings to hot afternoons) and the cultural context (Jordan is a Muslim-majority country with modest-dress norms, though Petra itself is a tourist site with relaxed standards). The headline answer: dress comfortably for moderate hiking, with a slight lean toward more rather than less coverage. The detail below covers what changes by season, time of day, and specific traveler considerations.

Footwear — the only thing you can't get wrong

Petra walking is moderate hiking, not sightseeing. The Monastery trail has 800 stone steps, many of them uneven and polished smooth by 2,000 years of foot traffic. The High Place trail is steeper and similar. The lower city has long stretches of compact dirt mixed with loose sand and rocks. Even the Siq, which is paved, has a slightly uneven floor where the original Nabataean cobbles meet modern restoration.

The right shoe is a closed-toe, gripped, comfortable walking or trail shoe. Trail runners (Salomon Speedcross, Hoka Speedgoat, Altra Lone Peak, etc.) are ideal. Light hiking shoes (Merrell Moab, Keen Targhee) are excellent. Standard sneakers (running shoes, cross-trainers) work if that's what you have. The categorically wrong choices: open sandals, flip-flops, dress shoes with leather soles, high heels. The site authorities will let you in wearing any of these but the Monastery trail with sandals is a hospital trip waiting to happen.

If your shoes aren't broken in, break them in before you arrive. Petra blister rates spike for travelers wearing brand-new hiking shoes for the first time. A day of moderate walking at home before the trip makes a real difference.

Layering for the temperature swings

The single most consistent mistake travelers make at Petra is dressing for the afternoon when they enter at 06:00. The morning canyon temperature is typically 8–15°C (46–60°F) — significantly colder than the 25–30°C (77–86°F) afternoon. A long-sleeve shirt or light fleece for the early morning, removed and packed in your daypack by 09:30, is the right pattern.

The standard recommended layering, for spring or autumn:

  • Base: Moisture-wicking T-shirt (synthetic or merino, not cotton — cotton retains sweat and chills you when you stop moving)
  • Mid: Long-sleeve shirt or light fleece for the early morning
  • Outer: Light packable windbreaker for the Siq breeze and dust
  • Bottom: Lightweight hiking pants or shorts (pants more practical for sun protection and cultural fit)

In winter, add a proper warm jacket and gloves. In summer, drop the long-sleeve mid-layer and reduce to a sun-protective long-sleeve top.

Cultural considerations

Jordan is more relaxed about dress than several neighboring Muslim-majority countries, and Petra specifically is a tourist site with international norms. There's no formal dress code at Petra; tank tops, shorts, and yoga pants are all common. That said, the cultural lean — and the smarter ethical position — is toward modest dress.

For women: knees and shoulders covered out of respect for local cultural norms is appreciated but not required. Long lightweight pants or a longer skirt with a T-shirt covers the basic baseline; a long-sleeve shirt over a tank top works for the same effect. Headscarves are not required at Petra (they are at mosques, including the King Abdullah Mosque if you visit it in Amman). Most importantly: the local Bedouin women working at Petra dress modestly, and dressing closer to their norm is a small gesture of cultural respect.

For men: shorts are acceptable but long pants are more practical for the trail anyway. Tank tops are technically acceptable but rarely seen on local men; a T-shirt is the more comfortable cultural fit. Avoid anything with religious or political imagery.

The dress code is meaningfully stricter in Wadi Musa town in the evenings — particularly during Ramadan — where you'll be eating in family restaurants. Long pants and a sleeved shirt is the practical norm there.

The sun protection problem

The combination of sandstone reflectance, high altitude (Petra sits at 800–900 meters), and minimal natural shade makes the Jordanian sun stronger than most travelers expect. Sunburn at Petra is common even on visits travelers planned around "winter weather." Sun protection through clothing is more effective than just sunscreen.

The practical additions:

  • A wide-brimmed hat or buff. Baseball caps leave your neck and ears exposed.
  • Sunglasses with full UV protection. Cheap sunglasses are worse than no sunglasses (they reduce pupil constriction without filtering UV).
  • A long-sleeve sun shirt for the Monastery trail in summer (Columbia, Patagonia, and most outdoor brands sell UPF 50+ versions in lightweight breathable fabric).
  • Light-colored clothing. White or pale blue reflects heat better than dark colors. The local Bedouin men's traditional dress is light gray or white for exactly this reason.

Petra by Night dress code

The evening experience requires its own layering. Petra at night, particularly October through March, is genuinely cold — canyon temperatures regularly drop below 10°C (50°F), and the Treasury plaza has no shelter or seating with back support.

Recommended:

  • Warm pants (jeans or hiking pants, not athletic shorts)
  • Thermal base layer if you're cold-sensitive
  • A sweater or fleece
  • A proper jacket (not just a windbreaker)
  • A beanie or buff for your head
  • A light scarf for your neck (the breeze in the Siq is real)
  • Comfortable walking shoes — same as daytime, the 1.2 km Siq walk doesn't change at night

Seasonal summary

Spring (March–May): Light layers, long pants, sun protection. The default Petra dress.

Summer (June–August): Lightweight light-colored clothing, full sun protection, sun-protective long-sleeve top, generous water. Mornings still benefit from a thin long-sleeve.

Autumn (September–November): Same as spring. October is the consensus best month for dress as well as weather — comfortable without extremes either direction.

Winter (December–February): Real winter clothing. Proper warm jacket, hat, gloves, layered base. Daytime highs can be deceiving — the temperature drops fast in late afternoon, and Petra by Night in winter can feel below freezing in the Siq.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to dress modestly at Petra?
Jordan is a Muslim-majority country and modest dress is the cultural norm, but the Petra archaeological park is a tourist site with relaxed standards. For women, knees and shoulders covered is appreciated but not required at Petra specifically. For men, shorts are acceptable but long pants are more comfortable for the trails anyway. The strictest dress codes apply at religious sites (mosques, the King Abdullah Mosque in Amman) — Petra is significantly more relaxed.
What shoes should I wear?
Trail runners or light hiking shoes are ideal — closed-toe, with grip, ankle support optional. Sneakers work if you don't have hiking shoes. Sandals are genuinely dangerous on the Monastery and High Place trails; the stone steps are uneven and polished smooth, and any slip risks an ankle. Dress shoes (heels, leather soles) are not just impractical but unsafe. Bring the shoes you'd wear on a moderate hike at home.
Will I need warm clothes?
In winter (December–February), yes — daytime highs of 13–15°C (55–59°F) with nighttime lows near freezing, and the early-morning Siq walk is genuinely cold. A warm jacket (not just a fleece), gloves, and a beanie make sense. In summer (June–August), no — but bring a sun-protective long-sleeve shirt anyway. In spring and autumn, a light fleece or pullover for early morning is enough.
Should I bring a swimsuit?
Not for Petra itself. If you're also visiting the Dead Sea, Wadi Mujib, or Aqaba, yes — but pack it separately. Many Wadi Musa hotels have pools (the Mövenpick, the Marriott, several mid-range options) and you can swim there in the evenings after a day at Petra; bring a swimsuit if your hotel has one.
Is a scarf or buff worth it?
Yes. A buff — the cylindrical multi-functional cloth — is one of the most useful items for Petra. Use it as: a sun shield around your neck, a face cover against blowing sand in the Siq, a head wrap if you forgot your hat, a sweat absorber on the Monastery climb. Cheap, packable, multi-use. The Bedouin shemagh (checkered Jordanian headscarf) serves the same purposes and makes a decent souvenir.
What about Petra by Night specifically?
Layers. The temperature at the Treasury plaza on a winter evening can be 5–10°C (40–50°F), and there's no shelter or seating with backs. Wear: warm pants, a thermal base layer, a sweater, a proper jacket, a hat, and a light scarf. Skip the bulky boots — comfortable shoes for the 1.2 km Siq walk matter more than warm feet.
