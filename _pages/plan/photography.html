Photographing Giza, properly

The pyramids are the most photographed objects on Earth and still routinely photographed badly — wrong hour, wrong spot, wrong fights with the rules. Here's the light, the five compositions, and the regulations as they actually run.

Last reviewed June 6, 2026◆7-minute read

Photo: Ricardo Liberato · CC BY-SA 2.0 · Wikimedia Commons

Everything difficult about photographing Giza is scheduling, not technique: the monuments are enormous, immobile, and lit by the most reliable sun on the planet. Put yourself at the right spot at the right hour and a phone produces keepers; fight the light and no lens saves you. The schedule below is the whole secret.

The light, hour by hour

7-9am: warm side-light rakes the east faces — texture hour for the masonry, and the only hours the Sphinx's face is properly lit. Work close: bases, casing stones, the Sphinx enclosure. 9am-2pm: vertical glare flattens everything monumental; shoot details, people, interiors, or nothing. 2-4pm: the viewpoint's hour — sun behind you, looking northeast, the full stack modeled in deepening warmth until the 4pm sweep ends the day. There is no in-site sunset or sunrise (hours run 7-4); the golden-hour pyramid shots you've seen are from rooftops and the desert outside — which is the rooftop strategy's photographic payoff.

The five compositions

1. The nine-pyramid stack from the panoramic viewpoint, slight telephoto compressing the rows. 2. The Sphinx with Khafre rising behind, from the northern overlook — the complex's designed alignment (the spots). 3. The base-of-Khufu vertical: courses of stone filling the frame to a vanishing apex, a person at the bottom for scale. 4. The caravan crossing: camels in the middle distance against the stack — wait two minutes at the viewpoint and one obliges. 5. The rooftop nightscape: the floodlit skyline (and the show's colors) from a Sphinx-gate terrace, the one tripod shot nobody regulates.

The rules, as they actually run

Personal photography in the open is free and unrestricted. The contested zone is equipment and interiors: tripods and pro rigs cycle through permit-required, tolerated, and refused — ask at the ticket point your morning; interiors rotate between phone-only, paid permits, and bans — ask when buying; drones are banned nationally, full stop, including in your luggage. Two etiquette rules carry more weight than any regulation: people are a permission transaction (ask first; expect the small-tip economy around the photogenic camel men — part of the agreed total), and security infrastructure is never a subject.

Gear worth its weight

A phone with good HDR genuinely covers the plateau. Beyond it, in earning order: a 24-70-equivalent zoom (the do-everything), a 70-200 for the stack compression and Khafre's summit casing, a polarizer for the relentless sky, and double the cards and batteries you planned (heat eats both). Leave: the tripod unless committed (see above), the bag bigger than the interiors allow, and anything you'd mourn in sand. Full kit logic in what to bring.

Frequently asked questions

Are tripods allowed at the pyramids?

Intermittently and inconsistently — tripod and 'professional equipment' policies rotate, sometimes requiring permits, sometimes waved through, sometimes refused at the gate. The working approach: travel handheld-first (modern stabilization covers the plateau's light), and if a tripod matters to you, ask at the ticket point that morning rather than trusting any page's snapshot.

Can you fly a drone at Giza?

No — drones are banned in Egypt generally and at antiquities sites absolutely, with confiscation at customs a routine traveler report. Don't bring one into the country; the aerial shots you've seen are licensed productions.

Is photography allowed inside the pyramids?

Interior policies rotate between phone-only, paid photo permits, and outright bans — and enforcement varies by attendant. Ask when buying the interior ticket. Honestly: the passages are dim, tight, and better experienced than photographed; the corbelled ceilings of the Red Pyramid are the one interior worth fighting for.

What's the single best photo spot?

The panoramic viewpoint, 2-4pm, sun behind you: the nine-pyramid stack in warm light, camels available for the foreground. Second place: the Sphinx overlook at 8am, face lit, Khafre rising behind. Both are covered with compositions in the viewpoint and Sphinx guides.
