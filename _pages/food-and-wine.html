Food & Wine

Tbilisi food & wine

Georgia's single strongest argument for the flight. Eight thousand years of winemaking, a feast culture with its own constitution, and a capital where eating brilliantly costs less than eating badly does elsewhere.

Last reviewed: June 7, 2026

Start with the claim, because it's literal: Georgia is where wine began. Residue on Neolithic pottery from sites south of Tbilisi pushes winemaking here back roughly eight thousand years, and the method those first vintners used — fermentation in large clay vessels called qvevri, buried to the neck in the earth — never stopped. It survived empires, phylloxera, and seventy years of Soviet industrial planning, and UNESCO now lists it as intangible cultural heritage. In Tbilisi, that deep history is not a museum exhibit; it's what's in your glass at the bar around the corner.

The food carries the same unbroken confidence. Georgian cuisine sits at a crossroads of Persian, Turkish, and Black Sea influences and answers to none of them — walnuts ground into sauces, herbs used in fistfuls rather than pinches, cheese baked into bread, dumplings engineered to carry soup. The capital's restaurant scene now runs from grandmother-style cellar taverns to new-wave kitchens reinterpreting the canon, and the remarkable part is the floor, not the ceiling: it is genuinely hard to eat badly in Tbilisi if you follow locals rather than laminated menus.

The canon, briefly

Two dishes anchor every first visit. Khinkali — pleated dumplings holding spiced meat (or mushroom, or cheese) in a pocket of broth — come with a technique: hold the twisted top, bite a window, drink the soup, then eat, abandoning the doughy handle on the plate as everyone does. Khachapuri is a family, not a dish; the boat-shaped Adjarian version with its raw egg and butter stirred into molten cheese is the famous one, the round Imeretian the everyday one, and choosing a favorite is a legitimate trip activity. Around these orbit the walnut-and-herb cold table — badrijani, pkhali, jonjoli — the bean pot lobio, and grilled mtsvadi, with churchkhela (walnuts dipped in thickened grape juice) hanging in every market as the original energy bar.

The wine scene now

Tbilisi's natural wine bars are the easiest entry point into a country with more than five hundred native grape varieties. The pattern of an evening: a flight of qvevri-made amber wines, a saperavi or two from the red side, bar staff who treat explanation as part of the pour, and prices that encourage curiosity. Sololaki and Vera hold the densest concentrations, and the bar page keeps a current shortlist — the scene moves fast enough that naming names here would age badly. For going to the source, the Kakheti day trip in the day trips cluster is the move.

The supra, the institution

Sooner or later you'll meet the supra — the Georgian feast, governed by a toastmaster called the tamada, where toasts proceed in a meaningful order (peace, family, the departed, friendship, and onward) and the table fills past any structural logic. It can be a formal banquet or four friends and a guesthouse kitchen; the grammar is the same. Guests need only two rules: toasts are drunk with wine, never beer, and a toast is an offering, not a drinking contest — sincerity outranks volume. The supra page covers the rest, including how to gracefully survive a tamada who has decided you are the guest of honor.

How eating out actually works

Georgian meals are shared, not plated. Dishes arrive when they're ready rather than in courses, the table fills rather than progresses, and over-ordering slightly is the expected form — a half-empty table reads as stinginess, a groaning one as hospitality. Order family-style for the whole group, lead with a couple of cold pkhali and a badrijani, add a khachapuri to share and khinkali by the half-dozen, and leave room you won't actually have. Bread is central and near-constant; the clay-oven tone bread and the cornbread mchadi both turn up without asking.

The price math stays gentle. Eating extremely well in Tbilisi costs a fraction of the Western equivalent — a generous sit-down dinner with wine lands in single-digit to low-double-digit dollars per person at most local restaurants, and the markets and bakeries make eating brilliantly the budget option rather than the splurge. Cards are accepted at most restaurants, but carry small cash for bakeries, market stalls, and the churchkhela sellers. Tipping is gentle: many places fold a service charge into the bill (check before adding more), and where they don't, rounding up or roughly ten percent is generous.

Two cultural notes worth carrying to the table. First, the supra — the structured feast with its toastmaster and toasts — is the form Georgian eating aspires to, and even a casual dinner carries its DNA. Second, wine is treated as food, not accessory: the qvevri tradition means the house pour is often a serious amber or saperavi, and asking the staff what they're drinking this month is the fastest route to the good stuff.

Frequently asked questions

What dishes should I try first?

Khinkali (the broth-filled dumplings — twisted top in hand, bite, sip, then eat, and leave the doughy knob on the plate) and khachapuri adjaruli, the boat-shaped cheese bread with egg and butter stirred in at the table. After those, the vegetable openers: badrijani (walnut-stuffed eggplant), pkhali, and a tomato-cucumber salad under walnut sauce. The full field guide ranks everything.

What is amber wine?

White wine fermented on its skins in buried clay qvevri, emerging deep gold, tannic, and unlike any conventional white. It’s Georgia’s eight-thousand-year signature, the method is UNESCO-listed heritage, and Tbilisi’s wine bars are the easiest place on earth to learn it glass by glass.

Is Tbilisi good for vegetarians?

Surprisingly excellent. The Orthodox fasting tradition built a deep repertoire of vegan-by-design dishes — bean stews, walnut pastes, mushroom khinkali, grilled vegetables — so a vegetarian eats wonderfully at any traditional table. Strict vegans should ask about cheese and egg in breads; the dish guide flags everything.

Do I need restaurant reservations?

For the famous rooms, yes — especially Thursday through Saturday evenings. The neighborhood places mostly don’t need them. The pattern that works: book one or two destination dinners when you book flights, and improvise the rest.

How does tipping work in restaurants?

Check the bill first — many places add a service percentage automatically. Where it isn’t added, around ten percent is generous and appreciated. Nobody will chase you over it either way; tipping culture here is relaxed.
