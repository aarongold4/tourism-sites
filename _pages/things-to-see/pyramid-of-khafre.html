The Pyramid of Khafre

The pyramid in most people's Giza photographs isn't the Great Pyramid — it's this one, standing higher on the plateau, rising more steeply, and wearing the last surviving piece of the white casing that once covered them all.

Khafre was Khufu's son, and his pyramid — built around 2530 BC — is the ancient world's most successful piece of sibling positioning. It is genuinely the second pyramid: about 136 meters originally to its father's 146, with a base about 15 meters shorter per side. But Khafre's architects set it on ground roughly 10 meters higher and pitched its faces at a steeper angle, and the result is that from almost every classic viewpoint, the second pyramid dominates the skyline. The silhouette on the postcards, the one behind the Sphinx, the one wearing the white cap — all Khafre.

The casing cap

That cap is the most historically valuable square meterage at Giza. All three pyramids were originally sheathed in polished white Tura limestone; nearly all of it was stripped through the medieval period to build Cairo. Khafre's upper courses kept theirs — too high, presumably, to be worth the trouble — and so the summit preserves, in place, what the entire plateau looked like for its first three millennia: smooth planes meeting in knife edges, blazing white. Binoculars or a long lens reward you here; from below you can see where the smooth sheath breaks off into the stepped core like a tide line.

The complex: the most complete at Giza

Khafre's is the best-preserved pyramid complex on the plateau, and walking it end to end is the clearest lesson available in how these monuments actually worked. From the pyramid's east face, the ruined mortuary temple — where the king's cult was maintained — connects to a causeway running down the slope to the granite-built valley temple at the plateau's edge, beside which crouches the Sphinx, carved from the quarry that supplied Khafre's builders and bearing what has long been read as his portrait. The valley temple is the architectural sleeper of Giza: monolithic red granite pillars and alabaster floors, the chamber where the famous diorite statue of Khafre (now a star of the Egyptian Museum) was found, and the route most visitors walk through to reach the Sphinx enclosure without registering what the building is.

The interior, when open

Interior access rotates between Khafre and Menkaure for conservation — which one is open, and at what price, changes seasonally (recently around 280 EGP for Khafre; current tiers in our fees guide). Khafre's route is a single descending passage to a long burial chamber under the pyramid's mass, holding the king's black granite sarcophagus — empty, like all of them, since antiquity. On its wall, in large 19th-century lettering, is the autograph of Giovanni Belzoni, the circus strongman turned excavator who forced the pyramid open in 1818 and could not resist signing his work. There's no Grand Gallery here, and that's the honest comparison with the Great Pyramid's interior; what Khafre offers instead is the same essential experience — descent, mass, chamber, sarcophagus — at a fraction of the cost, usually in something close to solitude.

Seeing it well

Khafre anchors the two best compositions at Giza: directly behind the Sphinx from the enclosure's southeast (the alignment the complex was designed around), and as the apparent-tallest peak in the nine-pyramid panorama from the desert viewpoint. Up close, the southwest corner offers the plateau's best surviving impression of pyramid-plus-casing in a single upward look. Like everything here, it's at its best in the first and last hours of the site day (opening hours).

The complex around the pyramid

Khafre's real distinction isn't the pyramid alone but the completeness of everything attached to it — his is the one Giza complex you can still read end to end. The valley temple by the Sphinx is the masterpiece: monolithic red-granite pillars on an alabaster floor, the austere geometry that defines Fourth Dynasty architecture, and the pits where Mariette's excavators found the diorite Khafre — twenty-three statue emplacements originally, one survivor, now the Tahrir museum's defining object. From it the causeway runs nearly half a kilometer up the plateau on the alignment the Sphinx guards, to the mortuary temple at the pyramid's east face — ruined but legible, its five-niche statuary hall and courtyard establishing the template every later pyramid temple copied. Around the pyramid's perimeter, look down: the enormous paving blocks and the sockets of the enclosure wall survive, and on the north and west sides the bedrock was quarried down to level the platform — the cut faces still showing the masons' trench-and-block method. One king's complete funerary machine, in walking order.

Inside Khafre, when the rotation allows

When the second pyramid holds the rotation ticket, its interior makes an instructive contrast with its father's: two descending entrances (one from the face, one from the bedrock platform — evidence of a mid-design change), converging on a single horizontal passage to the burial chamber, cut mostly into bedrock with a gabled granite-and-limestone ceiling. No Grand Gallery, no ascending system, no relieving chambers — Khufu's structural adventures answered with confident simplicity. Belzoni's 1818 graffiti sprawls across the chamber wall (the circus-strongman-turned-excavator announcing his entry), and the sarcophagus sits sunk into the floor, lid alongside, exactly the polished black granite the era did best. The climb is shorter and gentler than Khufu's — the right interior for travelers who want the inside-a-pyramid experience without the Great Pyramid's gauntlet of crouch, and at the rotation price (200-280 EGP recently) the value case writes itself.

The middle child's case, closed

Summing the second pyramid's quiet argument: it photographs as the tallest, keeps the plateau's only original summit, owns the best-preserved temple circuit, commands the Sphinx, and — when the rotation favors it — offers the friendliest interior on site at a fraction of its father's ticket. Khufu gets the superlatives and Menkaure gets the statues; Khafre got the site planning, and forty-five centuries later the site planning is still winning every wide shot from the viewpoint. Give it the dedicated hour the standard circuit grants it in passing.

Timing your hour

The middle pyramid's clock: first light for the summit casing's glow (binoculars from the northeast), mid-morning for the valley temple before the Sphinx crowds thicken around it, and the 2-4pm viewpoint window for the wide shots in which Khafre plays the lead. The interior, when the rotation favors it, is best taken early while the passage air is cool — and the temple circuit is best taken slow at any hour, because granite architecture this old and this intact exists in exactly one other place on the plateau, and that place is also Khafre's.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Pyramid of Khafre taller than the Great Pyramid?

No, but it plays taller. Khafre's pyramid is about 3 meters shorter than Khufu's, but it stands on bedrock roughly 10 meters higher and rises at a steeper angle — so from most viewpoints on the plateau, including the famous panoramic one, it reads as the tallest of the three. It's the most photographed pyramid at Giza, usually mistaken for its father's.

What is the white cap on top of Khafre's pyramid?

Surviving original casing. The smooth Tura limestone sheath that once covered all three pyramids was stripped over the centuries for building stone, but Khafre's summit retains a band of it — the single best surviving evidence of how the finished monuments looked: smooth, white, and sharp-edged against the sky.

Can you go inside the Pyramid of Khafre?

When it's open — the Ministry rotates interior access between Khafre and Menkaure for conservation. When available, Khafre's interior ticket has recently run around 280 EGP. The route is a descending corridor to a single burial chamber, less athletic than the Great Pyramid's climb and far less crowded.

Is Khafre's interior worth it compared to the Great Pyramid?

As an experience-per-pound calculation, often yes. There's no Grand Gallery — that's the Great Pyramid's trump card — but the descent, the chamber, Belzoni's 1818 graffiti on the wall, and the near-solitude cost a fraction of the price with no daily-cap scramble. Many repeat visitors prefer it.
