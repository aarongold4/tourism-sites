The Treasury (Al-Khazneh)

Petra's most photographed facade is a 2,000-year-old Nabataean tomb carved straight into the cliff. Here's what the guidebooks miss.

The Treasury is the moment Petra becomes real. You walk a kilometer and a half through the Siq — a narrow sandstone canyon that gradually closes overhead — and at the very end, framed by the slot canyon's walls, the 40-meter facade of Al-Khazneh comes into view. It's the image on every Jordan postcard and the closing shot of Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade. In person, it's bigger than you expect and somehow also smaller.

What you're actually looking at

Despite the name, the Treasury isn't a treasury. It's a royal tomb, almost certainly built for the Nabataean king Aretas IV, who ruled from 9 BC to 40 AD — making this one of the high points of Nabataean civilization. The "treasury" name comes from a Bedouin legend that bandits hid gold inside the urn at the top of the facade. You can still see pockmarks where people shot at the urn over the years trying to crack it open.

The carving is Hellenistic in style — Greek columns, pediment, decorative figures — but the underlying engineering is pure Nabataean. The facade was carved top-down, directly into the existing cliff face, by craftsmen working from scaffolding lowered from above. There's almost no margin for error: a missed cut couldn't be undone.

When to visit

For the iconic photograph — facade lit, no people in front of it — there are exactly two windows: just before the gates open at 6 AM, when the canyon is still dim and tour groups haven't arrived, and again in the late afternoon from about 4 PM onward when most groups have moved on toward the Monastery. The middle of the day is the busiest, with hundreds of people in the small plaza in front of the facade.

Petra by Night

On Sunday through Thursday evenings (expanded from three nights a week in late 2025), the Siq is lit by over 2,000 candles and the Treasury plaza is set up for a Bedouin music performance followed by a 3D projection-mapping show on the facade. It's a separate ticket (30 JOD as of 2026, not included in the Jordan Pass) and runs from 20:30 to about 22:30. Worth it if you've got a free evening in Wadi Musa; not worth rearranging your itinerary for.

Frequently asked questions

What time should I visit the Treasury for the best photos?
For direct sunlight on the facade, arrive between roughly 9:00 and 11:00 AM, when the sun has cleared the canyon walls. For the iconic glowing-from-within effect, you need an overcast morning — diffused light brings out the rose color of the stone. Sunrise is dramatic but the Treasury itself is in shadow until later.
Is the Treasury actually a treasury?
No. Despite the name (al-Khazneh, "the treasury" in Arabic), it's a tomb — most likely built for the Nabataean king Aretas IV in the early 1st century AD. The "treasury" name comes from a Bedouin legend that bandits hid loot inside; you can still see bullet marks on the urn at the top from people shooting at it hoping to crack it open.
Can you go inside the Treasury?
Not anymore. The interior is a single empty chamber and has been closed to the public for years to prevent damage. You can look in from the entrance but cannot enter.
