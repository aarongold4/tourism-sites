The Great Pyramid of Khufu

The oldest of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World and the only one still standing. Here's what you're looking at, what the interior climb actually involves, and how to decide whether the most expensive ticket on the plateau is your money well spent.

Some numbers stay astonishing no matter how many times you've heard them. The Great Pyramid was built around 2560 BC for the pharaoh Khufu of the Fourth Dynasty, from roughly 2.3 million blocks of stone averaging about two and a half tons each. Its base covers thirteen acres and is level to within a couple of centimeters. Its sides align to true north more precisely than almost any building before the modern era. It held the title of tallest structure on Earth for nearly four millennia. And it was already more ancient to Cleopatra than Cleopatra is to us.

On the ground, the experience of it is scale. Photographs compress the pyramid into a familiar shape; standing at the base recalibrates it. The individual blocks at the northeast corner are chest-high. The summit is far enough away to have weather. Whatever else you do at Giza, give yourself ten unhurried minutes at the base doing nothing but looking up.

The exterior: what to look for

The casing stones. The pyramid you see is the structural core. Originally the whole monument was sheathed in polished white Tura limestone, fitted so finely the surface read as a single smooth plane — it would have blazed in the sun. Most of the casing was quarried off for Cairo's construction in the medieval period, but a few casing stones survive at the base of the north face. Find them; they're the best single clue to what this place looked like for its first three thousand years.

The original entrance and the robbers' tunnel. On the north face, the true entrance sits about 17 meters up, framed by massive gabled blocks. Below and to its side is the rough tunnel attributed to the 9th-century forced entry under the caliph al-Ma'mun — and that tunnel, not the original door, is how today's visitors go in.

The boat pits and the queens' pyramids. Along the east and south sides lie the rectangular pits that held Khufu's dismantled funeral boats — one of which was excavated, reassembled, and now stands in the Grand Egyptian Museum (covered in our Things to See cluster) — plus the three small queens' pyramids and the mastaba fields of Khufu's court.

The interior: what the climb is really like

The route runs through al-Ma'mun's tunnel to the junction with the original passages, then up the ascending passage: about 40 meters at a 26-degree slope and roughly 1.2 meters of headroom, climbed in a crouch on wooden boards with handrails. It's the hardest stretch, and it's one-way traffic in alternating pulses on busy days.

Then the passage opens into the Grand Gallery, and the crouch becomes a cathedral: a corbelled space 47 meters long and 8.6 meters high, walls of polished limestone stepping inward overhead, still climbing at the same angle. It is the architectural high point of the visit and arguably of the Old Kingdom. At its top, a low granite passage delivers you into the King's Chamber — a bare, precise room of red granite, walls and ceiling beams of it (the heaviest roughly 50-80 tons, hauled from Aswan, 800 kilometers south), containing one object: Khufu's lidless granite sarcophagus, slightly wider than the entrance passage, meaning it was placed here while the pyramid was being built around it.

There are no decorations, no inscriptions on the route, no mummy, no artifacts. The chamber is hot, dim, and usually shared with a dozen strangers. What it offers instead is the fact of where you are: inside the densest, most exact building of the ancient world, in the room it was built around.

Is the interior ticket worth it?

The interior costs 1,500 EGP on top of general admission — the priciest ticket on the plateau, with daily entries capped around 150, which sell out in peak season (full tier breakdown in our entrance fees guide). The honest decision framework:

  • Worth it if: this is your one visit to Egypt and the idea of standing in the King's Chamber means something to you; you're physically comfortable with a hot, crouched climb; you can arrive early or book online to beat the cap.
  • Skip it if: claustrophobia, knees, or heat are real concerns; you're traveling with young children; or the budget is tight — in which case the second pyramid's interior (Khafre or Menkaure, on rotation, at a fraction of the price) delivers a genuinely similar experience without the name.

A useful rule from many visits: nobody regrets the Grand Gallery. The regrets come from people who went in expecting golden treasure, and from people who knew the climb would be a problem and went anyway.

Practical notes

Photography inside has been restricted or phone-only at various times — check current policy at the ticket point rather than risking a confiscation argument. Go early: the interior is coolest and least crowded in the first hour, and morning light is best for the exterior anyway (see opening hours). Leave bags you don't need at your hotel; there's nowhere good to stash things mid-climb. And drink water before you enter, not inside.

The history in brief

Khufu reigned roughly 2589-2566 BC, second king of the Fourth Dynasty, son of Sneferu — the great experimenter whose Bent and Red Pyramids at Dahshur solved the engineering problems Khufu's architects inherited solved. The pyramid was the center of a complex: mortuary temple on the east, causeway descending to a valley temple at the Nile's edge, the queens' pyramids, the boat burials, and the city of workers to the south whose excavated bakeries, breweries, and honored graves rewrote the story of who built all this — paid crews, not slaves, a story told properly in who built the pyramids.

The numbers that still stagger

The Great Pyramid's statistics deserve one concentrated paragraph, because each is a fact you can verify with your eyes from the base. Original height 146.6 meters (138.5 today, the capstone and crown long gone); base sides averaging 230.3 meters and agreeing with each other to within centimeters; orientation to true north within a twentieth of a degree — accuracy no building matched again for millennia; roughly 2.3 million blocks averaging 2.5 tons, with the granite beams over the King's Chamber running 25-80 tons apiece, shipped 800 kilometers from Aswan; a footprint of 13 acres leveled to within about 2 centimeters; and a construction window of about 20 years that works out — do the arithmetic at the base, everyone does — to a block set every few minutes of every working day for two decades. It held the title of tallest human structure for nearly 3,800 years, until Lincoln Cathedral's spire in the 14th century. No single fact about it is unbelievable; the stack of them is the experience.

The satellite story around the giant

Budget twenty minutes for the monument's supporting cast, all within a few hundred meters. The queens' pyramids on the east side — three small pyramids for Khufu's mother and wives, climbable-looking and not-climbable like everything else — with the hidden shaft tomb of Hetepheres (the mother whose gilded furniture fills a Tahrir room) discovered intact among them in 1925. The boat pits: the sealed trenches flanking the pyramid held full-size ships — the excavated one now at GEM (its page), its twin examined and left in place. The trial passages and mastaba rows eastward, where the court arranged itself around the king in death as in life — including, fifty meters out, the painted chapel of Queen Meresankh III, the plateau's best small ticket. The giant reads differently with its household around it: less an isolated wonder, more the center of a city of the dead that worked exactly as designed.

Last practical word

The Great Pyramid asks one decision of every visitor — the 1,500 EGP interior — and this page's counsel holds: buy it if the once-in-a-lifetime framing is true for you and your knees consent; take the rotation pyramid or Meresankh's painted chapel if value or comfort governs; and whichever you choose, give the exterior the unhurried first hour it deserves, because the climb is optional and the standing-before-it is the monument.

Frequently asked questions

How tall is the Great Pyramid?

Originally about 146.6 meters (481 feet); today roughly 138.5 meters after the loss of its capstone and outer casing. It was the tallest human-built structure on Earth for around 3,800 years — nothing surpassed it until the spires of medieval European cathedrals.

Is there anything inside the Great Pyramid?

Architecture, not treasure. The interior route climbs through the ascending passage and the Grand Gallery — one of the most astonishing spaces ever built — to the King's Chamber, a granite room holding Khufu's empty sarcophagus. There are no paintings, no hieroglyphs along the route, no artifacts. People who expect a museum are disappointed; people who understand they're walking through the building's own engineering are not.

Can you climb the outside of the pyramid?

No. Climbing the exterior has been illegal for decades, is enforced, and has been fatal — the casing-stone steps are over a meter tall and the descent is far worse than the ascent. The handful of viral climbing videos were criminal acts that resulted in bans and prosecutions.

Is the interior climb hard?

Moderately, and in specific ways: the ascending passage is roughly 1.2 meters high, forcing a crouched climb of about 40 meters; the Grand Gallery is steep with slick wooden ramps; and the air is hot and still year-round. Reasonable fitness handles it fine. Claustrophobia, bad knees, or heat intolerance are genuine reasons to choose a different ticket.
