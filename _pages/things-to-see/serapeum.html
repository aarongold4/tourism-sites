The Serapeum: Egypt underground, at its strangest

Deep beneath Saqqara run vaulted corridors lined with granite boxes the size of garden sheds — the tombs of sacred bulls, buried like kings for a thousand years. Nothing else on the circuit feels like it.

Every site in the pyramid fields plays variations on a theme — kings, tombs, eternity in limestone. The Serapeum changes key entirely: an underground avenue of burial vaults for bulls, each interred in a granite sarcophagus heavier than a loaded truck, in galleries that stay cool and dim and genuinely uncanny. It is Saqqara's strangest ticket and, for many visitors, its most memorable twenty minutes.

The cult, briefly

The Apis bull was Memphis's living god: one bull at a time, identified by sacred markings, housed in temple luxury as the herald of Ptah, consulted as an oracle, and on death mummified and buried with national mourning while priests searched Egypt for his successor. The practice ran from at least the New Kingdom deep into Ptolemaic times — the galleries' great expansion owes much to Khaemwaset, the antiquarian son of Ramses II who restored monuments already ancient in his day and earned the title of history's first Egyptologist. The Ptolemies folded the cult into their syncretic god Serapis, whose name the site now carries.

Discovery and the galleries today

Auguste Mariette found the place in 1851 by following a buried avenue of sphinxes — the discovery that launched both his career and, eventually, Egypt's antiquities service. Within, he found the niches and their monstrous sarcophagi (one walled-up burial still intact, its contents now in museums), plus stelae whose dates underpin chunks of Egyptian chronology. The visit today: a descent into the main gallery, a long vaulted corridor with burial niches opening alternately left and right, two dozen granite sarcophagi in place — several climbable-adjacent (don't), one conveniently lidded-open for the interior view — and the lighting kept low enough that the strangeness survives. The engineering question everyone asks in the dark — how do you turn an 80-ton polished box in an underground corridor — has prosaic answers (ramps, sand, levers, patience) and an irreducible residue of respect.

Fitting it in

The Serapeum sits in Saqqara's northwestern sector, a short drive or dusty walk from the Step Pyramid complex, near the mastaba of Ti — pair them. Ask for the ticket arrangement when buying Saqqara admission, give it twenty to thirty minutes, and slot it mid-circuit per the site guide. It suits everyone the pyramid interiors screen out — the galleries are walkable-height throughout — and it is the southern day's best answer to a visitor who thinks they've seen what Egypt has to offer. They have not seen the bulls.

Mariette's avenue, and what it started

The discovery story carries a coda worth knowing: Mariette came to Egypt in 1850 to collect Coptic manuscripts, saw a sphinx's head breaching the Saqqara sand, remembered Strabo's ancient description of a sphinx-lined avenue to the Serapeum, and spent his manuscript budget excavating instead. The gamble made his career, scandalized his employers, and ultimately gave Egypt its Antiquities Service and first national museum — institutions born, in a real sense, from this site's sand. The galleries you walk are thus doubly historic: the strangest monument of Egypt's late religion, and the founding excavation of the system that now protects everything else on your itinerary. Few tickets anywhere buy that much consequence per minute underground.

Comfort notes for the descent: the galleries run cool year-round (a pleasure in summer, a layer's worth in winter), the footing is good on installed walkways, lighting is deliberately dim — phone torches help with the niche interiors — and the visit suits almost every mobility level that handles the entrance ramp. Photography has generally been permitted without flash; ask at the ticket. Twenty minutes covers it; lingerers report the silence between tour groups as the experience's actual peak.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Serapeum of Saqqara?

The underground burial galleries of the Apis bulls — the sacred bulls of Memphis, each treated in death like a king and entombed in a colossal granite sarcophagus. The vaulted corridors run for hundreds of meters, lined with burial niches, mostly from the New Kingdom through Ptolemaic times.

How big are the sarcophagi?

Absurdly: the granite boxes run to roughly 60-80 tons with lids, polished inside and out, maneuvered through underground corridors by methods that still impress engineers. Standing beside one in a niche is the strangest scale experience at Saqqara.

Is the Serapeum included in the Saqqara ticket?

It is ticketed at Saqqara — historically as a separate add-on at the site's ticket point at a modest price, with arrangements subject to the usual Egyptian revision cycle. Ask when buying your Saqqara admission; it has been reliably open since its 2012 restoration.

Why were bulls buried like kings?

The Apis bull was a living god — the herald of Ptah, Memphis's creator deity — identified by sacred markings, pampered in life, mummified and mourned nationally in death. The cult ran for well over a thousand years, and Khaemwaset, Ramses II's antiquarian son, rebuilt the galleries — history's first heritage restorer working on what was already ancient.
