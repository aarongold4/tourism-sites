HISTORY

The Bdoul

The Bedouin community who lived in Petra's caves and tombs for centuries — until 1985, when they were resettled to a purpose-built village north of the site. Today most of the Bedouin people you meet at Petra are Bdoul, maintaining a deep connection to the place their families inhabited for generations.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 7-minute read · See our editorial standards

When travelers walk through Petra and notice soot-darkened ceilings inside the carved tombs, see Bedouin tea stalls perched at strategic viewpoints, or hear guides speak about generational family knowledge of the trails — they're seeing the contemporary legacy of the Bdoul, the Bedouin community who lived inside Petra for hundreds of years before being resettled to a nearby village in 1985. The Bdoul story is one of the most distinctive elements of Petra's modern history: the site wasn't simply an abandoned ruin awaiting tourist discovery, it was an inhabited cultural space whose contemporary residents have an ongoing relationship with the place.

This page covers who the Bdoul are, how they came to live in Petra, what the cave habitation was actually like, the 1985 resettlement and its complications, and the community's continuing role at the site today. The framing throughout: the Bdoul are not a historical curiosity but the modern human community of Petra, and any thoughtful visit to the site benefits from understanding their story.

Who they are

The Bdoul (sometimes spelled B'doul, Bidoul, or Bedoul) are one of several Bedouin tribes traditionally inhabiting the area around Petra. The name "Bdoul" is the tribal designation; individual members carry the broader cultural identity of Jordanian Bedouins as well as the specific community connection to Petra.

Tribal traditions suggest the Bdoul have inhabited the Petra region for at least 700 years, with some accounts placing their arrival as early as the Crusader period (12th-13th centuries). The exact timing of their settlement is debated, but the broader pattern is clear: Bedouin communities have continuously inhabited the region around Petra since before the Nabataean rise, through the major political changes of the past two millennia, and into the present.

During the long centuries when Petra was unknown to the wider world (roughly the 9th century CE through Burckhardt's 1812 rediscovery), the Bdoul and related Bedouin communities were essentially the sole human population at the site. They used the carved tombs and caves for shelter, grazed livestock through the surrounding wadis, and maintained the local knowledge of the trails, water sources, and seasonal patterns that has informed every subsequent visit to the site.

The cave habitation

The Bdoul didn't live in Petra in the same way the Nabataeans had — they didn't reconstitute the urban civilization that the earthquakes and political changes had ended. They lived more sparsely, using the carved structures as natural shelter rather than as a designed urban environment.

The advantages of cave shelter. The carved tombs and caves provided several benefits over tent dwellings: they maintained moderate temperatures year-round (warm in winter, cool in summer), they provided shelter from rain and dust storms, and they were essentially free building stock that didn't require maintenance. For a tribal community living off limited resources, the carved structures of Petra were an extraordinary infrastructure inheritance.

How the spaces were used. Larger tomb chambers became family living spaces, sometimes with simple wooden doors added at the entrance. Smaller caves and ledges served as storage, animal pens, or cooking areas. Some families maintained primary residence in caves and supplementary tent encampments in the surrounding wadis. The soot-darkened ceilings visible today in many lower-city caves come from centuries of cooking fires.

Daily life. The Bdoul economy combined traditional Bedouin practices (sheep and goat herding, seasonal migration patterns) with use of the local environment. The wadis around Petra provided seasonal grazing; agricultural plots existed in the valleys where soil and water permitted; and from the late 19th century onward, occasional tourist visits provided supplementary income through guiding and souvenir trading.

The 1985 resettlement

Petra was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985, which formalized its status as a globally-significant cultural property requiring protection. The UNESCO framework required that the site not be continuously inhabited — both because habitation causes wear and damage to the archaeological monuments, and because the active human community's daily life created legitimate competing claims on a space that international heritage protocols treated as primarily archaeological.

The Jordanian government negotiated with the Bdoul community for relocation to a purpose-built village called Umm Sayhoun (the name means "the mother of the cleansing place"), about 3 kilometers north of the Petra Visitor Centre. The new village provided modern housing, water and electricity infrastructure, a school, and basic services. The Bdoul retained primary access rights to Petra for tourism work — guiding, donkey rides, tea stalls, souvenir sales — preserving the economic connection to the site they had inhabited for generations.

The process. Most Bdoul families accepted the relocation, often after significant negotiation about housing terms, service provision, and tourism work arrangements. The relocation was largely completed by the late 1980s, though some Bdoul families maintained partial residence in caves into the 1990s.

The controversy. The relocation was not universally welcomed. Some Bdoul argued that the resettlement disrupted traditional ways of life without adequate compensation; that the new village was too far from the income-generating tourist areas to be practical; and that the cultural and emotional cost of leaving ancestral living spaces wasn't sufficiently recognized. Others viewed the relocation as a positive trade — modern housing and infrastructure for cave dwelling, with continued access to economic opportunity. The community's position on the resettlement remains varied, with different generations and family lines holding different views.

The continuing relationship. The Bdoul community today numbers several thousand people, most living in Umm Sayhoun with some additional families in Wadi Musa town and other nearby areas. The economic and cultural connection to Petra continues — most of the Bedouin workers visitors encounter at Petra are Bdoul, and the community maintains family-level knowledge of the site that no formal training can replicate.

Meeting Bdoul people at Petra

Most visitors to Petra will interact with Bdoul people during their visit, often without realizing it. Specific contexts:

The tea stalls. Throughout Petra, particularly along the trails to the Monastery, High Place, and Al-Khubtha viewpoint, Bedouin tea stalls operate on a casual basis. Most are run by Bdoul families. The tea (typically black tea with sugar, sometimes with mint or sage) costs 1-3 JOD and is a small but real cultural exchange — the operator is often happy to talk, share information about the site, and continue conversations beyond the transaction.

The donkey owners. The donkeys offering rides through the lower city and to the Monastery are mostly owned by Bdoul families. The donkey owners often have specialized routes and historical knowledge they share during the rides. Tipping is appreciated; the going rate is the agreed ride fee (5-10 JOD typically) plus 1-2 JOD tip.

The souvenir vendors. Bedouin-style jewelry, sand bottles, woven scarves, and other crafts are sold throughout Petra. Most vendors are Bdoul. Prices are negotiable; the cultural exchange is part of the experience. The bottle of layered colored sand (with the sand arranged into Petra silhouette patterns) is a Bdoul-tradition souvenir that's become iconic.

The licensed guides. Many of the official Petra guides operating from the Visitor Centre are Bdoul. A guide booked through the Visitor Centre (50-150 JOD for half-day to full-day visits) often brings family-level knowledge of the site that adds significantly to what you'd get from reading alone.

Visiting Umm Sayhoun

Travelers specifically interested in the Bdoul community can visit Umm Sayhoun, the village where most of the community lives today. Practical considerations:

Getting there: 5 JOD taxi from Wadi Musa, 10-15 minutes. The village is also a natural extension of a Little Petra visit (Little Petra is between Wadi Musa and Umm Sayhoun).

What to expect: A working Jordanian Bedouin community, not a curated cultural experience. The village has homes, a school, small shops, a mosque, and several Bdoul-owned businesses (restaurants, craft shops, occasionally informal guides). Approach it as you would any small Jordanian town — respectfully, with attention to local cultural norms.

Bdoul-owned tourism enterprises: Several small restaurants and tour operators based in Umm Sayhoun offer experiences ranging from traditional Bedouin meals (often in tents or cave-style settings) to guided walks on alternative Petra routes (entering from the north side via the back route rather than the standard Siq entrance). These businesses are growing as the Bdoul community develops more direct tourism enterprises.

The cultural ethics of the visit

A few practical notes on visiting Petra and meeting Bdoul people with cultural awareness:

  • Photography of people requires permission. Most Bdoul are happy to be photographed if you ask first; a "may I take your picture?" gesture suffices when you don't share a language. Photography without permission is rude in any context and particularly so when the subjects have a complicated relationship with tourism.
  • Tipping for service is appreciated. Small tips beyond the formal price for tea, donkey rides, and guiding work directly support the Bdoul community.
  • Engagement is welcomed. Bdoul people working at Petra are accustomed to international visitors and generally enjoy conversation. Asking about family history, the cave-dwelling past, or daily life produces richer experiences than purely transactional interactions.
  • Don't over-romanticize the cave past. The cave-dwelling life was harder than tourist imagination suggests, and the resettlement, while controversial, brought real material improvements. Approach the history with the complexity it deserves rather than projecting an idealized "lost authentic life" framework onto it.

Frequently asked questions

Who are the Bdoul?
The Bedouin tribe (also spelled B'doul, Bdoul, or Bidoul) who lived in the caves and rock-cut tombs of Petra for centuries — from at least the medieval period until 1985, when they were resettled by the Jordanian government to a purpose-built village called Umm Sayhoun, about 3 kilometers north of the Petra entrance. The Bdoul continue to work at Petra today as guides, donkey owners, tea-stall operators, and souvenir sellers, maintaining a deep connection to the site that their families inhabited for generations.
Did people really live inside Petra?
Yes — for hundreds of years. After the Nabataean city declined and the broader population dispersed, Bedouin communities used the carved tombs and caves as shelter, especially during the cold winter months. The carved chambers stay warm in winter and cool in summer, making them surprisingly habitable. Many of the lower-city caves and some of the smaller Royal Tombs show centuries of habitation soot on the ceilings. This continued continuously until the 1985 resettlement.
Why were they resettled?
Petra's 1985 UNESCO World Heritage designation required protecting the site from continuous habitation, and the Jordanian government negotiated the relocation of the Bdoul community to a new village. The relocation was contested and complicated — most Bdoul families accepted the move (which provided modern housing, services, and continued economic access to Petra) but the process was emotionally difficult and remains a sensitive topic. Some Bdoul families maintained partial residence in caves for years after the official relocation.
Can I meet Bdoul people at Petra?
Yes — most of the Bedouin people you encounter at Petra are Bdoul. The tea-stall operators along the Monastery and High Place trails, many of the donkey owners offering rides through the lower city, the souvenir vendors near the major monuments, and a significant share of the licensed guides at the Visitor Centre all come from the Bdoul community. Conversations with them are part of what makes Petra distinctive — they bring centuries of family-level knowledge of the site that no tourism textbook can match.
Is it controversial to talk about the Bdoul?
Somewhat. The 1985 resettlement was difficult for the community, and not all members agree it was the right outcome. Some Bdoul argue the resettlement disrupted their traditional way of life without adequate compensation; others have benefited economically from the more structured access to tourism work. The relationship between the Jordanian government, the UNESCO management framework, and the Bdoul community remains evolving. Visitors should be aware of the complexity but not over-romanticize the cave-dwelling past or the resettlement controversy.
Where is Umm Sayhoun and can I visit?
Umm Sayhoun is about 3 km north of the Petra Visitor Centre, accessible by taxi (5 JOD) from Wadi Musa. The village itself is a working community, not a tourist destination — you can visit but should approach it as you would any small Jordanian town rather than expecting a curated experience. Some Bdoul-owned businesses (small restaurants, craft shops, guides) operate from Umm Sayhoun and welcome visitors. The Little Petra (Siq al-Barid) area is between Wadi Musa and Umm Sayhoun, often combined with a visit to the village.
