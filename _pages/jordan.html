JORDAN

Jordan travel essentials

Petra is the headline, but Petra is also in Jordan — a country with its own visa rules, currency, food, dress norms, and a half-dozen other destinations worth your time. Here's the practical guide to the rest of it.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 11-minute read · See our editorial standards

Most travelers arrive in Jordan thinking about one site, but the country itself is a relatively easy place to get to know — small (about the size of Portugal), stable, English-friendly in tourist areas, and reasonably priced compared to its regional neighbors. The practical logistics that catch first-timers off guard are mostly small things rather than big ones: that the Jordan Pass is essentially always worth buying, that cash is required for most of what you'll spend on day to day, that the country has a single rest day on Friday rather than the Western weekend, and that the dress norms are more conservative than most Western Mediterranean countries but more relaxed than the Gulf states.

This page is the index to those essentials, organized in the order most travelers actually need them. The first set — visa, currency, the Jordan Pass — affects how you book and what you pay. The second — food, language, dress, tipping — is the day-to-day texture of the trip. The third — Amman, Wadi Rum, the Dead Sea, Jerash, Aqaba — covers the places in Jordan that aren't Petra but should be on your itinerary.

Day-to-day Jordan

Beyond the entry logistics, the texture of a Jordan trip is shaped by a handful of practical norms most travelers want to understand before they arrive. The food is excellent and generally familiar to anyone who's eaten Middle Eastern cuisine — mezze, grilled meats, mansaf (the national dish), Bedouin zarb cooked underground. English is widely spoken in tourism settings. Tipping is modest and appreciated rather than aggressive. Dress is moderately conservative in cities and at religious sites; more relaxed at Petra, the Dead Sea, and Aqaba.

The single biggest thing that catches travelers off guard is the weekend — Friday is the official day of rest in Jordan, which means many shops and government offices are closed and Friday lunch is the major family meal of the week. Saturday is a half-business day. The Western Saturday-Sunday rhythm doesn't apply; plan your major shopping, banking, or visa-extension errands for Sunday through Thursday.

The rest of Jordan

Petra justifies the trip on its own, but five other destinations in Jordan are worth at least a day each, and most travelers who do the full Jordan loop see four or five of them. Amman is the capital, a hilltop city of stacked white buildings with a Roman amphitheatre at its heart. Wadi Rum is the desert south of Petra where Lawrence of Arabia campaigned — the Bedouin camp overnights here are one of the trip's high points. The Dead Sea is the lowest point on earth, with mineral-rich water you can float in and resort hotels along the Jordanian shore. Jerash is the best-preserved Roman city outside Italy, an hour north of Amman. Aqaba is the Red Sea port at Jordan's southern tip, with diving, snorkeling, and a useful international airport.

For an itinerary that combines Petra with these destinations, the itineraries pillar covers durations from 3 days to 2 weeks. The honest summary: 5 days is the minimum for a meaningful Jordan visit, 7 days is the sweet spot, and 10 days is the version most worth flying internationally for.

The five things that catch travelers off guard

For travelers who want the short version: five things about Jordan that are surprisingly different from what most first-timers expect, and that are easier to handle if you know them in advance.

The Jordan Pass requires two nights' minimum stay. The pass waives your 40 JOD visa fee — but only if you stay in Jordan at least two nights (three days). One-night transits don't qualify, and the visa fee gets charged at the airport regardless. We cover the math on the Jordan Pass page.

Friday, not Sunday, is the day of rest. Many shops, government offices, and small businesses close on Friday for the noon prayer and the family meal that follows. Saturday is a partial business day. Plan errands for Sunday through Thursday.

Cash is essential, but not the only thing. Card payments work at hotels, larger restaurants, and tour operators. Everything else — taxis, market stalls, tea stops, tipping — needs cash. Plan to carry 50–100 JOD per person per day.

The water in Petra is real. Two liters per person, minimum, for a Petra day. The site is much larger than the photographs suggest and the climbs are real climbs. Sunstroke is the most common reason tourists need medical attention in Jordan.

The Israeli border crossings have specific hours. The Wadi Araba (Yitzhak Rabin) crossing near Eilat is closed on Saturdays during Shabbat and on several Jewish holidays per year. The Allenby/King Hussein Bridge crossing near Jericho has different hours again. Anyone doing a Petra-from-Israel itinerary needs to check the specific dates before booking flights.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a visa for Jordan?
Most nationalities can get a visa on arrival at Queen Alia (Amman) or King Hussein (Aqaba) International Airports. The fee is 40 JOD, single-entry, valid for one month. The Jordan Pass waives this fee if you buy it before you arrive and stay at least two nights in Jordan. Citizens of a handful of Gulf states and Turkey enter visa-free. A small number of countries (Bangladesh, Pakistan, several African nations) need to apply in advance. Check the Jordan Ministry of Foreign Affairs site for the current list before you book flights.
What currency does Jordan use? Can I use credit cards?
The Jordanian Dinar (JOD), pegged to the US dollar at roughly 1 JOD = 1.41 USD. Bills come in denominations of 1, 5, 10, 20, and 50 JOD. Credit cards work at hotels, larger restaurants, tour operators, and the Petra ticket booth. Cash is essential for taxis, small restaurants, market stalls, tipping, and most of the small transactions that make up a Petra day. Expect to carry 50–100 JOD in cash per person per day. ATMs in Amman, Aqaba, and Wadi Musa are reliable and accept international cards. Avoid the airport currency exchange — the rates are noticeably worse than the in-city exchange bureaus.
Is Jordan safe?
Yes, by any reasonable measure. Jordan is consistently one of the most stable countries in the region — the tourism industry operates under tight government oversight, and crime against tourists is rare. The US and UK travel advisories typically rate Jordan in the same range as most European countries, though they specifically warn against travel to the Syrian border area in the north and to certain Iraqi border zones in the east. Petra, Wadi Rum, Amman, the Dead Sea, and Aqaba are all comfortably safe. The realistic risks are sunstroke, dehydration, and falls on uneven terrain — not crime or political instability.
What do I tip in Jordan?
Tipping is expected but modest. Round up or leave 10% in restaurants where service isn't included; 1–3 JOD for hotel porters and housekeeping; 5–10 JOD per day for a tour guide if you're happy with their work, and a similar amount for a driver. Taxis don't expect tips but you can round up the fare. Bedouin tea-stall operators along the Petra trails appreciate 1 JOD for tea, even though they often don't formally charge. The G Adventures suggested $11–13 USD per person per day combined for guide-and-driver tipping on a multi-day tour is a reasonable benchmark.
Do people speak English in Jordan?
Widely, yes — especially in Amman, around Petra, in Aqaba, and at the Dead Sea resorts. Most younger Jordanians have studied English in school, hotel and tourism staff are typically fluent, and tour guides at archaeological sites are licensed in multiple languages. In smaller towns and away from tourist areas, Arabic is more dominant. Learning a few phrases (marhaba for hello, shukran for thank you, la for no) is appreciated but not strictly necessary.
What should I wear in Jordan?
Jordan is moderate but conservative. The practical rule for women is shoulders and knees covered — light long-sleeve shirts and loose trousers or long skirts work well, and a scarf for visiting mosques (King Abdullah I Mosque in Amman is the only major one open to non-Muslim visitors). Men have a wider berth but should still avoid shorts in cities and religious sites. Petra is more relaxed than many religious sites because of the physical activity involved — hiking gear is fine, but visible tattoos, very short shorts, and tank tops still get noticeable attention. The exception is the Dead Sea resorts, which are essentially Western beach environments.
