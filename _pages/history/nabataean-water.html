Nabataean water management

The engineering that made Petra possible. Dams, channels, cisterns, and ceramic pipes — a hydraulic infrastructure sophisticated enough to support a desert city of 20,000+ people in a region that gets less than 200 mm of annual rain.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 8-minute read · See our editorial standards

The Nabataeans built a city in a desert canyon. The visible Petra — the Treasury, the Monastery, the carved tombs and temples — only exists because the Nabataeans solved the most fundamental problem first: how to get and store enough water to support 20,000-30,000 people in an environment that receives less than 200 mm of annual rainfall, most of it falling in destructive flash floods rather than usable rainfall. The water management system they built is among the most sophisticated hydraulic engineering achievements of the ancient world, comparable to Roman aqueducts in scale though different in technique.

This page covers how the system worked, what survives to see today, and why it matters for understanding Petra. The framing throughout: this is the invisible infrastructure that made the visible monuments possible.

The water problem

Petra sits in a basin in the Edomite mountains of southern Jordan, at roughly 1,000 meters elevation. The climate is Mediterranean-desert: hot and arid in summer (June-September), cool and occasionally wet in winter (December-March). Total annual rainfall averages 150-200 mm, but the distribution is the real problem — rainfall arrives in concentrated bursts during winter storms, often producing flash floods through the wadi systems that catastrophically channel water through canyons in volumes that can drown anyone caught in the path.

For a small Bedouin-style settlement, this rainfall pattern is workable — flash floods are dangerous but rare, and a few hundred people can subsist on small-scale water management. For a city of 20,000-30,000 people running a major trade entrepot, the same rainfall is wholly inadequate without sophisticated infrastructure. The Nabataeans solved this problem through what archaeologists now describe as an integrated water management system — capture, conveyance, storage, and distribution working together as a coordinated whole rather than as discrete elements.

Capture — the dams

The first element of the system was water capture. The Nabataeans built a series of dams in the wadis (dry riverbeds) feeding into the Petra basin, designed to catch flash-flood runoff during winter storms and channel it into the city's storage system.

The Siq dam. The most visible and famous of the dams, blocking the wadi just upstream from the entrance to the Siq. The original Nabataean dam (1st century BCE) diverted floodwaters away from the Siq, preventing the catastrophic flooding that would otherwise sweep through the canyon. The dam was rebuilt by Jordanian authorities in 1963 after a fatal flash flood killed 23 French tourists in the Siq — the modern reconstruction follows the Nabataean engineering design.

The Wadi Mataha dam. A larger structure upstream of the Royal Tombs area, capturing runoff from the wider basin to the north.

Smaller capture works. Throughout the surrounding wadis, smaller dams, check-walls, and diversion structures captured runoff into the conveyance system. Many of these are partially preserved and visible on the trails to the Monastery, High Place, and Jebel Haroun.

Conveyance — channels and pipes

Once captured, water needed to travel from the surrounding hills to the inhabited center of Petra. The Nabataeans built two parallel systems:

Open stone channels. Carved into the bedrock along trails and cliff faces, these channels carried water by gravity over distances of several kilometers. The most famous are the channels along both walls of the Siq — visible today as carved grooves at chest height along the rock. These channels brought water from the Ein Mousa spring (about 2 kilometers east of the Siq entrance) into the city.

Ceramic pipes. For sections where open channels weren't practical (steep gradients, areas where water needed to be protected from contamination, distribution within the city), the Nabataeans used ceramic pipes — typically about 25 cm in diameter, with each pipe section roughly 30-40 cm long, joined together with sealing compounds. Excavated fragments of these pipes appear in museum collections; in some sections of the site, the pipes are still visible in their original positions in carved channels in the rock.

Subterranean conduits. Some sections of the conveyance system ran underground, carved as tunnels through bedrock or built as covered channels. These underground sections protected water from evaporation, contamination, and the more dramatic temperature swings of the surface.

Storage — cisterns and reservoirs

The flash-flood capture pattern produced water in concentrated winter bursts; the population needed water through the long dry summer. The Nabataeans solved this through large-scale storage:

Carved bedrock cisterns. Throughout Petra, the Nabataeans carved cisterns directly into the sandstone bedrock — typically 10-30 cubic meters in volume, sealed with hydraulic plaster to prevent leakage. The cisterns appear on trails to the Monastery, the High Place, around Qasr al-Bint, and on the slopes of nearby mountains.

Built reservoirs. Larger storage structures combining excavated and built elements. The most famous is the Nymphaeum complex on the colonnaded street, which combined ceremonial water display with practical storage.

Household cisterns. Most Nabataean houses had small private cisterns, fed from the public distribution system, providing individual household water supply through the dry season.

The total storage capacity across the system was substantial — estimated at sufficient to support the city's population through several months of dry conditions without any new input.

Distribution — bringing water to homes

The conveyance system delivered water to central distribution points; the distribution system delivered water from those points to the population. This final stage used:

Public fountains. At the major civic squares and along the colonnaded street, public fountains provided community access to clean water. The Nymphaeum was the largest of these — a multi-story decorative fountain serving as a major civic monument as well as a practical water source.

Private supply. Wealthier households had private water connections delivering filtered water directly to household cisterns. The piping systems for these connections are partially preserved at several excavated villa sites.

Industrial supply. The Nabataean economy included industries (dyeing, leather processing, food production) that required significant water. The distribution system included industrial supply lines to these workshops.

Why this matters

Three reasons the water management system is historically significant:

It made Petra economically viable. Without the water infrastructure, Petra couldn't have grown beyond a small Bedouin-style settlement. The integrated system enabled a population of 20,000-30,000 — large enough to support a major trade entrepot, monumental architecture, and a sophisticated political class. The wealth that produced the Treasury and the Monastery depended on the engineering that produced reliable household water.

It demonstrates Nabataean technical sophistication. The water system shows the Nabataeans as more than wealthy middlemen on the spice trade — they were engineers, architects, and urban planners working at a level comparable to Roman or Hellenistic peers. The system required mathematical modeling (slopes, flow rates, storage capacities), materials science (hydraulic plaster, ceramic manufacturing), and large-scale organized labor across decades.

It explains the city's resilience and decline. Petra survived as a major city for roughly 500 years (200 BCE - 300 CE), then declined slowly. The water system explains both the survival and the decline — sustained infrastructure investment kept the city viable, while the gradual collapse of that investment (after the 363 CE earthquake and subsequent political changes) made continued population at scale impossible.

What to look at on your visit

Specific features to notice on a standard Petra visit:

  • The Siq channels. Walking through the Siq, look at both walls at chest height — you'll see the carved channels (one on each side) that originally carried ceramic pipes. The channels are easy to miss because the eye goes to the canyon walls and the eventual Treasury reveal, but they're prominent once you know to look.
  • The Siq dam. Just outside the Siq entrance, on the wadi to the right as you face the Siq. The 1963 reconstruction follows the Nabataean original.
  • Cisterns near the Monastery. On the climb to the Monastery, several Bedouin tea stalls operate on top of carved cisterns. You can often see the carved opening with steps leading down into the storage chamber.
  • The Nymphaeum. On the colonnaded street, the two-story fountain structure. Even in its damaged state, the original water-display function is visible.
  • Pipe fragments at the museum. The on-site Petra Museum at the Visitor Centre includes ceramic pipe fragments and water-system artifacts on display.

Frequently asked questions

How did the Nabataeans get water in the desert?
Through one of the ancient world's most sophisticated water management systems. The Nabataeans built dams to catch flash-flood runoff in the surrounding wadis, channeled water through carved conduits for kilometers to storage cisterns, distributed it through ceramic pipes and stone channels to public fountains and private homes, and built large reservoirs to hold water through the dry summer months. The system supported a desert city of 20,000-30,000 people in an environment that gets less than 200 mm of annual rainfall.
Can I see the water systems at Petra today?
Yes — water channels and cisterns are visible throughout the site. The most obvious are the carved channels along both walls of the Siq (originally with ceramic pipes embedded in them), the large dam at the entrance to the Siq (rebuilt by Jordanian authorities in 1963 after a fatal flash flood), the carved cisterns along the trails to the Monastery and High Place, and the visible channels and pipe fragments around the Great Temple. The Jordan Museum in Amman has artifacts from the water system on display.
Why is Nabataean water management so famous?
Because it was extraordinarily sophisticated for the period — comparable in scale and design to Roman aqueduct systems, but achieved through different techniques. The Nabataeans solved the technical problem of supporting a major city in an extreme arid environment by combining flash-flood capture, long-distance conveyance, multiple-cistern storage, and household distribution into an integrated system. The engineering helped make Petra possible economically; without water management at this scale, the city couldn't have grown beyond a small trading post.
When was the water system built?
The basic infrastructure dates from the 1st century BCE through the 1st century CE — the period of Petra's peak under Aretas IV and his predecessors. Earlier, simpler water systems existed, but the integrated network visible today was the product of about 150 years of sustained engineering investment. Later Roman and Byzantine modifications added to the original system but didn't fundamentally restructure it.
What ended the system?
Decline rather than catastrophe. The 363 CE earthquake damaged sections of the system; subsequent earthquakes (551 CE in particular) caused further damage; the political and economic decline that followed reduced the labor available for maintenance; and the gradual depopulation of Petra over centuries meant that the system was eventually abandoned rather than destroyed. Modern restoration projects have rebuilt some sections (the Siq dam being the most prominent example).
Is the water management visible in regular tourist visits?
Yes, if you know what to look for. On any standard Petra visit, you walk past water channels, cisterns, and dam fragments without necessarily registering them as engineering. The Siq channels (carved into the walls on both sides), the dam at the Siq entrance, and the various cisterns near the Monastery and High Place are all on the main tourist paths. Bringing a guide who can point them out adds meaningful depth to the visit; alternatively, knowing what you're looking at in advance lets you spot the features on your own.
