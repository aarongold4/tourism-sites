History of Tbilisi

Fifteen centuries as a capital, destroyed and rebuilt dozens of times, ruled from Ctesiphon, Baghdad, Tehran, St. Petersburg, and Moscow — and stubbornly Georgian through all of it.

Last reviewed: June 7, 2026

The legend first, because the city insists on it: King Vakhtang Gorgasali, hunting in the forested gorge of the Mtkvari in the fifth century, watches his quarry — a pheasant in most tellings — fall into a hot spring. Struck by the warm waters, he orders a city built on the spot and names it for them: tbili, warm. The archaeology is more prosaic — the site was settled long before Vakhtang — but the legend encodes the true fact that matters: Tbilisi exists because of its springs, and the sulfur baths still steaming in Abanotubani are the city's founding infrastructure, fifteen centuries in continuous service.

What followed was shaped by an unlucky excellence of location. The gorge commands the crossing of routes between the Black and Caspian seas and between the Eurasian steppe and the Middle East — wealth-making geography that every passing empire wanted. Arabs ruled an emirate here for four centuries; David the Builder took the city back in 1122 and opened Georgia's golden age, the era of Queen Tamar and the national epic. Then the catalog of catastrophe: Khwarazmians, Mongols, Timur repeatedly, Persians and Ottomans in alternation, culminating in the Persian sack of 1795 that burned the city almost completely. Nearly everything "old" in the Old Town postdates that fire — the carved balconies are the nineteenth century's reconstruction, which is its own kind of historical document.

The Russian and Soviet centuries

Annexation by Russia in 1801 made Tiflis — the Persianate name the empire used — the administrative capital of the Caucasus, and the tsarist century built the other city: Rustaveli Avenue's opera house and theaters, the European facades, the funicular up Mtatsminda. For a few extraordinary years after 1918 the city was the capital of an independent democratic republic; the Red Army ended that in 1921, and the Soviet decades layered on their own strata — monumental public buildings, mass-housing districts, the engineering confidence of the cable cars, and the traumas, from the purges to the 9 April 1989 massacre of demonstrators on Rustaveli that hastened the union's end. The Soviet Tbilisi page reads those decades through the buildings that survive them.

Independence in 1991 opened hard years — civil conflict, blackout winters — followed by the 2003 Rose Revolution and the rapid, uneven modernization visible everywhere today: glass landmarks beside unrestored courtyards, a capital arguing with itself about its direction in real time. That argument, periodically visible on Rustaveli Avenue, is the current chapter, and watching the city conduct it is part of what makes Tbilisi feel alive rather than preserved.

Reading the city as a document

The practical payoff of the history is a richer walk. Anchiskhati Basilica has stood since the sixth century; Sioni Cathedral holds the grapevine cross of St. Nino, who converted the kingdom in the fourth century — the event that made Mtskheta sacred and Georgia one of the oldest Christian nations. The bath district keeps the Persian connection visible in its tilework; Rustaveli keeps the Russian century; the Chronicle of Georgia monument on its northern hill keeps the late-Soviet appetite for the colossal. The spoke pages below take the two most-asked chapters — the founding and the Soviet era — and the cluster grows from there into the golden age, the Silk Road centuries, and independence.

The long arc, in brief

Tbilisi's story runs in legible chapters, and holding the shape of it makes the city readable on the ground. It opens in the fifth century with King Vakhtang Gorgasali and the founding legend of the hot springs, when the capital shifted downriver from ancient Mtskheta. A medieval golden age followed under David the Builder and Queen Tamar, when Georgia was a regional power and its art and architecture flowered — the era that built the churches and the confidence the country still draws on.

Then came the long centuries of being a crossroads worth fighting over. Mongols, Persians, and Ottomans sacked the city repeatedly; the 1795 Persian destruction was so total it pushed Georgia toward Russian protection, and Russian imperial rule from 1801 rebuilt Tbilisi as a provincial capital with the grand avenues and balconied mansions that still define Sololaki and Rustaveli. A brief independent republic (1918–1921) was crushed by the Red Army, and seventy Soviet years — covered in our Soviet Tbilisi page — layered Stalinist facades, brutalist landmarks, and a monumental metro over the medieval bones, before the independence movement and the 1989 April 9 tragedy carried the country to the restoration of independence in 1991.

What makes the city so rewarding to read is that none of these chapters was erased. A sixth-century basilica stands a block from a Tsarist opera house, a Soviet ministry, and a glass bridge from the 2000s building boom — the strata stacked and visible. The pages below take two of the chapters in depth; the rest you can read straight off the streets once you know what you're looking at.

Frequently asked questions

How old is Tbilisi?

The traditional founding by King Vakhtang Gorgasali dates to the fifth century, making the city roughly 1,500 years old as a capital — though archaeology shows settlement on the site centuries earlier. The founding-legend page separates the story from the evidence.

Why was the city destroyed so many times?

Geography. Tbilisi commands the easiest corridor between the Black and Caspian seas and between the steppe and the Middle East, which made it rich and made it a target — Persians, Arabs, Khwarazmians, Mongols, and Turks all took their turns. The conventional count runs to several dozen sackings; the city’s identity is the rebuilding.

What does the name mean?

It derives from tbili, Georgian for “warm” — a reference to the natural hot springs that the founding legend credits with the site’s selection, and that still feed the sulfur baths today. The older European name Tiflis is the Persian-mediated form of the same word.

Where can I actually see the layers?

In one walk: Anchiskhati Basilica (sixth century), Sioni Cathedral (medieval core, much rebuilt), the Persian-inflected bath district, the tsarist-era avenues around Rustaveli, Soviet monumental architecture up the hill, and the glass Bridge of Peace below. Few cities stack fifteen centuries this legibly.

Is the history cluster useful before visiting, or just for enthusiasts?

An hour with it changes what you see. The Old Town reads as pretty decay without context and as a survivor’s document with it — and the day trips (Mtskheta especially) double in meaning once the Christianization story is in your head.
