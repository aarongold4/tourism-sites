THINGS TO SEE

Saqqara and the Step Pyramid

Half an hour south of Giza stands the building where architecture in stone begins — and around it, the decorated tombs that hold the most vivid art of the Old Kingdom, seen by a tenth of Giza's crowd. This is the best half-day in Egypt.

Saqqara was the necropolis of Memphis, Egypt's ancient capital, and it was in continuous funerary use for over three thousand years — longer than the span separating us from its own founding. Its centerpiece is the Step Pyramid of Djoser, built around 2670 BC, a century before Giza: six mastabas of diminishing size stacked skyward by the architect Imhotep, the first monumental building in the world constructed entirely of cut stone. Every pyramid that followed — Dahshur's experiments, Giza's perfection — descends from this building. Stand in front of it and you're at the root of the tree.

But the Step Pyramid is, honestly, only half the reason to come. The other half is underground: the decorated tombs of Old Kingdom officials, carved and painted with scenes of the life they hoped to continue — fishing, herding, baking, brewing, dancing, force-feeding geese, arguing with boatmen — in relief work of astonishing liveliness. Giza's monuments are mute stone at scale; Saqqara's tombs talk.

The Step Pyramid complex

Djoser's pyramid stands inside an enormous enclosure entered through a colonnade of forty stone columns — themselves a first, stone imitating bundled reeds because stone architecture had no traditions of its own yet. The complex includes the great south court with its ritual markers for the king's jubilee race, the serdab box on the north side where a statue of Djoser still gazes out through peepholes (the original is in Cairo; a replica sits in place), and the restored facades that give the best sense anywhere of what a 4,600-year-old royal monument looked like new.

Interior access to the pyramid itself — reopened in 2020 after extensive restoration — descends to the granite burial vault at the bottom of a great central shaft, past walls of blue faience tiles imitating reed matting. It's a separate ticket, less strenuous than the Great Pyramid's climb, and worth it.

The tombs: where the art is

Which tombs are open rotates with conservation work, but the canonical stops include the mastaba of Ti (the finest reliefs of the Old Kingdom by common consent), the double mastaba of Kagemni and Mereruka near the Teti pyramid (Mereruka's is the largest private tomb of the era, thirty-plus rooms), and the tomb of the two brothers, Niankhkhnum and Khnumhotep, with its famously intimate paired imagery. The Serapeum — the colossal underground galleries where the sacred Apis bulls were buried in granite sarcophagi the size of garden sheds — is Saqqara's strangest and most memorable interior, and usually open on its own ticket.

Saqqara is also Egypt's most productive active dig: the past few years have produced a steady run of headline discoveries — sealed coffin caches, new tomb openings — which means the visitable site genuinely changes year to year. Whatever was true on your last visit, something new is probably open.

The Imhotep Museum

The site museum near the entrance is small, modern, and excellent — the architectural elements, the blue faience, and the statuary recovered from the complex, presented with unusual clarity. Fifteen to thirty minutes, best taken first: it makes everything on the plateau above legible.

Tickets and practicalities

Saqqara's general site ticket has recently stepped from 450 to around 600 EGP for foreign adults (300 for students), including the Imhotep Museum, with separate tickets for the Step Pyramid interior, the Serapeum, and certain tombs — Egypt revises these often, so verify current figures on the official egymonuments.com platform near your date (the same advice as our Giza fees guide, and the reason every page here carries a review date). Hours follow the standard Egyptian site day, roughly 8 AM to 4 PM. The site is spread out and sandy underfoot; transport between clusters of tombs — your own driver, typically — saves real energy in warm months.

Crowds are Saqqara's gift. Mid-morning, when Giza is at maximum load, Saqqara's tombs are often near-empty — which is precisely why the classic combined day (Giza at opening, Saqqara late morning, covered in our itineraries) sequences them that way.

The tombs: what the painted walls actually show

Saqqara's mastabas are the Old Kingdom's home movies, and knowing what to look for doubles them. In the great double mastaba of Mereruka (when the rotation opens it) and its Sixth Dynasty peers — Kagemni, Ankhmahor, the famous Ti near the Serapeum, and the exquisite Two Brothers (Niankhkhnum and Khnumhotep) — the repertoire repeats with local genius: the deceased spearing fish from a papyrus skiff while crocodiles eye hippopotami; cattle fording canals, calves carried over shoulders; force-feeding of cranes and hyenas (they tried everything); scribes recording grain while defaulters are dragged to account; carpenters, metalworkers, dwarf jewelers at their benches; boys playing games and girls dancing; and the offering scenes that made it all eternal. Read them as the owners intended — a world summoned by depiction — and the bare chambers at Giza make sudden sense as a different theological choice, not a poverty.

Beyond the headline circuit

With a second hour or a second visit, Saqqara's depth chart continues. The Serapeum — the colossal underground galleries where the sacred Apis bulls were buried in granite sarcophagi of absurd tonnage — is the site's strangest experience and its ticket-window sleeper. The pyramid of Unas, modest outside, holds the oldest religious texts on Earth on its chamber walls when access allows; the Teti pyramid's interior commonly stands in when it doesn't. The Imhotep Museum at the entrance — fifteen minutes that organize everything — keeps the architect's statue base, the blue faience from beneath the Step Pyramid, and the site's greatest hits. And the horizon does the rest: from the plateau's edge, on clear days, the pyramid fields of Abusir, Giza north, and Dahshur south string along the desert rim — the entire hundred-year experiment visible in one slow turn of the head. New tombs emerge from Saqqara's sand almost annually; this page's review date is doing real work.

Saqqara's standing claim

This site's verdict, for the record: minute for minute, Saqqara is the best archaeological experience in greater Cairo — the first stone building, the painted rooms Giza lacks, the underground strangeness of the Serapeum, and working excavations producing news annually, all at a fraction of the plateau's crowds. Giza is why you came; Saqqara is what you'll talk about. Plan it as a destination, not a stop (the day built around it).

Frequently asked questions

How far is Saqqara from Giza?

About 25-30 minutes by car south along the desert edge — close enough that combining the two sites in one day is the standard move, and the right one. There's no practical public transport; a driver, tour, or ride-hail arrangement is how everyone gets there (details in our Getting There cluster).

How much time do you need at Saqqara?

Two to three hours covers the essentials well: the Step Pyramid complex, the Imhotep Museum, and two or three decorated tombs. Enthusiasts can fill a half day — the site is vast and excavation is ongoing, with new tombs opening to visitors regularly.

Can you go inside the Step Pyramid?

Yes — interior access to Djoser's pyramid opened to visitors in 2020 after a long restoration, on a separate ticket. The descent leads to the burial shaft beneath the world's oldest monumental stone building. It's less physically demanding than the Great Pyramid climb and, for many, more atmospheric.

Is Saqqara better than Giza?

Different, and in some ways yes. Giza has the scale and the icons; Saqqara has the art, the history's starting point, and a fraction of the crowds. The decorated Old Kingdom tombs at Saqqara contain the most vivid scenes of ancient Egyptian daily life anywhere — things Giza simply doesn't offer. Serious answer: do both.
