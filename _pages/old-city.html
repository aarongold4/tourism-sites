THE OLD QUARTER

Old City & Islamic Tashkent

Madrasas, mosques and the low mud-walled mahallas that survived the Soviet rebuild.

When the 1966 earthquake levelled much of Tashkent, the Soviet rebuild swept away whole districts of the old town. What survives — the madrasas around Hazrat Imam, the Kukeldash Madrasa above Chorsu, and the lanes of low courtyard houses — is concentrated in the western, older half of the city, and it is where Tashkent feels most like the Silk Road oasis it once was.

What survived, and where

The old quarter, Eski Shahar, sits west of the modern centre around Chorsu Bazaar. Here the streetscape changes: wide Soviet avenues give way to narrow lanes, flat-roofed adobe houses and the occasional blue-tiled portal. The two anchors are the Kukeldash Madrasa on the rise above the bazaar and the Barak-Khan Madrasa on the Hazrat Imam square, with the surviving mahallas filling the streets between.

How to see it

Treat the old city as a half-day on foot rather than a checklist. Start at Chorsu early, climb to the Kukeldash Madrasa, then let the side streets lead you through the mahallas toward the Hazrat Imam complex. It is an easy, atmospheric walk, broken up by teahouses and bakeries.

A note on respect

These are living religious and residential spaces, not a museum. Dress modestly at the mosques and madrasas — covered shoulders and knees, a scarf handy for women — keep your voice down inside, and ask before photographing people or into courtyards. A little courtesy goes a long way in the mahallas.

Frequently asked questions

Is there much of an old town left in Tashkent?

Less than in Samarkand or Bukhara — the 1966 earthquake and the Soviet rebuild erased a great deal — but the western quarter around Chorsu still has its madrasas, mosques and the narrow lanes of the traditional mahallas. It is the most historic-feeling part of the city.

What should I wear to visit the madrasas and mosques?

Dress modestly: covered shoulders and knees for everyone, and women may want a scarf for the working mosques. These remain places of prayer and study, so visit quietly and respectfully.
