What to eat in Tbilisi

Georgian food is one of the world's great undersung cuisines — bread you stir an egg into, dumplings you drink, walnuts in everything. Here's the field guide, dish by dish.

Last reviewed: June 7, 2026

Two dishes anchor the canon, and learning them is half the battle. Khinkali are the soup dumplings of the Caucasus: twisted pouches of spiced meat (or mushroom, or cheese) and hot broth, eaten by hand — grip the knot, flip, bite, sip the broth, devour, and leave the chewy top on your plate as a tally mark. Khachapuri is the cheese bread that comes in regional dialects: round Imeruli for every day, the cheese-topped Megruli for more, and the showstopping Adjaruli — a bread boat of molten sulguni cheese crowned with butter and a raw egg yolk that you stir into the cheese and tear the bread to scoop. One Adjaruli feeds two honest appetites.

Around those two orbit a deep supporting cast, much of it accidentally vegetarian thanks to centuries of Orthodox fasting. Badrijani: fried eggplant rolled around a walnut-garlic paste, topped with pomegranate. Pkhali: jewel-colored pâtés of spinach, beet, or bean pounded with walnut. Lobio: a slow-cooked bean stew eaten with crumbly cornbread (mchadi). Mtsvadi: vine-charred pork or veal skewers, the national barbecue. And the herbs and walnuts that thread through nearly everything, alongside the sour-spicy blends — khmeli suneli, blue fenugreek — that give Georgian food its unmistakable signature.

Eating it well in the city

Order family-style and over-order slightly — the table is meant to fill. Pace the khinkali (they\u2019re heavier than they look), save room you won\u2019t have for churchkhela from a market stall, and treat the side of adjika pepper paste with respect. The best way to compress the whole education into one evening is a food tour, which teaches the khinkali technique and the khachapuri map in real time; the best way to drink alongside it is covered in our natural wine bars guide; and the cultural frame for the whole feast is the supra.

Beyond the headliners

Once khinkali and khachapuri are behind you, the menu opens into a deep second tier worth ordering on purpose. Ostri, a spicy beef stew, and chakhokhbili, chicken braised with tomato and herbs, are the comfort mains; shkmeruli drowns fried chicken in a garlic-milk sauce that ruins you for ordinary roast chicken. Chashushuli (a hearty meat-and-tomato stew) and kuchmachi (spiced offal with pomegranate, better than it sounds) reward the adventurous. From the tone oven come the cheese breads' savory cousins, and from the grill comes mtsvadi, the vine-charred skewers that are the national barbecue. Wash it down with tarragon lemonade — the lurid green tarkhuna — if you're off the wine.

Save room, because the sweet and the in-between courses are half the fun. Churchkhela — walnuts strung and dipped in thickened grape juice — is the ancient travel food to buy fresh from a market rather than shrink-wrapped; pelamushi is its softer, pudding form. For breakfast or a snack, chase a warm nazuki (spiced sweet bread) or a fresh lobiani bean-bread from a bakery. The deeper context for how all of this lands on a shared table is the supra, the wine that belongs beside it is covered in our natural wine bars guide, and the fastest way to taste across the whole canon in one evening is a food tour.

Frequently asked questions

How do I actually eat khinkali?

Grip the doughy top knot, turn it upside down, bite a small hole in the side, sip the broth so it doesn’t escape, then eat the rest — and leave the knot (the kudi) on your plate. Counting the knots is how the old-timers tally who ate how many. Never use a fork; it punctures the broth and marks you instantly as a tourist.

Which khachapuri should I order?

Start with Imeruli (the round, evenly-filled everyday version), graduate to Adjaruli (the boat of bread with a molten cheese, butter, and egg center that you stir and tear into), and try Megruli (Imeruli with extra cheese on top) if you’re committed. Adjaruli is a meal for two; order accordingly unless you have ambitions.

What should vegetarians prioritize?

Georgia is a vegetarian’s quiet paradise thanks to centuries of Orthodox fasting cuisine: badrijani (walnut-stuffed eggplant rolls), lobio (spiced bean stew with cornbread), pkhali (vegetable-and-walnut pâtés), mushroom ketsi, and of course the cheese breads. You will not go hungry or bored.

Is the food spicy?

Not in the chili sense — Georgian cooking is about herbs, walnuts, garlic, and the sour-spicy spice blends (khmeli suneli, blue fenugreek) rather than heat. The one thing to watch is adjika, the red pepper paste served on the side; it has real kick, so dip before you drown.

What’s the one sweet to try?

Churchkhela — strings of walnuts dipped repeatedly in thickened grape juice until they form a chewy, candle-shaped bar. It looks alarming, travels well, and is the country’s oldest energy bar, originally made to feed soldiers. Buy it fresh from a market rather than the shrink-wrapped tourist versions.
