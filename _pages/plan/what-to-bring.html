WHAT TO BRING

What to bring to Petra

The day-pack checklist for a full day on site. Realistic quantities, what's worth the extra weight, and what you can leave at the hotel.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 6-minute read · See our editorial standards

Most travelers overpack for Petra. The site has no formal facilities beyond the Visitor Centre and the Basin Restaurant, but the things you actually need are surprisingly few — water, sun protection, comfortable shoes, cash, a camera, and a power bank cover 90% of a typical day. This page covers the non-obvious additions and the realistic quantities.

The essentials

Water — 2 to 3 liters per person. The single most important item. Most travelers underestimate. A full Petra day involves 8 to 12 kilometers of walking on uneven ground, often in direct sun, with significant elevation gain on the Monastery and high-place trails. Even in winter, 2 liters is the minimum; in summer, 3 liters is closer to comfortable. The Bedouin tea stalls sell water for 2–4 JOD per bottle but the prices rise the further you are from the Visitor Centre.

Sunscreen — SPF 50+. The Jordanian sun is stronger than most European or American travelers expect, even in winter. Apply before entering the site and reapply at least once during the day, particularly after the Monastery climb. The fair-skinned should bring a stick or spray for face touch-ups; sunburn is the most common Petra injury after blisters.

Hat or buff. A wide-brimmed hat is the best option. A buff (a cylindrical multi-functional cloth) is a close second and packs smaller — you can wear it as a hat, a neck protector, or a face cover against blowing sand. The Bedouin sellers inside Petra sell traditional shemagh (the checkered Jordanian headscarf) for around 8–12 JOD; these work well as both sun protection and a souvenir.

Sunglasses. Non-negotiable. The sandstone reflects UV strongly even on overcast days.

Comfortable closed-toe shoes. See the what to wear page for detail, but the headline is: trail runners or light hiking shoes are ideal, sneakers are acceptable, sandals and dress shoes are dangerous on the Monastery trail.

Cash — 30 to 50 JOD per person. The Petra entrance ticket is paid at the Visitor Centre (cash or card). Inside the site, almost everything is cash-only: tea stalls, donkey rides, souvenirs, restaurant bills outside the Basin's main room. Bring smaller denominations — 1, 5, and 10 JOD notes — because the Bedouin sellers rarely have change for a 50.

Phone with offline maps. Download the Petra map area on Google Maps before you enter (cell service is patchy inside the site, particularly on the Monastery trail). Maps.me and Organic Maps both have decent Petra coverage and work offline by default.

Power bank. 10,000 mAh is the sweet spot. A full day at Petra drains a phone battery completely, particularly if you're taking lots of photographs or running long-exposure shots.

Worth bringing

A small first aid kit. Blister bandages (Compeed or equivalent) are the most useful single item. Add ibuprofen, a few standard bandages, and antiseptic wipes. The Visitor Centre has a basic medical post but inside the site, you're on your own.

Light layers for early mornings. If you're entering at 06:00 — which you should — the morning temperature in Wadi Musa is typically 15–20°F colder than the afternoon. A light fleece or long-sleeve shirt that fits in your daypack matters. By 10:00 you'll have shed it.

Wet wipes or hand sanitizer. Petra is dusty. After 3 hours of walking, your hands are coated in fine red sand. Useful before lunch and before handling your camera.

A small camera in addition to your phone. Optional. Phone cameras handle Petra well in daytime light, less well in the Siq's deep shadows or at Petra by Night. If you have a dedicated camera, bring it; if not, your phone is fine.

Snacks for the trail. Granola bars, nuts, dried fruit. Easy to carry, helpful for blood sugar on the Monastery climb. Don't bring anything that melts.

Specifically for Petra by Night

The evening experience requires its own short list:

A warm jacket. Petra at night, particularly October through March, is significantly colder than the daytime — canyon temperatures regularly drop below 10°C (50°F), and there's no shelter at the Treasury plaza. A fleece is not enough; a proper warm jacket is.

A camera with long-exposure capability. The standard candlelit-Treasury photograph requires either a phone in night mode (held very steady) or a real camera on a small tripod. Bring whatever you have.

No flashlight. Flashlights are actively discouraged at Petra by Night — the candlelit experience depends on the soft light, and a flashlight beam disrupts it for everyone around you. Use the candle light or your phone screen at lowest brightness if you absolutely need to see.

What to leave at the hotel

Heavy backpacks. A daypack with the essentials is enough. The site has nowhere to "set up" and you'll just be carrying weight.

Tripods larger than a small travel model. Technically banned around the Treasury and the Monastery, rarely enforced for small tripods, often confiscated for full-size models. A Gorillapod or similar small tripod is fine.

Drones, unless you have a permit. The Royal Aviation Commission of Jordan requires advance permits for all drone flights, and Petra specifically restricts drone use even with general permits. Skip this unless you've planned ahead by 2+ weeks.

Selfie sticks. Technically banned around the Treasury, occasionally enforced. Some Bedouin staff will ask you to put them away.

Cash in foreign currency. US dollars and euros are accepted at the Visitor Centre ticket booth but not inside the site. Exchange for JOD before your visit.

The seasonal adjustments

Summer (June–August). Add: a second 1-liter water bottle, an electrolyte powder or oral rehydration salts, a cooling towel. Subtract: the light fleece (you won't need it even at 06:00).

Winter (December–February). Add: a proper warm jacket (not just a fleece), gloves for the morning Siq walk, a beanie. Subtract: one of the water bottles (you won't need 3 liters in 15°C weather).

Spring and autumn. The default list above is calibrated for these seasons.

Frequently asked questions

How much water do I need for a day at Petra?
Two to three liters per person, more in summer. Tap water in Jordan is technically potable but most travelers stick to bottled — buy a few large bottles at your hotel or in Wadi Musa before entering the site. Inside Petra, water from the Bedouin tea stalls runs 2 JOD per bottle and reaches 3–4 JOD on the Monastery trail. Bring more than you think you need.
Do I need cash inside Petra?
Yes — 30 to 50 JOD per person per day is the right amount. The Bedouin tea stalls, donkey rides, souvenir sellers, and the Basin Restaurant all prefer cash. Credit cards are accepted at the Visitor Centre ticket booth and at the Basin Restaurant's indoor area, but nowhere else inside the site. An ATM is available at the Visitor Centre if you need to top up.
Should I bring my own snacks?
A few snacks are useful, full meals aren't necessary. Granola bars, nuts, and dried fruit are easy to carry and help with the Monastery climb. The Basin Restaurant at the foot of the Monastery trail serves a reasonable lunch buffet (~18–20 JOD), and the Bedouin tea stalls along the trails sell tea, water, and basic snacks. The combination usually covers a full day. Avoid bringing anything that needs refrigeration or melts in the sun.
What about a power bank or charger?
Bring a power bank. A full day at Petra typically drains a phone battery (between photographs, navigation, and the camera taking long-exposure shots at the Monastery or Treasury). A 10,000 mAh power bank gives you 2–3 full phone recharges, which is enough for the longest days. No charging points exist inside the archaeological park.
Is there anywhere to store luggage?
The Visitor Centre has a small luggage storage facility (typically 2–3 JOD per bag per day) for travelers who arrive directly from Amman or Aqaba and want to visit Petra before checking into a hotel. Most Wadi Musa hotels will also store bags for free after checkout, which is the better option if your departure is the same day.
Anything I should avoid bringing?
Selfie sticks are technically banned (rarely enforced but the Petra Authority discourages them, particularly around the Treasury). Drones require a permit from the Royal Aviation Commission of Jordan — applications take 1–2 weeks and most travelers skip them. Heavy backpacks are unnecessary; the site has nowhere to "set up" and you'll just be carrying weight. Anything that looks like military gear (camouflage clothing, tactical bags) may attract additional scrutiny at the gate.
