Why Tbilisi, and why now

Tbilisi sits in a river gorge where Europe and Asia have been trading, feasting, and occasionally fighting for fifteen centuries. The result is a capital that doesn't look or feel like anywhere else: Persian-style bathhouses a five-minute walk from Art Nouveau mansions, Soviet modernism looming over medieval churches, and a generation of chefs and winemakers who have turned the city into one of the most talked-about food destinations in the world.

It is also genuinely easy to visit. Citizens of more than ninety countries can enter Georgia visa-free for a full year. English is widely spoken in tourism, hospitality runs deep in the culture, and costs remain well below Western European levels for hotels, restaurants, and transport. The hard part isn't logistics — it's deciding what to fit in.

That's what this site is for. Rather than a single sprawling article, the guide is organized into ten clusters, each one going from a broad overview down to the specific, practical detail you need to actually book and go: which sulfur bath to choose and what a private room costs, whether Kazbegi is doable as a day trip in winter, where the Bolt pickup point is at the airport, and which khachapuri you order first.

The shape of a Tbilisi trip

Most travelers base themselves in or near the Old Town — the tangle of lanes between Liberty Square and the sulfur baths — and spend their first day on foot: Narikala Fortress by cable car, the bathhouse district, the leaning clock tower, and dinner somewhere serving khinkali by the dozen. Day two usually adds Mtatsminda's funicular, the Holy Trinity Cathedral across the river, and the museums and wine bars of Rustaveli Avenue and Sololaki.

From day three, the day trips take over. Mtskheta, the ancient capital and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, is barely thirty minutes away. The Georgian Military Highway runs north past the Ananuri fortress and the Gudauri ski resort to Kazbegi and the Gergeti Trinity Church, photographed against a 5,000-meter peak. East lies Kakheti, the wine region where families have been fermenting in buried clay qvevri for millennia — a tradition on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list.

A long weekend covers the city. Five days covers the city plus two day trips without rushing. A week opens up Batumi, Kutaisi, or an overnight in the mountains. The itineraries cluster maps each of these out hour by hour.

How this guide works

Every page on tbilisitourism.com follows the same editorial standard: facts like prices, opening hours, and transport schedules are verified against official and primary sources before publication, each page carries the date it was last reviewed, and guides are re-checked on a rolling basis.
