What to see at Petra

Petra is bigger than the Treasury photo suggests — 264 square kilometers of carved tombs, hidden temples, sacrificial high places, and trails that most day-trippers never reach. Here's the complete guide.

The mistake most first-time visitors make at Petra is treating it as a single monument. The Treasury is what you came to see — it's also what every photograph and film clip and tourism poster prepares you to see — and that's exactly why the rest of the site is so disorienting. Petra is a city. The Nabataeans carved tombs into the cliffs, ran water through engineered channels for thousands of residents, built temples on the valley floor, established sacrificial sites on the high places overlooking the canyon, and connected it all with processional paths that are still walkable today. The Treasury is the front door. There are roughly 800 monuments behind it.

This page is the index to that city. We've structured it the way the site itself unfolds — starting with the Siq and the Treasury (which everyone sees), moving through the Royal Tombs and the lower city (which most people see), then up to the Monastery and out to the high-place trails (which roughly half of visitors see), and finally to the further-flung sites like Jebel Haroun, Little Petra, and the back routes that even seasoned visitors often skip.

Each monument and trail below links to its own dedicated page with photography timing, what's actually there, the historical context, and the practical details — opening hours, entry requirements, what guides won't tell you. If you have one day, the first three sections cover what you'll have time for. If you have two or more, keep reading.

The headline monuments

AL-KHAZNEH

The Treasury

The 40-meter Hellenistic facade at the end of the Siq. A 1st-century Nabataean tomb, almost certainly for King Aretas IV.

AD-DEIR

The Monastery

Petra's largest monument, 800 steps up at the far end of the site. Even bigger than the Treasury, far less photographed.

THE SIQ

The Siq

The 1.2 km sandstone canyon that leads to the Treasury. Not a transit corridor — it's one of the experiences itself.

ROYAL TOMBS

The Royal Tombs

The Urn Tomb, Silk Tomb, Corinthian Tomb, and Palace Tomb — four monumental facades carved into the eastern cliff.

PETRA BY NIGHT

Petra by Night

Sunday through Thursday: the Siq lit by over 2,000 candles. Worth doing once if you're staying overnight.

ROMAN THEATRE

The Roman Theatre

A 4,000-seat amphitheatre carved directly into the rock, with tomb chambers visible behind the upper rows.

The trails

Petra has seven significant marked trails. Five of them — the Main Trail, the Monastery, the High Place of Sacrifice, Al-Khubtha, and Little Petra — are accessible to anyone with reasonable fitness. The remaining two — Jebel Haroun and Umm al-Biyara — are full-day hikes that require an early start, real water, and ideally a guide. Worth knowing that several of the trails connect: the back route from Little Petra to the Monastery skips the 800 steps entirely, and the Wadi Farasa loop from the High Place of Sacrifice exits past the Garden Temple, a quiet area most visitors never see.

MAIN TRAIL

The Main Trail

The Visitor Centre to Qasr al-Bint and back. 8 km round-trip, mostly flat. The non-hiking baseline visit.

MONASTERY TRAIL

The Monastery Hike

800 ancient steps up to Ad-Deir. 2.5 km each way, 1 hour up. The single best ascent in Petra for most visitors.

AL-KHUBTHA

Al-Khubtha Trail

3.5 km up the cliff face to the Treasury viewpoint. The famous overhead photo, and far less crowded than the Monastery.

HIGH PLACE

High Place of Sacrifice

3 km up Jebel Madbah to the Nabataean ritual altar. Loop down through Wadi Farasa to skip the steps both ways.

JEBEL HAROUN

Jebel Haroun

Petra's highest peak (1,350m) and the traditional burial site of Aaron, brother of Moses. Full-day, strenuous.

LITTLE PETRA

Little Petra (Siq al-Barid)

The Nabataean caravan staging post 8 km north of the main site. Free, smaller, and home to the Painted Biclinium.

The lesser-known sites

Beyond the headline monuments and the marked trails, Petra contains hundreds of smaller carved structures, water-engineering features, and quiet corners that even seasoned visitors miss. None of them justify a single-purpose trip, but if you have a second day or a slow afternoon, they're what separate a good Petra visit from a great one.

The Qasr al-Bint is the largest free-standing structure at Petra and the only major monument that wasn't carved into a cliff — a 1st-century BC temple to Dushares, the principal Nabataean god. The Great Temple next door has been under American excavation since the 1990s and is one of the few places at the site where you can see active archaeology in progress. The Colonnaded Street connecting them was the city's main commercial axis, lined with shops and buildings that are still being uncovered.

Further afield, the Garden Temple in Wadi Farasa is part of the High Place of Sacrifice loop and contains an unusual Nabataean water-engineering structure. The Tomb of Sextius Florentinus, around the back of the Royal Tombs, is the only tomb at Petra with an identifiable occupant — a Roman governor of Arabia who asked to be buried there in 130 AD. And the Obelisk Tomb, sitting just outside the Visitor Centre, is the first major monument most visitors walk past without realizing what it is.

QASR AL-BINT

Qasr al-Bint

The temple of Dushares — Petra's largest free-standing structure and the spiritual heart of the city.

GREAT TEMPLE

The Great Temple

Under American excavation since the 1990s. The only place at Petra to watch active archaeology.

COLONNADED STREET

The Colonnaded Street

The Roman-era main street through Petra's commercial heart, lined with shop foundations.

Planning your visit

The order in which you encounter the monuments matters more than guides usually mention. Petra is a one-way canyon: you enter through the Siq, descend gradually through the Royal Tombs and Roman theatre to the lower city, climb to the Monastery at the far end, and then have to walk all the way back. By 11 AM, the Treasury plaza is full of cruise ship passengers and Eilat day-trippers; by 2 PM, the heat is real. The traditional advice — arrive at 6 AM, do the Monastery first while you have energy, save the Treasury for the late-afternoon return walk when the light is better and the crowds have left — is the right advice. We have a full breakdown on the best time to visit page.

If you only have one day, the realistic itinerary is the Siq, the Treasury, the Royal Tombs, the Roman theatre, and one of: the Monastery (if you have the legs), Al-Khubtha (if you want the photo), or the High Place of Sacrifice (if you want the views without the steps). Trying to do all three in a single day is possible but punishing. We have detailed itineraries for one day, two days, and three days.

Most visitors come on a tour from Amman, Aqaba, or Eilat, and a tour is the right answer for first-timers who want logistics handled. Independent travelers should consider the JETT bus from Amman, which is by far the cheapest way to get there. Either way, you'll want a Jordan Pass — it bundles your visa fee with Petra entry and pays for itself the moment you arrive.

Frequently asked questions

How long do you need to see Petra properly?
Two days is the right answer for most visitors. One day covers the headline monuments — the Siq, the Treasury, the Royal Tombs, the Roman theatre, and the Monastery — but only if you start at 6 AM and don't stop walking. Two days lets you do at least one of the high-place trails (Al-Khubtha for the Treasury viewpoint, or the High Place of Sacrifice) without rushing, and gives you a second pass at the Siq in different light. Three days is for serious hikers planning Jebel Haroun, photographers, or anyone who wants a leisurely visit.
What's the difference between the Treasury and the Monastery?
The Treasury (Al-Khazneh) is the iconic facade you see in every Petra photograph — a 40-meter-tall Hellenistic-style tomb at the end of the Siq, almost certainly built for Nabataean king Aretas IV around 1 AD. The Monastery (Ad-Deir) is even larger (50m wide, 45m high) but rougher in execution, located at the top of an 800-step climb at the far end of the site. The Treasury is more photogenic; the Monastery is more impressive in person, partly because of how few people make the climb.
How many steps to the Monastery?
Roughly 800, depending on how you count. The official trail starts behind the Basin Restaurant in the lower city and climbs steadily up an old processional route carved into the cliff. Plan for 45 minutes to an hour going up, less coming down. Donkey hire is available for around 20 JOD return — we strongly recommend walking up and considering a donkey down only if you're struggling, since donkeys descend the steep steps quickly and the ride is genuinely uncomfortable.
Where is the famous Treasury viewpoint photo taken from?
The Al-Khubtha Trail. It branches off the main path just past the Royal Tombs and climbs roughly 200 meters up the cliff face on the north side of the canyon. The Bedouin-run viewpoint at the top sits directly above the Treasury, looking down at the facade from a flat rocky ledge with carpets laid out for visitors. It's a 1.5–2 hour round trip, moderately strenuous, and the most rewarding hike in Petra for a typical visitor.
Is Petra by Night worth it?
It's worth doing once if you're already staying overnight in Wadi Musa. The show runs Sunday through Thursday at 20:30 (expanded from three nights a week in late 2025) — over 2,000 candles line the Siq, the walk to the Treasury is genuinely magical, and a Bedouin musician plays before a 3D projection-mapping show on the Treasury facade. The criticism is that the projection portion is short and divides opinion, but the candle-lit Siq walk is the real reason to go. Tickets are 30 JOD, separate from your daytime entrance and not included in the Jordan Pass.
Can you visit Little Petra and is it worth the time?
Yes and yes — it's about 8 km north of the main Petra entrance, free to visit, and most travelers spend an hour or two there. Little Petra (officially Siq al-Barid, "the cold canyon") was the Nabataean caravan staging post for traders coming in from the north. The scale is much smaller than the main site, but the painted ceiling in the Painted Biclinium is one of the few surviving examples of Nabataean interior decoration anywhere. Worth combining with a half-day at the main Petra site, not a full-day visit on its own.
