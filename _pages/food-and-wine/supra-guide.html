Food & Wine

The supra explained

The Georgian feast is not just dinner — it's the culture's central ritual, run by a toastmaster, fueled by wine, and built on toasts that climb from the dead to the future. Here's how it works.

Last reviewed: June 7, 2026

The supra is where Georgian hospitality becomes architecture. The table is loaded with every dish at once — no courses, just abundance, replenished faster than you can clear it — and the meal is structured not by food but by toasts, delivered in sequence by the tamada, the toastmaster chosen for eloquence and endurance. You do not drink freely; you drink to his toasts, which climb a near-ritual ladder: peace first, then the occasion, then — standing, in silence — those who have died, then ancestors, children, women, friendship, the homeland. Guests are invited to elaborate, and sincerity outranks brevity or polish.

There are two registers. The keipi is the joyful supra — a birthday, a wedding, a Tuesday that got out of hand — and the memorial supra is its solemn twin, same structure, different weight. Both run on wine, poured generously and toasted seriously; chacha, the grape spirit, circulates for the brave. The tamada paces it all, and a great one turns a dinner into something between a performance and a liturgy, the kind of evening guests describe for years.

Being a good guest

If you\u2019re invited — and Georgian hospitality makes it likelier than you expect — go, and go prepared. Bring a small gift, defer to the tamada, eat continuously (it\u2019s the only real defense against the wine), and when your turn to toast comes, speak from somewhere honest rather than clever. Refusing a toast outright reads as cold; sipping rather than draining is usually forgiven from a guest. Don\u2019t plan anything for afterward, and don\u2019t try to leave early — you won\u2019t win. The wine that fuels it is the qvevri tradition, the dishes are the canon covered elsewhere in this cluster, and the deepest versions happen out in Kakheti, where the wine and the table were born together.

Where a visitor meets one

You don't have to wait for an invitation to feel the supra's gravity. Its rhythms run through ordinary restaurant dinners — the loaded table, the shared dishes, the wine treated as food — and some venues stage live polyphonic singing, the UNESCO-listed three-part harmony that is the supra's soundtrack and one of the most moving sounds in the country. A food tour that ends in a long group table is the gentlest structured introduction, and any wine evening in Vera carries the DNA. But the real thing — a private supra in someone's home or a village — tends to find you, through a guesthouse host, a guide, or the Georgian habit of turning a chance acquaintance into a feast.

If it happens, the deepest version is out in the country, and especially in Kakheti, where the wine and the table were born together and a harvest-season (rtveli) supra is the high point of the Georgian year. Bring a small gift, defer to the tamada, eat continuously, toast from somewhere honest rather than clever, and abandon any plan for afterward. It is, by common consent of everyone who has sat through one, the single most memorable thing Georgia offers a traveler — the point all the food and wine has been building toward.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly is a supra?

A Georgian feast — the central ritual of the culture’s hospitality. A table loaded with dishes all at once (not in courses), endless wine, and a structured sequence of toasts led by a tamada, or toastmaster. It can be a joyful celebration (keipi) or a solemn memorial; both follow the same architecture.

Who is the tamada?

The toastmaster who runs the table — chosen for eloquence and stamina, he (traditionally) sets the order and theme of toasts, decides the pace, and gives everyone room to speak. A good tamada turns dinner into theater. You drink to his toasts, not whenever you feel like it; freelance sipping between toasts is mild bad form.

What do people toast to?

A near-fixed sequence: peace, the occasion or hosts, those who have passed (drunk in silence, standing), parents and ancestors, children and the future, women, friendship, the homeland, and onward. The toasts build from the solemn to the warm, and guests are often invited to elaborate — brevity is fine, sincerity is required.

How do I avoid getting dangerously drunk?

Pace, eat constantly, and respect the wine’s honesty. You toast with wine (refusing a toast outright is rude, but sipping rather than draining is usually accepted from guests), and chacha — the grape spirit — should be approached with deep caution. Eating throughout, which the loaded table encourages, is the real defense.

Will I ever be invited to a real one?

More easily than you’d think — Georgian hospitality toward guests is close to a national religion, and a casual acquaintance can become a supra invitation fast. If it happens, go: bring a small gift, defer to the tamada, toast sincerely, and accept that leaving early is nearly impossible. It’s the deepest cultural experience the country offers.
