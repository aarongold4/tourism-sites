Getting to Tbilisi

Tbilisi is the transport hub of the Caucasus — two useful airports, a fast train to the coast, and overland routes in every direction. Here's how the pieces fit, and which option wins for your route.

Last reviewed: June 7, 2026

Two airports serve the capital question. Tbilisi International (TBS)sits about seventeen kilometers east of the center and handles the full-service and regional carriers; it's the simple choice when fares are comparable, since you land in the city you're visiting. Its one quirk matters for planning: a large share of flights, especially from Europe and the Gulf, arrive between midnight and dawn — book accommodation with genuine 24-hour check-in and decide your transfer before you land rather than half-asleep in arrivals.

Kutaisi (KUT) is the low-cost gateway — Wizz Air built its Georgian base there — and it sits in a different city entirely, roughly three and a half to four hours from Tbilisi by road. The transfer economy around it is mature: coach companies time departures to flight arrivals and sell seats online in advance, so the journey is longer but rarely complicated. For many European travelers the low-cost fare plus the coach still beats flying direct to TBS; the Kutaisi page runs the math.

From the airport to your bed

From TBS, the trio of options is Bolt, the fixed-rate taxi desk, and city bus 337. Bolt is what most visitors use: order from the app in arrivals, walk to the pickup point, and ride to the center in twenty-five to forty minutes depending on traffic. The bus is the budget play — it runs toward Station Square through the center, costs small change, and takes a bank-card tap on board; the trade is time, with the full run over an hour. A twice-daily airport rail link to the central station exists and is charming, but its sparse timetable makes it a curiosity more than a plan. The dedicated page keeps current fares, night-service status, and the pickup-point geography, all of which shift often enough that we re-verify them every review pass.

Trains, coaches, and marshrutkas

Domestically, the headline service is the modern double-decker train between Tbilisi and Batumi on the Black Sea — around five hours, assigned seating, and a far gentler experience than the highway. Tickets sell through Georgian Railways online and at stations; summer weekends sell out, so book once dates firm up. Everywhere the rails don't go, the marshrutka network does: shared minibuses radiating from Didube and other stations to every town in the country, departing when full, costing very little, and demanding a small tolerance for adventure. Between the two sit the scheduled coach operators, which add luggage space and fixed timetables on the main intercity runs.

Internationally, Tbilisi connects overland to Yerevan and Baku by both road and overnight rail — slow, sociable journeys that suit travelers stitching the Caucasus together. Those routes, plus the land borders from Turkey, get their own pages as the cluster expands.

Inside the city

Once you're in, the system is simple: walk the center, tap a contactless card on the metro and buses for the rest, and use Bolt where the hills or hours argue against both. The metro's two lines cover the river axis usefully — Station Square, Liberty Square, Avlabari for Sameba — and the bus fleet is modern and tracked accurately on Google Maps. Street taxis still exist and still overcharge the unwary, which is the entire reason Bolt won; the Bolt page covers the residual tricks, including the unofficial "taxi" offers inside the airport terminal.

Getting around once you arrive

Inside the city, the system is cheap, modern, and largely cashless. The metro runs two lines that cover the practical axis — the airport-side stations, the center, the train station at Station Square, and Avlabari for Sameba — with deep Soviet-built stations and trains every few minutes; you tap a contactless bank card or the rechargeable Metromoney card at the gate for small change. Buses and the yellow municipal fleet reach everywhere the metro doesn't, paid the same way, with routes legible in Google Maps. For door-to-door, Bolt has effectively replaced the street-taxi economy: fares are pre-agreed in the app, low by any standard, and far safer than the curbside quotes the airport and bath district still attract.

Two pieces of the network are attractions in their own right. The Rike–Narikala cable car floats you up to the fortress for the price of a bus ride, and the century-old Mtatsminda funicular climbs the holy mountain to the city's best terrace. But the truth most visitors land on is that central Tbilisi is a walking city — the historic core, the baths, the river, and Rustaveli all sit within a compact, if steep and cobbled, radius, and good shoes do more for your trip than any transit pass. Save the apps and the metro for crossing the river or reaching the outer neighborhoods.

Frequently asked questions

Which airport should I fly into?

Tbilisi (TBS) if schedules and fares are comparable — it’s in the city. Kutaisi (KUT) is the budget-carrier hub, roughly three and a half to four hours from Tbilisi by coach; the all-in cost and time often still wins for travelers on low-cost routes from Europe, and the transfer is well organized around flight times.

How do I get from Tbilisi Airport to the center?

Three real options: Bolt (order in the app from arrivals; the ride into the center takes roughly twenty-five to forty minutes), the official taxi desk at fixed rates, or city bus 337 toward Station Square via the center — by far the cheapest, paid by tapping a bank card on board. Exact current fares and the late-night picture live on the airport page.

Can I arrive overland from Armenia or Azerbaijan?

Yes — Tbilisi is the hub of the Caucasus. From Yerevan, shared minivans and a slow but scenic overnight train both run; from Baku, an overnight train and buses. Border formalities are routine for visa-free nationalities. Detailed overland pages join this cluster in a later phase.

Is the train from Batumi worth taking over flying or driving?

Usually, yes. The modern double-decker train links the Black Sea coast to Tbilisi in around five hours city-center to city-center, with assigned seats and far less fatigue than the highway. Book ahead in summer — seats sell out around weekends and holidays.

Do I need to rent a car?

Not for Tbilisi, and not for the classic day trips — tours, drivers, and marshrutkas cover those. A rental starts making sense for multi-stop trips into the regions (Svaneti, remote Kakheti), with the caveats that Georgian driving style is assertive and the zero-tolerance alcohol limit is enforced.
