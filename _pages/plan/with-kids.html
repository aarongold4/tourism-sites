WITH KIDS

Visiting Petra with children

Kids generally love Petra — the Siq feels adventurous, the carved facades are dramatic, and the Bedouin tea stalls are friendly. The constraints (uneven ground, lots of walking, no shade) are real but manageable with a 4-6 hour family-paced visit.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 7-minute read · See our editorial standards

Petra works well as a family destination — better than most archaeological sites of its scale. The Siq genuinely feels like an adventure (kids respond strongly to the slot canyon's narrowness), the Treasury reveal at the end is dramatic enough to register even with younger children, and the carved facades of the Royal Tombs photograph well at any age. The Bedouin tea stalls are warm and welcoming with children, and the donkey rides — for all their complications — are a hit with most kids.

The constraints are also real. Petra is a moderate hiking destination, not a museum with paved paths and shaded benches. Kids under 6 typically need to be carried or stroller-pushed for parts of the day. The Monastery climb is too demanding for most kids under 10. The site has limited shade and no formal play spaces. And families should plan for substantially shorter visits than the standard adult itinerary — 4 to 6 hours is realistic, not the 8 to 10 hours an adult might do.

What works at what age

Toddlers and preschoolers (0–5)

Doable but logistically demanding. A hiking backpack carrier (Deuter, Osprey, or similar) is the right choice — strollers struggle past the first 800 meters and the rough surfaces stress both kid and parent. The horse-drawn carriage to the Treasury (~25 JOD round trip) is the standard fallback for families with very young children, but be honest about the experience: the carriage ride is bumpy on the cobbled Siq floor, and many young children find it more stressful than the walk.

The right family itinerary at this age: enter at 08:00 (later than the adult ideal), take the carriage or walk to the Treasury (45–60 minutes), spend an hour at the Treasury plaza and the Street of Facades, return to the Visitor Centre. Total time: 3–4 hours. Skip the lower city, the Monastery, and all trails.

Early elementary (6–9)

The sweet spot for Petra family visits. Kids this age can typically walk the full Siq, handle the lower city, and have meaningful interest in the carved monuments. The Monastery climb is borderline — some kids manage it, others hit a wall halfway up. Plan for the full headline visit (Siq, Treasury, Royal Tombs from below, lower city) and decide on the Monastery based on how the morning goes.

Make it a game: count the carved camel reliefs in the Siq, find the water channels on the walls, look for the bishop's inscription inside the Urn Tomb. The site rewards attention; kids who are encouraged to look closely typically engage more than kids who are just walked through.

Tweens and teens (10–17)

Effectively adult visits, with two adjustments. First, the social phone-photo experience matters — let them have meaningful photo time at the Treasury and the Monastery rather than rushing past. Second, the Wadi Farasa loop (or the Al-Khubtha trail) is more interesting than just the main route, and most teens handle the longer hikes well. By 14 or 15, the full two-day itinerary works without modification.

The realistic family itinerary

For a family with kids aged roughly 6–12, here's the schedule we'd recommend:

07:30: Enter the site. The early-bird 06:00 strategy is harder with kids and the gain is smaller — by 07:30 the Treasury isn't yet crowded with day-trippers and the morning light is still good.

07:50: Begin walking the Siq. Don't rush. Point out the water channels, the camel relief, the niches. The Siq itself is the part many kids find most exciting.

08:30: Treasury reveal. Spend 45 minutes here — photos, snack break, the kids will want to look. If you've offered a camel ride (~10 JOD each), this is where they happen.

09:15: Walk past the Street of Facades and the Royal Tombs to the Roman theatre. Climb briefly on the lower seats. Allow 45 minutes.

10:00: Walk the colonnaded street to the Basin Restaurant area. The Great Temple ruins and the lower city are interesting but kids' attention will be flagging.

10:45: Lunch at the Basin Restaurant (~18 JOD buffet for adults, often half-price for kids; reasonable Jordanian food, shaded outdoor seating).

12:00: Begin the slow walk back. Take the carriage or donkey for kids who are flagging (the carriage is 25 JOD round trip and meets travelers at multiple points along the lower city — ask any of the carriage drivers).

13:30: Exit. Back to your Wadi Musa hotel for an afternoon at the pool, ice cream, and naps.

Total time on site: 6 hours. That's enough for the headline experience without exhausting kids past the point of memory formation.

The donkey question

Petra has working donkeys, horses, and camels operated by Bedouin families. The animals are part of the local economy and most of the operators are skilled handlers with multi-generation experience. The welfare conditions, however, are inconsistent — some animals are well-cared for, others are visibly worked too hard or fed too little.

The Petra National Trust has been working with local operators to improve standards, and conditions have gradually improved over the past decade. But the practical situation today is: each ride is a separate ethical decision based on the specific animal you're considering. Look at the donkey before agreeing. Healthy donkeys are alert, have clean eyes, no visible saddle sores, and are at a reasonable weight. Avoid any animal that looks distressed.

For shorter, less strenuous routes (Visitor Centre to Treasury via the horse-drawn carriage), the welfare situation is generally better than for the Monastery donkey ride, which is the most physically demanding work the animals do. We don't recommend the Monastery donkey ride; if a child genuinely cannot make the climb, the better choice is to skip the Monastery entirely and plan a return visit when they're older.

What to pack specifically for kids

  • Extra water — kids dehydrate faster than adults, particularly in summer
  • Snacks — granola bars, dried fruit, nuts (avoid anything that melts)
  • A change of clothes in your daypack — kids will spill, fall, or get hot
  • Sunscreen — apply before entering, reapply at lunch
  • A hat for each child — non-negotiable
  • Wet wipes — Petra is dusty, and you'll be glad of them
  • Blister bandages — kids' shoes often aren't broken in for hiking
  • A small toy or book for the lunch break and downtime

Activities kids tend to love

The horse-drawn carriage ride from the Visitor Centre to the Treasury, the Bedouin tea stalls (offer them sweet mint tea — a real cultural moment), looking for the camel reliefs in the Siq, watching the cats that live around the Basin Restaurant, the small Bedouin souvenir stalls (small carved sand bottles cost 2–5 JOD and are a memorable take-home), and — for kids old enough — the Treasury plaza Bedouin guides who tell stories about Indiana Jones being filmed there.

Frequently asked questions

Is Petra suitable for children?
Yes, with realistic expectations. Kids generally enjoy Petra — the Siq feels adventurous, the carved facades are dramatically photographable, donkey and camel rides are available, and Bedouin tea stalls break up the walking. The constraints are real though: 4–6 hours of walking on uneven ground is the realistic family-paced visit, the Monastery climb is too much for most kids under 10, and there's no shade or formal play space. Plan for shorter visits and more breaks than you would with adults.
What's the age cutoff for the entrance fee?
Children under 12 are free with a paying adult. Petra by Night is free for children under 10. There's no formal Jordan Pass for children, but kids under 12 get free Petra entry whether the accompanying adult uses a Jordan Pass or pays separately at the booth.
Is Petra stroller-friendly?
Not really. The path from the Visitor Centre to the Siq is paved and stroller-passable for the first 800 meters. After that, the surfaces get progressively rougher — the Siq has a paved floor but with uneven joints, and the lower city is mostly compact dirt and sand. A jogging stroller with large wheels works for the main path; a standard urban stroller will struggle. Most parents with young children either use a hiking backpack carrier or take the horse-drawn carriage to the Treasury (the carriage costs ~25 JOD round trip).
How long should I plan for a family visit?
4–6 hours on site is realistic for families with kids under 12, versus the 8–10 hours an adult visit takes. The compressed family itinerary: Siq, Treasury, walk into the lower city, maybe the Roman theatre and a brief look at the Royal Tombs from below, lunch at the Basin Restaurant or back at the entrance, and return through the Siq. The Monastery climb is the major attraction families typically skip — kids under 10 rarely make it, and it adds 3 hours to an already-tiring day.
Are the donkey rides ethical?
Mixed. The donkeys at Petra are working animals, owned by Bedouin families, and their welfare conditions vary significantly. Some are well-cared-for; some are visibly underfed or overworked. The Petra National Trust has worked with operators to improve standards but progress is uneven. Our recommendation: if you have to use one for a child who genuinely cannot walk further, pick a donkey that looks healthy and well-cared for, and pay the higher rate (it goes to the better operators). Generally, walking is preferable for both ethical and experience reasons.
What about toilets and changing facilities?
The Visitor Centre has clean modern toilets. Inside the site, there are basic toilets at the Treasury plaza, at the Basin Restaurant, and at the Monastery cafe — variable quality, generally functional. Changing facilities are limited; pack what you need.
