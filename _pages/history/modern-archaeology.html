HISTORY

Modern archaeology at Petra

A century of systematic excavation has transformed how we understand the site. From Walter Bachmann's 1916 site mapping to the 1990 Petra Church discovery to ongoing international team work — here's the archaeological history that shapes what we know today.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 6-minute read · See our editorial standards

The Petra you visit today exists in the form it does because of a century of systematic archaeological work. The 19th-century European explorers (see the explorers page) established Petra in the international imagination but produced limited rigorous documentation. The actual archaeological knowledge — site mapping, chronological dating, monument identification, cultural interpretation — comes from the systematic work that began in the early 20th century and continues today.

This page covers the major archaeological projects and discoveries that have shaped modern understanding of Petra. The framing throughout: the visible site is the product of cumulative professional work, much of it still ongoing, and recognizing this dimension adds depth to the visit.

The early 20th-century foundations

Brünnow and Domaszewski (1898-1909)

The German scholars Rudolf-Ernst Brünnow and Alfred von Domaszewski conducted the first comprehensive scholarly survey of Petra and the broader Roman province of Arabia. Their three-volume Die Provincia Arabia (1904-1909) became the standard reference for the broader region for decades. The work focused on description, photography, and basic mapping rather than excavation, but it established the academic baseline that later researchers built on.

Walter Bachmann (1916-1921)

The German architect Walter Bachmann produced the first comprehensive measured drawings of the major Petra monuments during his work at the site between 1916-1921. His drawings of the Treasury, the Monastery, the Royal Tombs, and other carved facades remain useful reference documents today — many of them more detailed than later photographic records, particularly for elements that have since suffered weathering damage.

Bachmann's work was part of a broader German archaeological program in the Ottoman Levant during the early 20th century. The First World War interrupted his work; the post-war establishment of British and French mandates over the broader region created new political contexts for archaeological access.

The 1929 Horsfield expedition

George Horsfield, the British Director of Antiquities for Transjordan in the late 1920s, led the first systematic excavation at Petra (as opposed to surface survey). His work focused on the colonnaded street area and produced the first stratigraphic dating of major Nabataean civic structures. The excavation methodology was rudimentary by modern standards but established the basic chronological framework that subsequent work refined.

The Brown University Great Temple project (1993-2008)

One of the major modern archaeological efforts at Petra was the Brown University excavation of the Great Temple complex, led by Martha Sharp Joukowsky between 1993 and 2008. The 15-year project systematically excavated one of the largest free-standing structures at Petra, producing detailed understanding of:

  • The structure's construction phases (late 1st century BCE through Roman-period modifications)
  • The complex's likely religious and civic functions
  • The architectural details and decorative program
  • The Byzantine-period reuse and adaptive modifications
  • The post-earthquake destruction layers

The Brown work produced a substantial body of publications and conserved the excavated material for future research. The Great Temple area today reflects the post-excavation state — partially restored foundations, identified architectural elements, and signage providing visitor context.

The Petra Church discovery (1990)

The most significant single archaeological discovery at Petra in modern times. The American Center of Oriental Research (ACOR), led by Pierre Bikai, discovered the Byzantine basilica on the slope above the colonnaded street in 1990. The subsequent excavation through the 1990s and early 2000s produced:

The mosaic floors. Three substantially intact 6th-century mosaic floors with sophisticated decorative programs (personifications of the seasons, animals, birds, vegetal motifs). The mosaics rank among the highest-quality Byzantine mosaics in the broader region.

The carbonized papyrus scrolls. Dozens of papyrus documents preserved by carbonization during a 6th-century fire. The scrolls record church business, real estate contracts, family documents, and personal letters from 6th-century Petra — a unique window into daily life of the period since paper documents almost never survive in normal archaeological contexts.

The architectural detail. The basilica's plan, construction sequence, and modifications over its working life. The church was destroyed by fire in the late 6th or early 7th century, sealing the archaeological context exceptionally well.

The Petra Church discovery transformed understanding of Byzantine-period Petra (see Byzantine Petra). The carbonized scrolls continue to be conserved and read at ACOR's facilities in Amman; new readings produce new historical insights periodically.

Other major modern projects

The Petra National Trust conservation work

The Petra National Trust (established 1989) coordinates conservation work across the broader Petra archaeological park, including stabilization of damaged structures, erosion control on the Siq walls, and visitor infrastructure development. The Trust's work isn't excavation per se but supports the broader archaeological enterprise by preserving sites for future research.

The German Archaeological Institute work

Multiple German Archaeological Institute (DAI) projects have focused on specific monument complexes and on the broader Nabataean kingdom context. Work at the Temple of the Winged Lions, the Petra Pool and Garden Complex, and various peripheral sites has been particularly significant.

The University of Sydney work

Australian researchers have conducted major work on the Wadi Mataha area, including the carved tomb complexes and water management features in this less-visited portion of the broader Petra site.

The Bdoul ethnographic studies

Beyond traditional excavation, modern researchers have conducted ethnographic and oral history work with the Bdoul community, documenting traditional knowledge of the site and the community's continuous relationship with Petra. The work informs both archaeological interpretation and contemporary site management. See the Bdoul page.

What ongoing work reveals

Continuous excavation and analysis since the 1990s has produced significant updates to the previous understanding of Petra:

Earlier Nabataean activity than previously thought. Excavation in some areas has produced evidence of substantial Nabataean activity dating to the 3rd-2nd centuries BCE, earlier than the traditional 1st-century BCE peak period. The Nabataean development was longer and more gradual than the older "sudden flourishing under Aretas IV" model suggested.

The role of pre-Nabataean settlement. Evidence of earlier (Iron Age, Hellenistic-period) occupation in the Petra basin indicates the Nabataean city was built on existing settlement patterns rather than founding the site from nothing.

The complexity of religious practice. Excavations of small shrines and ritual spaces have produced more nuanced understanding of Nabataean religion than the older "Dushara plus Allat" model — multiple deities, complex ritual programs, and significant variation across the city.

The post-Byzantine continuity. Archaeological evidence has clarified that Petra wasn't simply abandoned after the 363 earthquake or the 7th-century Arab conquest — the population continued at reduced scale for centuries longer than previously thought, with archaeological evidence of occupation through the 8th-9th centuries and (in some areas) into the medieval period.

What remains unexcavated

The visible monumental architecture has been documented in detail, but estimates suggest only 15-20% of the broader Petra archaeological zone has been systematically excavated. The unexcavated material includes:

  • Residential neighborhoods. The actual housing where 20,000-30,000 Nabataeans and later inhabitants lived. Largely undocumented at scale.
  • Peripheral cemeteries. Substantial burial areas around the central monuments, only partially surveyed.
  • Surrounding settlements. Satellite communities and rural housing in the broader Petra region.
  • Small religious sites. Dozens of small shrines, betyl niches, and ritual spaces identified but not excavated.
  • Industrial and craft areas. The Nabataean economy involved substantial craft production (pottery, metal, leather, dye) at sites only partially identified.
  • Water management infrastructure. Many of the smaller dams, channels, and cisterns identified but not fully documented.

Ongoing excavation continues to produce significant findings. Petra is not an exhausted archaeological site; it's an active research location with decades of further work in prospect.

Where to engage with the archaeology

For visitors specifically interested in the archaeological dimension:

  • The Petra Museum at the Visitor Centre. Regularly updated exhibits cover recent findings, methodology, and conservation work. The displays include actual artifacts (pottery, coins, small finds) alongside interpretive material. Free to enter; budget 30-45 minutes.
  • The American Center of Oriental Research (ACOR) in Amman. Lecture programs and library access for travelers with specific research interests. Public lectures during the spring and fall research seasons.
  • The Petra National Trust visitor materials. The Trust produces publications, signage, and online resources supporting deeper visitor engagement.
  • Active excavation sites. When work is in progress (typically spring and fall), some excavation sites are visible from the standard visitor paths; posted information sometimes identifies the work.

Frequently asked questions

When did systematic archaeology at Petra begin?
The early 20th century. Walter Bachmann's German documentation work in 1916-1921 established the basic site mapping and produced the first comprehensive measured drawings of major monuments. The Brünnow and Domaszewski survey (Die Provincia Arabia, 1904-1909) was an even earlier scholarly publication but focused on description rather than excavation. The first systematic excavations began in the 1920s-1930s, and sustained professional archaeology has continued essentially continuously since the 1950s.
Who's working at Petra today?
Multiple international research teams in partnership with the Jordanian Department of Antiquities. The American Center of Oriental Research (ACOR) has been a major continuous presence since the 1980s — they led the Petra Church discovery and the carbonized papyrus scroll conservation. Brown University excavated the Great Temple complex from 1993-2008. Other major institutions involved include the German Archaeological Institute, the French CNRS programs, the University of Sydney, and the Council for British Research in the Levant. Jordanian university researchers from Yarmouk and Jordan universities lead substantial portions of current work.
What's the most important recent discovery?
The Petra Church and its carbonized papyrus scrolls, discovered in 1990 and excavated through the early 2000s. The Byzantine basilica preserves intact mosaic floors and (uniquely) dozens of carbonized papyrus documents from the 6th century that survived because of a destructive fire — paper documents from this period almost never survive in normal archaeological contexts. The Petra Papyri are still being conserved and read, providing detailed insight into daily life at Byzantine Petra.
How much of Petra is still unexcavated?
A substantial majority — estimates suggest only 15-20% of the broader Petra archaeological zone has been systematically excavated. The visible monumental architecture (Treasury, Monastery, Royal Tombs) has been documented in detail, but the residential neighborhoods, smaller monuments, peripheral cemeteries, surrounding settlements, and broader infrastructure are largely undocumented. Ongoing excavation continues to produce new discoveries at a steady pace.
Can I see archaeological work happening?
Sometimes, depending on timing. Active excavation typically happens in spring and fall (March-May, September-November) to avoid the summer heat and winter cold. Some excavation sites are visible from the standard tourist paths; others are in areas not regularly accessed by visitors. Posted information sometimes identifies current work; the on-site Petra Museum at the Visitor Centre regularly updates its exhibits with recent findings.
