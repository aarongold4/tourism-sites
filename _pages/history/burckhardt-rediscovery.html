HISTORY

The rediscovery of Petra

August 22, 1812. A Swiss explorer in disguise as a Muslim pilgrim walked through Petra in a single afternoon and became the first European to see the site in roughly 1,400 years. His journal, published posthumously, opened the ancient city to the Western world.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 7-minute read · See our editorial standards

The story of Petra's "rediscovery" is a story that needs careful telling, because the word "rediscovered" obscures a more interesting truth. Petra was never lost to the people who lived around it — the Bdoul Bedouin tribe had occupied the site continuously from the city's gradual abandonment in the early medieval period. What was lost was the European awareness of Petra, the connection between the classical references in Strabo and Pliny and the actual physical site, which had been severed for over a millennium by the time a Swiss explorer named Johann Ludwig Burckhardt arrived in disguise as a Muslim pilgrim on the afternoon of August 22, 1812.

What Burckhardt did was bring Petra back into the European intellectual world — and through it, eventually, into the global tourism economy that supports the modern Petra Archaeological Park. The story of his single afternoon at the site is one of the more genuinely interesting moments in early 19th-century exploration, and the documentary record he left is unusually rich for an eyewitness account from that period.

The explorer

Johann Ludwig Burckhardt was born in Lausanne, Switzerland in 1784 to a wealthy merchant family. Trained as a classical Orientalist at Leipzig and Göttingen — institutions that, in the early 19th century, were producing some of the most rigorous European scholars of Arabic language and Islamic culture — Burckhardt was a natural candidate for the British-funded explorations of the Middle East that were intensifying after the Napoleonic disruption of established travel routes.

In 1809, the African Association (a British learned society dedicated to exploration of the African interior) recruited Burckhardt for a multi-year mission. The ultimate goal was to reach Timbuktu via the trans-Saharan caravan routes. To prepare, Burckhardt spent two years in the Middle East perfecting his Arabic and his knowledge of Islamic religious practice. He adopted the alias "Sheikh Ibrahim ibn Abdallah," presented himself as a Muslim convert from India, and traveled extensively through Syria, the Levant, and what is now Jordan and Saudi Arabia under that identity.

The disguise worked. By 1812, Burckhardt could speak Arabic fluently enough to pass casual conversation, understood Islamic religious practice well enough to perform the daily prayers convincingly, and looked the part of a slightly travel-worn pilgrim of indeterminate origins. He was 28 years old.

The journey south

In June 1812, Burckhardt set out from Damascus heading south toward Cairo, planning a leisurely overland journey that would take him through the area east of the Dead Sea — a region that had received almost no European exploration in the previous several centuries. His route took him through Karak (the Crusader fortress, which he described in detail) and then south through the mountainous region toward the Wadi Araba and Aqaba.

As he traveled, he heard increasingly insistent rumors from local Bedouin about extraordinary carved ruins in a valley to the east of his route — a place called "Wadi Musa" (the Valley of Moses), supposedly containing entire facades of buildings cut directly into the cliff faces. The descriptions matched, in fragmentary form, classical references he knew from Strabo and Pliny to the Nabataean capital at Petra. The site had been theorized but never physically located by European scholars; many had assumed it was lost or destroyed.

Burckhardt resolved to verify the reports. The problem was access: the Bdoul Bedouin who controlled the area around Wadi Musa had a strong cultural prohibition against allowing foreign travelers into the ruins, which they considered both sacred and a potential source of antiquities theft. Direct request was unlikely to work.

The pretext

Burckhardt's solution was the tomb of Aaron. At the southern edge of the Petra valley sits Jabal Haroun (Mount Aaron), a 1,350-meter peak topped by a small Mamluk-era shrine that local Islamic tradition identifies as the burial site of the Prophet Harun — Aaron, the brother of Moses, an important figure in both Muslim and Christian/Jewish religious tradition. Sacrificing a goat at the tomb was a credible Islamic act of devotion, particularly for a foreign Muslim pilgrim making his way through the region.

Burckhardt presented himself to his Bedouin guides in Eldji as wishing to fulfill this religious obligation. The guides, while initially skeptical, eventually agreed — the request was religiously plausible, the pilgrim seemed genuine, and the modest payment Burckhardt offered for guidance was attractive. The route to Jabal Haroun, crucially, passed directly through the abandoned ancient city.

August 22, 1812

On the morning of August 22, Burckhardt and a Bedouin guide entered the Siq from the east. Burckhardt's journal records the encounter:

"We entered a narrow defile, about two hundred paces in length, between rocks of considerable height, the bottom of which becomes wider and wider until it opens into a beautiful valley..."

What follows in his journal is a brief but unusually accurate description of the site — given that he was traveling under a cover identity, could not openly take notes or sketches, and had only a single afternoon to observe and remember what he saw. He describes the Siq, the Treasury (which he calls "the Khasneh" — the same Arabic name used by the Bdoul guides), the Royal Tombs, the Roman theatre, and the lower city. He correctly identifies the site as the classical Petra. He notes the rose-pink color of the stone. He records the bullet marks on the Treasury urn — local Bedouin had been firing at it for years in the belief that it contained hidden treasure (it doesn't; it's solid stone).

Burckhardt's account is fragmentary because his cover was nearly broken. His guide grew suspicious of how much time he was spending looking at the carvings — a real Muslim pilgrim would have been hurrying toward Aaron's tomb, not lingering in pre-Islamic ruins. The guide grew openly hostile by the afternoon, and Burckhardt was forced to cut short his observation and continue up to the shrine at Jabal Haroun, where he performed the symbolic sacrifice as promised before being led out of the valley.

He spent perhaps four hours inside what is now the Petra Archaeological Park.

The published account

Burckhardt continued his journey south, reached Cairo, and from there made several further expeditions — including a visit to Mecca in 1814 in his Muslim-pilgrim guise (he is one of the very few European writers of the period to have observed the Hajj firsthand). But the main southern mission to Timbuktu kept being delayed. In 1817, while waiting in Cairo for a caravan that would take him into the Sahara, Burckhardt contracted dysentery. He died in Cairo on October 15, 1817, at age 32, never having returned to the African interior that had been the original objective of his decade-long mission.

His journals — including the brief Petra account — were edited posthumously by his African Association sponsor and published in London in 1822 as "Travels in Syria and the Holy Land." The Petra section, modest in length though it was, caused an immediate sensation in European archaeological circles. The rediscovery of a major classical city after 1,400 years of European inaccessibility was a genuine intellectual event, and within a decade, other European travelers and artists began arriving at Petra to verify and expand Burckhardt's observations.

The British artist David Roberts visited in 1839 and produced the lithographs that would become Petra's first widely distributed visual record. The American biblical scholar Edward Robinson visited in 1838 and produced the first detailed scholarly survey. By the late 19th century, Petra had become a fixture on the European Grand Tour-extended into the Levant. The site has not been "rediscovered" again since.

The longer arc

Burckhardt's afternoon visit was the spark, not the foundation. The serious archaeological excavation of Petra began in the late 19th century and accelerated through the 20th, with major projects led by Czech (1958), American (1960s onward), Swiss (1980s), and most recently Jordanian and international consortia. The UNESCO World Heritage designation came in 1985, the same year the Bdoul Bedouin were relocated from the caves to the nearby village of Umm Sayhoun.

What Burckhardt actually accomplished — and what his journal makes clear in places — was less a discovery than a documentation. The Bdoul had always known Petra was there. What Burckhardt did was make the connection: the half-mythical "Wadi Musa ruins" of Arab tradition and the half-historical "Petra" of classical scholarship were the same place. That connection, made on a single afternoon in 1812 and published in London ten years later, opened a 200-year arc that has not yet ended.

Frequently asked questions

When was Petra rediscovered?
August 22, 1812. The Swiss explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt, traveling in disguise as a Muslim pilgrim and using the alias "Sheikh Ibrahim ibn Abdallah," reached Petra after persuading a local Bedouin guide to take him to the site under the pretext of wanting to sacrifice a goat at the nearby tomb of Aaron (Jabal Haroun). His journal entry from that day is the first known European description of Petra in roughly 1,400 years.
Was Petra completely lost before 1812?
Lost to the Western world — yes. Lost in any meaningful sense — no. The local Bdoul Bedouin tribe had lived in and around the Petra ruins continuously through the abandonment of the city, and they knew the site intimately. Arab geographers in the medieval period mentioned the ruins occasionally. What was lost was the European awareness of Petra — the connection to the classical references in Strabo and Pliny had been severed for over a millennium by the time Burckhardt reached the site.
Who was Burckhardt?
Johann Ludwig Burckhardt (1784–1817) was a Swiss-born explorer working under the patronage of the African Association, a British learned society dedicated to exploration. Trained as an Orientalist scholar, fluent in Arabic and conversant in Islamic religious practice, Burckhardt traveled the Middle East in the disguise of a Muslim convert from India. He reached Petra at age 28 during a longer journey from Damascus to Cairo. He died of dysentery in Cairo five years later, at age 32, before he could return to verify his Petra observations.
How did he gain access to the site?
Through pretext. The Bedouin guides at the nearby village of Eldji had been hostile to foreign travelers, who they associated with Christian missionaries and antiquities thieves. Burckhardt presented himself as a Muslim pilgrim from India who had vowed to sacrifice a goat at the tomb of the Prophet Harun (Aaron) — which sat on Jabal Haroun, the mountain at the southern edge of the Petra valley. The vow was a credible Islamic religious obligation, and the route to the tomb passed directly through the ancient city. Burckhardt walked through Petra in essentially a single afternoon, taking what notes he could without arousing suspicion.
How did his account become known?
Posthumously. Burckhardt wrote his Petra observations into his travel journal but died in 1817 before publishing. His patron, the African Association, edited and published his journals in 1822 as "Travels in Syria and the Holy Land." The Petra section caused a sensation in European intellectual circles — the rediscovery of a major classical city after 1,400 years was a genuine archaeological event. Within a decade, other European travelers began arriving at Petra to verify and expand on his account.
What does his account actually say?
It's relatively brief — perhaps 12 pages in the published edition — but unusually accurate for a single-visit eyewitness account. He correctly identifies Petra by its classical references in Strabo and Pliny. He describes the Siq, the Treasury (which he calls the "Khasneh" following the local Bedouin name), the Royal Tombs, and the lower city. He notes the rose-pink color of the stone and the carved water channels. He records the urn on top of the Treasury facade being shot at by local Bedouin who believed it contained hidden gold — explaining the bullet marks still visible today. The whole account remains worth reading in the original 19th-century prose.
