WHAT TO SEE

The Royal Tombs

Four monumental tomb facades carved side-by-side into the eastern cliff above the lower city. The most overlooked of Petra's headline monuments — and the place where the rose-red color really earns its name in the late afternoon.

The Royal Tombs are what you see first when you emerge from the Outer Siq into the broader expanse of the lower city — a row of four enormous tomb facades cut directly into the eastern cliff face, side by side, with the Roman theatre and the colonnaded street stretched out in front of them. Most visitors photograph them from a distance and walk on toward the Monastery trail. That's a missed opportunity. The Royal Tombs are second only to the Treasury and the Monastery as Petra's most impressive monumental architecture, and unlike those two, they're meant to be entered as well as viewed.

The four — the Urn Tomb, the Silk Tomb, the Corinthian Tomb, and the Palace Tomb — were almost certainly built for high-ranking Nabataeans, possibly kings, possibly senior royal family members, possibly wealthy merchants from the city's golden age. The exact attributions are less settled than the name "Royal Tombs" suggests. What is settled is that each one is a genuine architectural achievement in its own right, and the cluster as a group is one of the great surviving testaments to Nabataean funerary art.

The four tombs

The Urn Tomb

The southernmost and most prominent of the four. The Urn Tomb (Al-Maqbara al-Kharaza in Arabic) is named for the large stone urn that sits atop the central pediment, and is built on a raised terrace supported by two storeys of arched vaults — a feature you don't see elsewhere in Petra. The facade is austere by Nabataean standards: four tall columns, a simple pediment, and the urn finial. The interior is the largest of any tomb in Petra, a vast vaulted hall roughly 18 meters wide and 14 meters high, with three smaller niches cut into the back wall. The Urn Tomb has been linked stylistically to King Malichus II (40–70 CE), though the identification isn't certain.

In 446 CE, the Urn Tomb was converted into a Christian church — Bishop Jason of Petra recorded the consecration in an inscription that is still visible inside, near the back wall. The conversion is one of the better-documented pieces of evidence for the Byzantine Christian phase of Petra's later history.

The Silk Tomb

The smallest of the four, immediately north of the Urn Tomb. The Silk Tomb is named for the dramatic patterns of color in its sandstone — the rock here is unusually rich in iron oxide and manganese, producing wavy bands of pink, orange, white, and grey that ripple across the facade like watered silk. The facade itself is a relatively modest Nabataean "crow-step" design, with stepped cornices but no elaborate column work. The interior is small and simple. Most visitors photograph the rock pattern rather than the architecture.

The Corinthian Tomb

Immediately north of the Silk Tomb. The Corinthian Tomb is named for the floral capitals on its columns, in the Greek Corinthian style — one of the clearer examples of Hellenistic influence on Nabataean architecture. The facade is large but partly weathered; the upper section in particular has eroded badly. Stylistically, it resembles the Treasury, and some scholars have argued it represents an earlier or later experiment in the same architectural tradition.

The Palace Tomb

The largest by facade size of the four, and the northernmost. The Palace Tomb is named for its three-storey facade design, which echoes the front of a Hellenistic royal palace. It is partly collapsed (the upper storey is largely gone), but the surviving lower two storeys are still imposing — eighteen columns across the front, multiple doorways, and an interior that originally would have contained multiple burial chambers. Of all four, the Palace Tomb is the one where you can most clearly imagine the original effect.

When to visit

Late afternoon, ideally between 15:00 and 17:00. The tombs face west, so morning light leaves them in shadow and afternoon light brings them out at their most saturated rose-red. The classic Petra photograph that includes the Royal Tombs — typically taken from the lower city looking up at the cliff face — only works in afternoon light. If you're doing the standard two-day Petra itinerary, the Royal Tombs make a natural late-afternoon return loop on Day 1, after a morning at the Treasury and a midday climb to the Monastery.

From the Urn Tomb terrace, the view back across the lower city — the colonnaded street, the Great Temple, and the Roman theatre below — is one of the best vantage points in Petra. Take 10 minutes here even if you don't go inside any of the tombs.

Frequently asked questions

How many Royal Tombs are there?
Four monumental tomb facades make up the group typically called "the Royal Tombs": the Urn Tomb, the Silk Tomb, the Corinthian Tomb, and the Palace Tomb. They sit side by side, carved into the eastern cliff face of the lower city, and are visible from the Roman theatre and the main path through Petra. A fifth tomb, the Tomb of Sextius Florentinus (a Roman governor who died around 130 CE), sits slightly further north along the same cliff and is sometimes grouped with the Royal Tombs.
Why are they called Royal Tombs?
Convention rather than confirmed identification. The four tombs are the largest and most elaborately carved funerary monuments in Petra outside the Treasury and the Monastery, which led 19th-century explorers to assume they were the burial sites of Nabataean kings and their families. Inscriptions and archaeological evidence have only partially confirmed this — the Urn Tomb has been linked to King Malichus II (40–70 CE) on stylistic grounds, but the others have no firmly identified occupants.
When is the best time to photograph the Royal Tombs?
Late afternoon. The tombs face west, which means they receive direct sunlight from roughly 14:00 onward and glow at their most saturated rose-red in the last 1–2 hours before sunset. In the morning they are in deep shadow and look surprisingly dull. The classic Royal Tombs photograph — full facade rows lit up, with the colonnaded street and Roman theatre in the foreground — is an afternoon shot.
Can I go inside the Royal Tombs?
Yes. All four are open and free to enter with your standard Petra ticket. The Urn Tomb has the largest interior — a vast vaulted hall about 18 meters wide and 14 meters high, with three small niches cut into the back wall (these may be tombs in their own right). The Silk Tomb and Corinthian Tomb are smaller inside but worth a brief look. The Palace Tomb, the largest of the four by facade size, is partly collapsed and has limited interior access.
How long do I need at the Royal Tombs?
Plan for 45 minutes to an hour. The climb up to the Urn Tomb terrace is a short flight of carved steps from the main path. From the terrace, you can walk along to each of the four tombs, enter each interior briefly, and return to the lower city. Photography from the terrace looking back over the lower city is one of the best vantage points in Petra.
Was the Urn Tomb used as a church?
Yes, briefly. The Urn Tomb was converted to a Christian church in 446 CE under Bishop Jason, who left an inscription on the interior wall that is still visible. The conversion is one of the more striking pieces of evidence for the Byzantine Christian phase of Petra's history, when the city — by then a much-reduced Roman provincial town — supported a small but active Christian community.
