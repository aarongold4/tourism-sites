The Pyramid of Menkaure

The smallest of the three has the best stories: a casing left half-finished mid-construction, a scar from a medieval attempt to demolish it, and a sarcophagus lying at the bottom of the Mediterranean. It's also where the crowds aren't.

Menkaure was Khafre's son and Khufu's grandson, and his pyramid — built around 2510 BC — closes the Giza sequence at a fraction of the scale: roughly 65 meters tall originally, against his grandfather's 146. Guidebooks treat it as the also-ran, and the crowds oblige by ignoring it, which is exactly why the walk to the plateau's southwest end is worth taking. The third pyramid is the most human of the three: smaller, stranger, scarred, and surrounded by the best evidence at Giza of construction as a process rather than a miracle.

The unfinished casing

Menkaure's lowest sixteen or so courses were cased not in white limestone but in red Aswan granite — a far harder, far more prestigious stone — and a band of it survives around the base. Look closely and you'll see the point: many of the granite blocks were never smoothed. They sit in place with their rough bosses still protruding, dressed flat only around the entrance, because Menkaure died before the work finished and his successor completed the complex hastily in cheaper materials. Nowhere else at Giza can you see the construction sequence frozen mid-step like this — fitted first, finished second, abandoned in between.

The scar

The great vertical gash in the north face is the plateau's best monument to its own permanence. In the late 12th century, al-Malik al-Aziz — son of Saladin — ordered the pyramids demolished, and his workforce started on the smallest. They labored for months, at reportedly ruinous expense, managing one or two stones a day, before the project was abandoned as hopeless. The trench they left is the medieval world's progress report against Old Kingdom engineering: a king's resources, defeated.

The lost sarcophagus

In 1837 the English officer Howard Vyse blasted his way into the pyramid and found a magnificent basalt sarcophagus carved in palace-facade panels — and shipped it for the British Museum aboard the merchant ship Beatrice, which sank in deep water en route from Malta in 1838. Menkaure's sarcophagus has been at the bottom of the Mediterranean ever since, location unknown: the only pharaoh's sarcophagus lost at sea, and one of archaeology's great recoverable prizes if it's ever found.

The complex and the triads

Beside the pyramid stand the three queens' pyramids — one cased, like the king's, partly in granite — and to the east the mortuary temple whose excavation in 1908-10 produced the greywacke triads of Menkaure: the king striding between goddesses, polished to a shine, by common consent the supreme royal sculpture of the Old Kingdom. The originals are split between the Egyptian Museum in Cairo and Boston; seeing them after walking the temple they came from is one of the quiet pleasures of pairing Giza with a museum day (museum guide).

The interior, when open

Menkaure's interior rotates with Khafre's (current status and the full tier system in our fees guide; recently around 200 EGP when open — the cheapest interior at Giza). The descending passage leads through an antechamber carved with palace-facade paneling — a decorative touch neither larger pyramid has — to the granite burial chamber Vyse emptied. Short, atmospheric, uncrowded.

Seeing it well

The southwest end is the plateau at its calmest: from beyond Menkaure's queens' pyramids, looking back northeast, all three pyramids stack into the desert panorama with the city hidden — the counterpart angle to the famous viewpoint, with no camel line. The free site shuttle (running since the 2025 vehicle ban) stops near the third pyramid; ride out, walk back, and you'll have done Giza in the right direction: from quiet to crescendo. Timing logic as ever in opening hours.

The statues that made the name

Menkaure's true legacy travels in greywacke, and knowing the pieces turns his quiet corner of the plateau into a treasure map's origin. Reisner's excavation of the valley temple in 1908-10 produced the era's supreme sculpture cache: the triads— the king flanked by the goddess Hathor and a province's personification, several now in the Tahrir museum's Old Kingdom rooms — and the dyad of Menkaure and his queen (probably Khamerernebty II), the pair striding forward with her arm around him, polished to a tenderness that has made it the textbook image of Old Kingdom art; the partage system of the era carried it to Boston's Museum of Fine Arts, where it remains the collection's anchor. Stand at the ruined valley temple's footprint at the plateau's quiet end and the inversion lands: the smallest pyramid funded the finest faces, and the king economists call the modest one is the one whose features — broad, calm, faintly smiling — the world actually knows. The museum pairing (the strategy) completes him; the pyramid alone is half the man.

Visiting notes

The practical residue: Menkaure anchors the plateau's quiet southwestern end, last stop before the viewpoint ridge on the standard circuit, with the gash on its north face — the 12th-century demolition attempt that defeated a sultan — as the storytelling moment for whoever you're guiding. Interior access follows the rotation system (check at the ticket point; recently in the 200-280 EGP band when open), the granite casing courses by the entrance reward a close look whether or not you descend, and the queens' three small pyramids beside it photograph best from the south with the giants stacked behind — the composition that makes the plateau's hierarchy legible in one frame.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the Pyramid of Menkaure so much smaller?

Several forces converged: prime plateau space was taken, the royal economy after two colossal building generations had limits, and royal ideology was shifting investment from pyramid mass toward temple complexity. Menkaure's pyramid is about a tenth of the Great Pyramid's volume — but its temples were more elaborate, and its statuary, now in museums, is the finest royal sculpture of the age.

What is the gash on the north face?

A 12th-century demolition attempt. Saladin's son al-Malik al-Aziz ordered the pyramids dismantled; his crews worked on Menkaure's — the smallest — for months, prying out a few stones a day at enormous cost, before abandoning the project. The vertical scar is their progress report: a medieval kingdom defeated by Old Kingdom masonry.

Can you go inside Menkaure's pyramid?

On rotation with Khafre — when open, the interior ticket has recently run around 200 EGP, the cheapest pyramid interior at Giza. The descent leads to a chamber with a carved-paneled antechamber unique among the three. Check what's open when you buy your plateau ticket.

Is Menkaure worth walking to?

Yes, precisely because most people don't. It anchors the quiet end of the plateau: the three queens' pyramids beside it, the unfinished granite casing at its base showing construction mid-process, and crowd density a fraction of the Great Pyramid's. The free site shuttle stops nearby.
