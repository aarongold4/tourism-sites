JORDAN

The Dead Sea

The lowest point on Earth's land surface, where the water is so dense you physically cannot sink. 55 km west of Amman, an hour from the capital, and the natural recovery stop on a Jordan loop after the high-energy Petra days. Here's how to do it.

Last reviewed June 4, 2026 · 9-minute read · See our editorial standards

The Dead Sea is one of those destinations where the experience reliably exceeds expectations. Travelers approach it as a novelty stop — float in the salty water, take the obligatory photograph reading a newspaper while bobbing on the surface, smear some Dead Sea mud on the skin, move on. What most travelers don't expect is that the surrounding landscape (the deepest land surface on Earth, dramatic mountain views, sunset across the water toward Israel) is as memorable as the floating itself. For travelers building a Jordan itinerary that includes both Petra and Wadi Rum, the Dead Sea works perfectly as a recovery day either before or after the southern destinations.

This page covers the practical version: where to actually go (the Jordanian side has several different beach options), what the experience involves, how to fit it into a wider Jordan trip, and the things travelers tend to underestimate (the salt is real, the mud is real, the sunset is real). The framing throughout: the Dead Sea earns its place on a Jordan itinerary, even for travelers who aren't typically beach-and-spa people.

The basic facts

The Dead Sea sits at -430 meters below sea level — the lowest exposed land surface on Earth. The water is hypersaline at roughly 34% salinity (about 10 times the typical ocean), which produces both the impossible-to-sink floating experience and the medicinal properties travelers associate with the sea. The lake straddles the border between Jordan (eastern shore) and Israel/the West Bank (western shore); from the Jordanian beaches you can see across to the Israeli coast in clear weather.

The Jordanian resort strip is concentrated on the northeastern shore at Sweimeh, about 55 km from Amman. The major resort hotels (Mövenpick, Hilton, Kempinski, Crowne Plaza, Marriott) cluster within a 5-kilometer stretch, each with private beach access. Public beach access is available at "Amman Beach" (a few kilometers further south) for travelers not staying at the resorts.

The four ways to visit

Public beach (Amman Beach)

The cheapest option. 25-30 JOD entry per person, includes basic facilities — changing rooms, showers, basic restaurant, beach access. The experience is the same floating experience as the resort beaches, in less polished surroundings. Best for budget travelers and day-trippers who don't care about resort amenities. Open daily 09:00 to sunset (extended hours in summer).

Hotel day pass at a resort

The recommended option for most travelers. The Mövenpick Dead Sea Resort and Spa, Hilton Dead Sea, Kempinski Ishtar, and Crowne Plaza Dead Sea all sell day passes to non-guests, typically $50-90 USD per person. Includes beach access, pool, lunch buffet, and sometimes a spa credit. The experience: comfortable lounge chairs on a sand-and-pebble beach, freshwater showers immediately adjacent, hotel restaurant for meals, infinity pool with views over the water if you want a freshwater alternative.

For most travelers, the hotel day pass is the right call. The $40-60 premium over the public beach buys you significantly more comfortable infrastructure and the option of spending a full half-day there without the experience feeling stripped-down.

Overnight resort stay

For travelers who specifically want spa time, sunset photography, or who are using the Dead Sea as a recovery break in the middle of a longer Jordan trip. Resort room rates run $200-450 USD per night depending on resort and season. The Mövenpick and Kempinski are the most consistently well-reviewed; the Hilton is solid value at the lower end of the range; the Crowne Plaza is the budget option among the international brands.

An overnight stay enables: sunset and sunrise photography (the light over the Dead Sea is genuinely beautiful), proper spa time at one of the major spas (Mövenpick's spa is the most famous, with mud treatments, salt scrubs, and traditional Dead Sea therapies), and a slower pace than a day trip allows.

Free public swimming areas (Wadi Mujib region)

A 15-minute drive south from the resort strip leads to several unofficial public swimming spots along the coast — undeveloped pebble-and-mud beaches with no entry fee and no facilities. The water is the same as anywhere else along the Dead Sea, but the swim is essentially free if you don't mind the lack of infrastructure. The Wadi Mujib Biosphere Reserve nearby offers canyon hiking (separate entry fee, very different experience) for travelers wanting more than just the float.

The floating experience

The actual sequence at a typical resort beach:

1. Walk in slowly. The water is shallow at the shore and the bottom can be uneven (salt crystals form sharp formations in places). Wear swim shoes if you have them — most resorts rent or sell them for 3-5 JOD.

2. Float on your back. Once the water is mid-thigh to waist deep, lean back. You'll find yourself naturally bobbing at the surface, with the water supporting your body. Don't try to swim conventionally — the buoyancy throws off normal swim mechanics. The optimal position is on your back with arms slightly out for balance.

3. Don't get water in your eyes. This is the only real "warning" of the experience. Salt water this concentrated stings genuinely painfully for 10-20 minutes if it gets in your eyes. Bring a freshwater bottle if you're concerned. Most resort beaches have freshwater stations every 50 meters along the shoreline.

4. The mud. Black Dead Sea mud (rich in minerals supposed to have medicinal properties) is available at most beaches — sometimes for free in bucket form, sometimes as part of an upcharged spa treatment. The standard approach: smear it on your skin, let it dry for 10-15 minutes, rinse off in the sea, then rinse with freshwater. Travelers either find this delightful or pointless; most try it once and don't feel strongly either way.

5. Rinse and dry. Spend no more than 20-30 minutes in the water at a stretch — the salt becomes uncomfortable on skin over longer exposures. Rinse thoroughly with freshwater (showers are immediately adjacent at all resort beaches). The salt residue can irritate skin if not fully washed off.

The famous photographs

Two iconic photo compositions every Dead Sea visitor sees:

Reading a newspaper while floating. Requires bringing a newspaper (most resorts will hand you one for free), positioning yourself on your back with the paper held above the water, and having someone shoot from chest level on shore. The visual joke is that you're clearly weightless on the water's surface. Worth doing once for the keepsake photograph.

The salt crystal formations. Around the shoreline, the ongoing salt deposition creates white crystalline formations — sometimes large enough to look like artistic sculptures. Walk along the shoreline at any of the resort beaches to find good examples. Particularly photogenic in late afternoon light.

Sunset is genuinely beautiful here — the Israeli coastline silhouetted across the water, the light hitting the Moab mountains behind the resort strip. Many overnight resort guests describe the sunset view as the best part of the visit, separately from the floating itself.

Fitting the Dead Sea into a Jordan trip

Three common positions in a Jordan itinerary:

As a half-day from Amman (before Petra). The most common arrangement. Amman to Dead Sea is about 1 hour by car; spend a half day at the beach, return to Amman for the night, drive south to Petra the next day. Works well for travelers using Amman as a base.

As the final stop returning from Wadi Rum. The recovery option. Drive from Wadi Rum up the Desert Highway to the Dead Sea (about 4 hours), spend the night at a resort, fly out from Amman the next morning. Particularly valuable after the physically demanding Petra and Wadi Rum days — the spa, the floating, the resort comfort all read as deserved recovery.

As a multi-night stop in itself. For travelers who want spa time, treat the Dead Sea as a destination rather than a stop. 2-3 nights at the Mövenpick or Kempinski with proper spa treatments produces a wellness-retreat experience distinct from the rest of the Jordan trip. Best for travelers extending to 9-10 day total trips.

When to visit

The Dead Sea has a longer comfortable season than the rest of Jordan because the low elevation moderates temperatures year-round. October through May are all genuinely pleasant. Summer (June-August) is hot (35-40°C+) but tolerable at the resorts with their pools, and the salty water is actually cooler than the air — counterintuitively, summer afternoons can be the best floating time.

Winter (December-February) is mild — daytime 18-24°C — and one of the best times for the spa and floating combination. The water itself is comfortable year-round (the deep-saline lake maintains relatively stable temperature). Winter also means dramatic light, often the clearest sunsets, and lowest tourist crowds.

What to bring

  • Bathing suit: The salt is very rough on fabric — bring an older suit you don't mind retiring.
  • Swim shoes or sandals: The shoreline can have sharp salt crystals and uneven stones. Worth wearing.
  • Sunscreen: Reflection off the water is intense and the sun at -430 meters elevation is filtered through more atmosphere, but UV exposure is still significant.
  • Wide-brim hat and sunglasses: Glare from the salt and water is bright.
  • Don't bring expensive jewelry: Silver tarnishes immediately on contact with Dead Sea water; even gold can pick up residue. Leave anything sentimental in your hotel room.
  • Camera in waterproof case if you want water-level photos: Phone in a waterproof pouch works fine.

Frequently asked questions

Where exactly is the Dead Sea?
The Dead Sea lies between Jordan (eastern shore) and Israel/the West Bank (western shore), about 55 km west of Amman and 200 km north of Petra. The water surface sits at -430 meters below sea level — the lowest point on Earth's land surface. The Jordanian resort strip (Sweimeh) is on the northeastern shore, with the main resort beaches and public beach access concentrated in a 15-kilometer stretch.
How much does it cost to visit?
Three pricing tiers. (1) Public beach (Amman Beach): 25-30 JOD entry per person, includes basic facilities — changing rooms, showers, basic restaurant. (2) Hotel day pass at one of the resort beaches (Mövenpick, Hilton, Kempinski, Crowne Plaza): 50-90 USD per person, includes beach access, pool, lunch buffet, sometimes a spa credit. (3) Free public swimming areas (Wadi Mujib, parts of the coast not developed): no entry fee but minimal facilities and a short walk to reach the water. For most travelers, the hotel day pass produces the best experience.
What's the floating experience actually like?
Genuinely strange. The water density is roughly 1.24 g/mL (compared to 1.00 for fresh water and 1.025 for typical ocean), which is high enough that you physically cannot sink — even if you try. The standard experience is to walk into the water until it's about waist-deep, then lean back and float on your back. You'll find yourself naturally bobbing at the surface with your shoulders, head, and feet all above the waterline. Most travelers find the experience strange for about 5 minutes, then enjoyable for about 30 minutes, then they're ready to get out — the salt is uncomfortable on skin over longer exposures.
How long should I spend at the Dead Sea?
Half a day is the right amount for most travelers — about 2-3 hours at the beach including the floating experience, a mud treatment, and a meal. A full day works well if you're using a resort with a real pool, spa facilities, and the option to alternate between Dead Sea time and freshwater pool time. Overnight stays are worthwhile if you specifically want spa time and don't mind paying resort prices ($200-400 USD/night for the main resorts).
Is the Dead Sea really shrinking?
Yes, and significantly — the water level has dropped roughly 35 meters since the 1960s and continues to drop about 1 meter per year. The shrinkage is caused by water diversion from the Jordan River (its main inflow) by Jordan, Israel, and Syria for irrigation and drinking water. The visible effects include sinkholes forming along the receding shoreline (some have caused road closures), beaches moving further from original resort buildings, and ongoing concern about long-term ecological collapse. Visiting today is essentially safe; the changes are slow enough that beaches still function normally, but the Dead Sea you visit in 2026 is meaningfully smaller than the one tourists visited in 1990.
Should I worry about the salt?
Worth respecting, not worth being scared of. The salt content (~34% — about 10 times typical ocean) means: don't get water in your eyes (genuinely painful, immediate stinging that lasts 15-20 minutes), don't shave the day before (any small cuts will sting noticeably), don't put your face underwater (the same eye issue but worse), and don't stay in the water more than 20-30 minutes at a stretch (the salt becomes uncomfortable on skin). All resort beaches have freshwater showers immediately adjacent for rinsing off after. Most travelers are mildly cautious for the first 5 minutes, then fine for the rest of the experience.
