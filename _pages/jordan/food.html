JORDAN

Jordanian food

Mansaf, mezze, knafeh, and the surprisingly strong Jordan food scene. What to actually order, where to find the best version, and the dietary considerations travelers most often ask about. Here's the practical guide.

Last reviewed June 5, 2026 · 9-minute read · See our editorial standards

Jordanian food is one of the more pleasant surprises of a Jordan trip. Travelers usually approach Middle Eastern food with the expectation of "Lebanese-adjacent mezze plus shawarma" and leave with a more nuanced sense — Jordan\'s Bedouin heritage shapes the cuisine (slow-cooked lamb dishes, dried-yogurt sauces, communal eating traditions) in ways distinct from Lebanon, Syria, or Palestine. The country also has a strong modern restaurant scene in Amman that translates traditional preparations into contemporary contexts. Travelers who lean into the food side of the trip — choosing restaurants thoughtfully, ordering beyond the obvious, taking a cooking class — often find Jordan\'s food among the more memorable aspects of their visit.

This page covers the major dishes worth ordering, where to find the best versions in Amman and the major tourist destinations, dietary considerations, and the practical questions travelers ask about Jordanian dining. The framing throughout: Jordan\'s food rewards attention beyond the default tourist menu.

The dishes worth ordering

Mansaf — the national dish

Lamb cooked in a fermented dried-yogurt sauce called jameed, served over rice and shrak (large flatbread), with toasted almonds and pine nuts on top. The traditional preparation takes 5-6 hours — the lamb is simmered in the jameed sauce until tender, the jameed itself is a fermented yogurt product that gives the dish a distinctive tangy depth that doesn\'t exist in any other regional cuisine.

Mansaf is the centerpiece of major Jordanian celebrations — weddings, Eid, important guest meals. The traditional eating method is communal: a large platter is placed in the center of a low table, diners stand around it and eat from a shared section, using only the right hand to scoop rice and lamb. Tourist versions are typically plated individually with cutlery; both are acceptable.

Best mansaf in Amman: Sufra (Rainbow Street), Fakhr el-Din (Jabal Amman), Beit Sitti (cooking class). Best mansaf outside Amman: any traditional Bedouin camp dinner in Wadi Rum often includes mansaf or a Zarb (underground-oven cooked meat). Prices range 12-25 JOD per person depending on restaurant tier.

Mezze — the start of every meal

The shared-appetizer tradition. A typical Jordanian mezze spread includes 5-10 small dishes, shared among diners at the start of the meal. Standard items:

  • Hummus: Chickpea and tahini dip, almost always present.
  • Baba ganoush (mutabbal): Smoky grilled eggplant with tahini.
  • Labneh: Strained yogurt cheese, often with olive oil and za\'atar.
  • Foul medames: Fava bean stew, eaten with bread for dipping.
  • Muhammara: Red pepper and walnut paste with pomegranate molasses.
  • Tabbouleh: Parsley and bulgur salad with tomato and lemon.
  • Fattoush: Mixed greens with toasted bread and sumac dressing.
  • Warak enab: Stuffed grape leaves with rice and meat (or meatless).
  • Kibbeh: Bulgur-and-meat torpedoes, fried or raw.
  • Olives and pickles: The Jordanian pickle tradition is strong — pickled turnips dyed pink with beetroot are a Levantine standard.

A standard restaurant mezze platter for two costs 12-25 JOD. Many travelers eat exclusively mezze plus a single shared main course for an entire dinner; this is the typical Levantine pattern.

Knafeh — the dessert

Sweet cheese pastry made with shredded wheat (kataifi), unripe akkawi cheese, and sugar syrup. Two main variations: nabulsi (the cheese version, named for Nablus where it originated) and khishneh (the rougher-textured version). Knafeh is genuinely one of the great regional desserts — the warm cheese inside a crisp pastry shell, soaked in syrup, sometimes with chopped pistachios on top.

Best knafeh in Amman: Habibah in downtown is the institution, open since 1951, makes the standard against which other knafeh is measured. A portion costs 1-3 JOD depending on size. Eat it fresh and hot — knafeh doesn\'t keep well and the texture degrades within an hour. Most knafeh shops let you watch the cheese being cooked through the front window.

Other essential dishes

Maqluba: Layered rice, meat, and vegetables (eggplant, cauliflower) cooked in a single pot and flipped upside-down for service — "maqluba" literally means "upside-down." A classic family dish, less common in tourist restaurants but available at traditional places.

Maglouba: A variant spelling of maqluba above. The dish is the same.

Falafel and shawarma: The street-food staples. Falafel is fried chickpea balls in pita with vegetables and tahini; shawarma is rotisserie meat (lamb or chicken) sliced into pita with garlic sauce and pickles. Both available everywhere from 1-3 JOD per sandwich.

Zarb: The Bedouin specialty — meat (lamb or chicken) and vegetables cooked in an underground sand oven, traditionally served at Wadi Rum Bedouin camps. The dish requires advance preparation and isn\'t typically available at restaurants.

Galayet bandora: A simple tomato-based dish — tomatoes, onions, garlic, sometimes meat — served with bread. Common at simpler restaurants, often a good cheap meal.

Musakhan: Sumac chicken on flatbread with caramelized onions. The Palestinian-Jordanian classic; particularly good in restaurants near the West Bank border.

Where to eat — by destination

Amman

Street food: Hashem (downtown, open since 1956, falafel and ful at picnic-table seating, the King eats here). Cost: 2-5 JOD per person for a full meal.

Traditional sit-down: Sufra (Rainbow Street), Fakhr el-Din (Jabal Amman), Reem (downtown). Cost: 15-25 JOD per person.

Modern Jordanian: Beit Sitti (cooking class restaurant), Levant (upscale modern interpretations), Sufra. Cost: 20-40 JOD per person.

Knafeh: Habibah (downtown, the institution). Cost: 1-3 JOD.

Petra (Wadi Musa)

Local restaurant scene is smaller but solid. The Three Steps and Al-Wadi are reliable mid-range traditional restaurants on Tourism Street; My Mom\'s Recipe is the most-recommended traditional dinner spot in town. The Basin Restaurant inside Petra is a buffet at the foot of the Monastery trail (18-20 JOD per person, lunch only). Hotel restaurants are generally fine but not exceptional.

Wadi Rum

Almost exclusively Bedouin camp meals. The standard camp dinner includes Zarb (underground-oven meat), salads, rice, hummus, and Bedouin bread baked over coals. Quality varies by camp — mid-range and luxury camps generally have better food than basic camps. Tea (the standard Bedouin chai or sage tea) is unlimited and free throughout your stay.

Aqaba

Strong seafood scene due to Red Sea access. Ali Baba Restaurant (city center, traditional Arabic seafood), Floka Restaurant (grilled fish, the freshest), Romero (Italian-Mediterranean upscale). Resort hotels have decent in-house restaurants. Street-food shawarma and falafel widely available.

Drinks

Tea: The default hospitality drink. Black tea with sugar, sometimes with mint, served everywhere from Bedouin camps to upscale restaurants. Refusing tea is generally considered slightly impolite in Bedouin contexts; accept the small glass even if you don\'t finish it.

Coffee: Two traditions. Turkish-style coffee (small cup, finely ground, often with cardamom) is the older form. Modern espresso-based coffee is widely available at urban cafes. The Bedouin "qahwa sada" (unsweetened cardamom coffee, served in tiny cups) is a hospitality ritual at camps — you accept three small cups, then gently shake your cup to signal "no more" on the fourth.

Fresh juice: Pomegranate, orange, lemon, and mixed fruit juices are widely available at street stalls and restaurants for 1-3 JOD. Genuinely fresh, often pressed in front of you.

Tap water: Technically safe in Amman and major cities, but most travelers drink bottled water for taste preference. Cost: 0.5-1 JOD per 1.5-liter bottle.

Alcohol: Available in tourist hotels, dedicated liquor stores (Carrefour and other supermarkets), and some Amman restaurants. The local beer is Amstel (brewed in Jordan); Carakale is the craft alternative. Local wine production exists (Jordan River Wines, Saint George\'s, Zumot) but is mostly available only in upscale restaurants. Strong drink (arak, whiskey, vodka) widely available. Ramadan affects alcohol availability — many restaurants don\'t serve alcohol during the holy month, even those that normally do.

Dietary considerations

Vegetarian

Easy. Most mezze dishes are vegetarian (hummus, baba ganoush, falafel, mejadra rice-and-lentils, fattoush, tabbouleh, warak enab vegetarian version). Many restaurants have vegetarian mains. Eating multiple mezze dishes plus bread is a completely satisfying meal pattern that many travelers settle into for the trip.

Vegan

Harder due to widespread dairy. Most mezze items contain yogurt, labneh, or cheese. Useful Arabic phrases:

  • Bedoun jubn — "without cheese"
  • Bedoun zabadi — "without yogurt"
  • Bedoun labneh — "without strained yogurt"
  • Bedoun samn — "without ghee/clarified butter"

Reliably vegan: hummus (when made without yogurt, which is most versions), falafel, mejadra, foul medames, most salads, stuffed grape leaves (vegetarian version), bread. Verify with the server before ordering.

Gluten-free

Possible but limited. Bread is central to most meals, and many mezze dishes are served with bread for dipping. Reliably gluten-free options: grilled meats, rice dishes (mansaf, maqluba), most salads (skip the tabbouleh which contains bulgur), hummus, baba ganoush, plain yogurt. Cross-contamination at busy restaurants is a real concern; travelers with celiac disease should be cautious.

Halal and kosher

Halal — virtually universal in Jordan. Almost no Jordanian restaurants serve pork or non-halal meat. Kosher — essentially nonexistent in Jordan. Observant Jewish travelers typically bring their own food or eat vegetarian/mezze options.

A cooking class is worth it

For travelers who specifically want to engage with Jordanian food beyond ordering at restaurants, take a cooking class. Beit Sitti in Amman is the institution — a traditional house in Jabal Amman where you cook a multi-course Jordanian meal under instruction, then eat what you\'ve made. About 4 hours, 40-55 JOD per person, runs lunch and dinner sessions. The class teaches mezze preparation, a major main (often maqluba or mansaf), and a dessert. Strong recommendation for travelers willing to spend half a day.

Petra Kitchen in Wadi Musa runs similar classes focused on the Bedouin-influenced southern Jordanian cooking tradition. Smaller-scale and more rustic than Beit Sitti but a genuine experience.

Frequently asked questions

What is Jordan's national dish?
Mansaf — lamb cooked in a fermented dried-yogurt sauce (jameed), served over rice and flatbread, traditionally eaten communally by hand from a shared platter. The dish has Bedouin origins and remains the centerpiece of major celebrations (weddings, holidays, important guest meals). Almost every traditional Jordanian restaurant serves it; the better versions take 5-6 hours to prepare and are typically only available with advance notice or on specific days of the week.
Where should I eat in Amman?
Three reliable categories. (1) Street food: Hashem in downtown for the best falafel and ful (fava bean stew) — open since 1956, the King of Jordan eats here. (2) Traditional sit-down: Sufra (Rainbow Street) or Fakhr el-Din (Jabal Amman) for proper mezze spreads and grilled meats. (3) Modern Jordanian: Beit Sitti (cooking-class restaurant where you prepare and eat traditional dishes), Levant (upscale modern interpretations). For knafeh dessert: Habibah in downtown is the institution. See <a href="/jordan/amman">the Amman page</a> for the full restaurant guide.
What's mezze?
The Levantine appetizer tradition — multiple small dishes shared at the start of a meal. Typical Jordanian mezze includes hummus, baba ganoush (smoky eggplant), labneh (strained yogurt), foul (fava beans), muhammara (red pepper and walnut paste), tabbouleh (parsley and bulgur salad), warak enab (stuffed grape leaves), kibbeh (bulgur and meat torpedoes), and various pickles and olives. Most traditional restaurants offer mezze platters; ordering one followed by a single shared main is the typical Jordanian dinner pattern. Plan for 8-15 JOD per person for a full mezze spread.
Is the food safe to eat?
Generally yes, with normal precautions. Tap water in Amman and major tourist areas is technically potable but most travelers drink bottled water to avoid the taste and any acclimation issues. Cooked food at restaurants is reliably safe. Street food is also generally safe at busy stalls with high turnover (Hashem is the classic example) — avoid food that's been sitting out. Be more careful with raw vegetables in remote rural areas. Standard traveler hygiene applies but Jordan is not a destination where food-borne illness is a constant concern.
Can I find vegetarian/vegan food?
Vegetarian — easily. Most mezze dishes are vegetarian (hummus, baba ganoush, falafel, mejadra rice-and-lentils, fattoush salad). Many restaurants have vegetarian mains beyond the mezze. Vegan is harder due to widespread dairy (labneh, yogurt sauces, cheese in many dishes). Vegan travelers should learn to ask for "bedoun jubn" (without cheese) and "bedoun zabadi" (without yogurt) — most restaurants will accommodate. The vegan options are limited but real: falafel, hummus (made without yogurt), mejadra, stuffed grape leaves (sometimes), most of the salads.
What about alcohol?
Available but limited compared to Western countries. Jordan is officially Muslim, but alcohol is legal and sold in tourist hotels, dedicated liquor stores, and some restaurants in Amman and the resort areas. Most traditional Jordanian restaurants don't serve alcohol. The local beer is Amstel (brewed under license in Jordan) and Carakale (a craft brewery in the Yarmuk region); wine is mostly imported with some local production. Strong drink is widely available in tourist contexts; in non-tourist neighborhoods and during Ramadan, expect that meals will not include alcohol.
