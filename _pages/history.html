HISTORY

The history of Petra

A city carved into a canyon by an Arab trading empire that ran the incense road from southern Arabia to the Mediterranean — and then forgotten by the outside world for a thousand years. The story of Petra is older and stranger than the photographs suggest.

Last reviewed April 28, 2026 · 14-minute read · See our editorial standards

The Petra most travelers come to see — the Treasury, the Monastery, the rock-cut tombs of the Royal Wadi — represents perhaps two centuries of the city's life. Before those facades were carved, Nabataean merchants had been growing wealthy on the incense trade for three hundred years. After Rome annexed the kingdom in 106 CE, the city remained one of the most important in the eastern empire for another two centuries. After the earthquakes that broke its water system, it slowly emptied — but Bedouin communities continued to use its caves for shelter into the 20th century. The "lost city" that Burckhardt rediscovered in 1812 had never really been lost to the people who lived next to it. It had been forgotten by the world that wrote the history books.

This page is the index to that longer story, divided into the periods that genuinely change how you understand what you're looking at on site. The Nabataean kingdom — its origins, its trade routes, the king who built the Treasury — gets its own section. The Roman and Byzantine periods, which built much of what visitors walk past without noticing, get another. The medieval and modern history, including the contemporary Bedouin communities, gets a third. And the rediscovery — Burckhardt, the explorers and artists who followed him, the systematic archaeology that began in the late 19th century and continues today — gets its own.

Roman, Byzantine, and Islamic periods

The Roman annexation of 106 CE didn't end Petra — it transformed it. The Nabataean kings were gone, replaced by a Roman governor, but the city itself prospered for another century and a half as the capital of the new province of Arabia Petraea. The colonnaded street that runs through the lower city, the Roman theatre carved into the cliff, and the temple complex at Qasr al-Bint all received major Roman-era investment. The emperor Hadrian visited in 131 CE.

The decline began gradually in the 3rd century, as maritime trade routes to India and East Africa drained traffic from the overland caravans that had made Petra wealthy. The decisive blows were tectonic: a massive earthquake in 363 CE destroyed much of the water-management infrastructure that had made desert urbanism possible, and a second in 551 CE accelerated what was already an unstoppable process. By the Islamic conquest in the 7th century, Petra was a minor town, and by the medieval period it was effectively abandoned — except by the Crusader knights who briefly used the High Place of Sacrifice as a watchtower in the 12th century, and by local Bedouin communities who continued to use the caves as shelter.

The rediscovery and modern Petra

On August 22, 1812, the Swiss explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt persuaded a Bedouin guide to lead him into the Siq under the pretense of wanting to sacrifice a goat at the tomb of Aaron. Burckhardt — traveling under the assumed name Sheikh Ibrahim, disguised as a Muslim pilgrim from India — spent a single afternoon inside the ancient city before his guide became suspicious and demanded he leave. His brief description, published in his posthumous travel journals in 1822, was the first western account of Petra since the Crusades. It triggered nearly two centuries of European exploration, drawing, surveying, and eventually serious archaeology.

The early visitors were mostly artists and adventurers. Léon de Laborde and Louis-Maurice-Adolphe Linant de Bellefonds produced the first accurate drawings in 1828. The British painter David Roberts, whose 1839 lithographs probably did more to fix Petra in the western imagination than any other single source, arrived shortly after. Systematic archaeology began only in the late 19th century and accelerated dramatically in the 20th, with major American, British, French, German, and Jordanian excavation programs running continuously since the 1950s. The most spectacular recent discovery — the cache of Byzantine papyri found inside a burned church in 1993 — is changing our understanding of late-Roman Petra in real time.

A timeline, briefly

For readers who want the whole story in one paragraph: people have lived in the Petra region for at least 9,000 years (the Neolithic village at Beidha is one of the oldest agricultural settlements ever excavated). The Nabataeans, an Arab people from northern Arabia, settled in southern Jordan by the 4th century BCE and made Petra their capital. They grew wealthy from the incense and spice trade and built most of the monumental rock-cut architecture between roughly the 1st century BCE and the 2nd century CE, with the peak under King Aretas IV from 9 BCE to 40 CE. Rome annexed the kingdom peacefully in 106 CE and ruled the city as the capital of Arabia Petraea. A catastrophic earthquake in 363 CE broke the water system; another in 551 CE accelerated decline. By the Islamic conquest in the 7th century, Petra was a minor town. It was effectively abandoned in the medieval period — known only to local Bedouin communities — until the Swiss explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt visited in 1812 and reintroduced it to the wider world. UNESCO designated it a World Heritage Site in 1985, and the New Seven Wonders vote in 2007 made it globally famous in its current form.

Frequently asked questions

Who built Petra?
The Nabataeans, an Arab people who emerged as merchants of the incense and spice trade across the deserts of Arabia. They settled in the area around Petra (which they called Raqmu) from the 4th century BCE onward, and by the 2nd century BCE had made it their political and commercial capital. Most of Petra's monumental architecture — the Treasury, the Monastery, the Royal Tombs — was carved during the reign of Aretas IV (9 BCE to 40 CE), the high-water mark of Nabataean wealth.
How old is Petra?
The site has been continuously inhabited for at least 9,000 years — a Neolithic settlement at Beidha, just north of the main Petra complex, dates to around 7,000 BCE and is one of the oldest agricultural villages ever excavated. The Nabataean city we recognize today began to take shape from the 4th century BCE, with the famous rock-cut facades dating mostly from the 1st century BCE through the 2nd century CE. By the standard archaeological reckoning, "Petra" as a city is roughly 2,300 years old.
When did Petra become part of the Roman Empire?
106 CE, when the emperor Trajan annexed the Nabataean kingdom under his governor Cornelius Palma. The transition was peaceful — there's no evidence of a Roman conquest or siege — and Petra was granted metropolitan status as the capital of the new Roman province of Arabia Petraea. The emperor Hadrian visited in 131 CE, after which the city was sometimes called Petra Hadriana on inscriptions.
Why was Petra abandoned?
A combination of factors over several centuries, rather than a single catastrophe. The most important was economic: by the 3rd and 4th centuries CE, sea-based trade routes to India and East Africa had largely displaced the overland caravans that made Petra wealthy. The decisive blow was the earthquake of 363 CE, which destroyed much of the city's sophisticated water-management infrastructure and damaged about half its buildings. A second major earthquake in 551 CE accelerated the decline. By the time of the Islamic conquest in the 7th century, Petra was a minor settlement; by the medieval period, it was largely abandoned and known only to local Bedouin.
Who rediscovered Petra?
Johann Ludwig Burckhardt, a Swiss explorer working for the British African Association, on August 22, 1812. Burckhardt was traveling through southern Jordan disguised as a Muslim pilgrim and heard local Bedouin describe the "ruins of an ancient city" in a hidden valley. He persuaded his guide to take him through the Siq under the pretense of wanting to sacrifice a goat at the tomb of Aaron (Jebel Haroun, just outside the main site). He spent only a single day in Petra, but the brief description he published reached European audiences and triggered the wave of 19th-century exploration that followed.
What's the difference between the Nabataeans and the Bedouin?
The Nabataeans were the ancient civilization that built Petra — a settled, urbanized Arab people who controlled long-distance trade and ruled a kingdom from roughly the 4th century BCE to 106 CE. The Bedouin are the contemporary Arab tribal communities who have lived in the Petra region for centuries, traditionally as nomadic and semi-nomadic pastoralists. The most directly Petra-connected Bedouin tribe is the Bdoul, whose members lived inside the caves of the ancient city until 1985, when they were relocated to the village of Umm Sayhoun on its northern edge as part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site designation. Many of the guides, donkey owners, and tea-stall operators inside Petra today are Bdoul.
